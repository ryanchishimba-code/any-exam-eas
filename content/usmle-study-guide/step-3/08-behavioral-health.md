# Behavioral health and substance use

> **Study aid only.** Safety first, then a treatment that has evidence. APA, VA/DoD, and SAMHSA outrank a habit. Behavioral health is about 4–6% of Step 3 MCQs and a regular CCS theme. Psychiatry was weighted more heavily on Step 2. Here the skill is office management and the emergency you cannot miss.

## What this chapter covers

The continuing-care visit for depression, anxiety, and substance use, and the emergencies: suicide, withdrawal, overdose, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, and serotonin syndrome.

## The framework

Screen, then ask about suicide directly, then treat or refer based on severity. Mild to moderate depression in a willing adult can start with therapy, a medication, or both. An SSRI is a common medication. Ask about bipolar symptoms first. See the person again soon. The FDA warning about suicidal behavior in young people is a counseling point, not a reason to abandon treatment of a severe depression.

| Situation | What unsupervised practice requires |
| --- | --- |
| Suicidal with plan and intent | Emergency evaluation. You do not contract for safety and hang up. |
| Depression in pregnancy or postpartum | Treat. Untreated illness has a risk. Choose the drug with the perinatal data, and do not stop a stable effective drug without a plan. ACOG and psychiatry share this. |
| Alcohol withdrawal risk | Benzodiazepine protocol and thiamine in a setting that matches the risk. |
| Alcohol use disorder, not in withdrawal | Naltrexone or acamprosate plus psychosocial treatment. Offer it. |
| Opioid overdose | Naloxone, airway, observation that outlasts the naloxone. |
| Opioid use disorder | Buprenorphine or methadone. This is standard care. |
| Tobacco | Varenicline or combination nicotine replacement, plus support. |
| NMS versus serotonin syndrome | Rigid and bradyreflexic versus clonus and hyperreflexia. Stop the drug. The Step 2 chapter has the contrast. |
| Psychosis, new, older adult | Medical workup. Not a new label of schizophrenia. |
| Antipsychotic follow-up | Weight, glucose, lipids, and the movement exam. Clozapine adds the neutrophil schedule. |

Capacity remains decision-specific. A person with a serious mental illness can refuse a medicine they understand. Imminent danger is a separate, local legal process. Do not invent the hold statute of a generic state.

## High-yield atlas

Behavioral health on Step 3 is an office you run without a psychiatrist in the hallway, and an emergency you do not send home. Screen, ask about suicide directly, and treat or refer by severity. Mild to moderate depression in a willing adult can start with therapy, a medication, or both. Ask about bipolar symptoms before the first antidepressant. See the person again soon. The FDA warning about suicidal behavior in young people is a counseling point, not a reason to abandon a severe depression. CCS wants the safety question, the drug or the therapy, the follow-up on a calendar, and the substance-use offer you do not skip because the visit was “for a physical.”

### Suicide is not a contract

A plan, the means, and the intent are emergency evaluation. Asking does not plant the idea. A no-harm contract is not a discharge. Remove access to a firearm when you can do that with the person and the family. Jurisdiction rules decide an involuntary hold. Do not invent a statute. Chronic thoughts without a plan, means, or intent can be a close outpatient plan after a real assessment. That is a different stem. Read the intent line twice.

| What you see | Where they sleep | The order and the source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan, means, and intent | Emergency evaluation. Not a clinic next week | APA practice. Asking is required |
| Thoughts without plan, means, or intent, and a reliable support | Close outpatient follow-up after a full assessment | A contract is not the plan |
| A named person and a credible plan to harm them | Protect that person. Warn and notify police as the duty requires | State law varies. This is not a statute |

**Vignette.** He says he will shoot himself tonight. The gun is in the truck. He is calm. His sister is in the waiting room and says he “always talks like this.”
**Reasoning.** Calm is not safety. The plan, the means, and the intent are present. He stays, the gun is addressed, and a full assessment happens now. The diagnosis is active suicidal intent with means. The trap is a safety contract and a Thursday appointment because he is not agitated.
**NBME trap.** They avoid the question because asking might “give him the idea.” Asking is the action.
**Hook.** Plan, means, intent: he does not leave. A contract is not a lock.
**CCS.** Direct question, remove means, one-to-one observation, psychiatric assessment, do not advance the clock to discharge.

### Depression you start, and bipolar disease you do not ignite

At least two weeks of low mood or anhedonia, with the sleep, interest, energy, and concentration changes, and a drop in function, is a major depressive episode. Screen for a week of elevated mood, less need for sleep, and risky behavior before you hand over an SSRI. Psychotherapy is also first line for mild to moderate disease. An SSRI is a common medication when one is chosen and bipolar disease has been excluded. See them soon. Do not start an antidepressant the same week as an MAOI.

**Vignette.** Three weeks of low mood, she has stopped seeing friends, and she sleeps all day. She once “did not sleep for a week and spent the rent.” That was two years ago. She asks for “whatever antidepressant is common.”
**Reasoning.** The buried week without sleep and the spending is a manic episode. An antidepressant alone can ignite another one. The diagnosis is bipolar depression. A mood-stabilizing regimen, not an SSRI as the only drug. Confirm the current options, which include lithium, lamotrigine, and several antipsychotics with evidence. The trap is sertraline because the current week looks like ordinary depression.
**NBME trap.** They refuse to treat a 22-year-old with severe depression because of the FDA warning. The warning is a reason to see her soon, not a reason to leave a severe illness untreated.
**Hook.** Ask about the week without sleep before the first prescription. Then actually treat.
**CCS.** PHQ-style screen, suicide question, bipolar screen, therapy or a drug, follow-up within a short interval, no MAOI overlap, counseling on the FDA warning if she is young.

### Depression in pregnancy is treated

Untreated illness has a risk. Do not stop a stable effective drug without a plan. Choose the drug with the perinatal data. ACOG and psychiatry share this. A postpartum visit that skips mood is an incomplete visit. Preeclampsia can also present after birth. Do both jobs.

**Vignette.** She is 20 weeks and has been on sertraline for three years. A friend told her every psychiatric drug is forbidden in pregnancy. She stopped it last week and is already worse.
**Reasoning.** Restart the drug that was working, unless a specific contraindication appears, and do not leave her untreated for the rest of the pregnancy. The diagnosis is recurrent depression destabilized by stopping a working drug. The trap is agreeing that all antidepressants are forbidden.
**NBME trap.** They switch a stable patient to a new drug in the first trimester “to be safer” without a reason. A switch exposes her to two drugs and to relapse.
**Hook.** A stable effective drug stays, with perinatal counseling. Stopping it without a plan is the harm.
**CCS.** Restart or continue, obstetric and psychiatric follow-up, suicide screen, do not advance past a stopped drug.

### Alcohol withdrawal and the morning he still looks fine

Tremor, sweating, and tachycardia hours to a few days after the last drink, or a history of a withdrawal seizure, is a benzodiazepine protocol and thiamine in a setting that matches the risk. Looking acceptable at hour 8 does not retire the risk. Outpatient detox is the mild, supported case only. Phenytoin is not the treatment of a withdrawal seizure. Haloperidol is not monotherapy. It lowers the seizure threshold. Give thiamine with glucose. Do not delay sugar for a low fingerstick.

**Vignette.** He stopped drinking this morning. He is tremulous and tachycardic. He had a withdrawal seizure two years ago. He wants to “sweat it out” at his brother’s house. His brother works nights.
**Reasoning.** The prior seizure and the absent overnight support are a monitored benzodiazepine protocol, not a couch. Thiamine. The diagnosis is alcohol withdrawal at high risk. The trap is a chlordiazepoxide prescription and a wave because he can still walk.
**NBME trap.** They give phenytoin for the seizure history and no benzodiazepine. The seizure of withdrawal is treated by treating the withdrawal.
**Hook.** A prior seizure or no one at home: he stays. Mild and supported: outpatient can be right.
**CCS.** Symptom-triggered benzodiazepine, thiamine, glucose if low, monitor, folate and the rest of the nutritional care, do not discharge the high-risk patient.

### Alcohol use disorder after the shaking stops

Offer naltrexone or acamprosate plus a psychosocial treatment. Naltrexone is a poor choice when he needs opioids for pain or is in acute liver failure. Acamprosate depends on the kidney. Confirm the renal cutoff rather than inventing one. Disulfiram is not first line and is dangerous if you hide it in food. Do not do that.

**Vignette.** He has been sober for two weeks after a monitored detox. Liver tests are near normal. He does not need opioid pain medicine. He is willing.
**Reasoning.** Offer naltrexone or acamprosate and a real psychosocial treatment. The diagnosis is alcohol use disorder in early remission, ready for maintenance medication. The trap is a pamphlet as the whole offer, or disulfiram slipped into the plan because it is famous.
**NBME trap.** They prescribe naltrexone to a person on methadone, or to a person who will need oxycodone after surgery next week. Naltrexone blocks opioid analgesia and precipitates withdrawal if opioids are on board.
**Hook.** Offer a drug that has evidence. Match it to the liver, the kidney, and the opioid.
**CCS.** The medication, counseling or mutual-support referral, thiamine if he is depleted, follow-up, do not advance the clock after “I advised him to stop.”

### Opioids: the overdose and the disease

Pinpoint pupils and slow breathing are naloxone and an airway, then observation that outlasts the antidote. Methadone and long-acting or potent synthetic opioids often outlast one dose. Offer buprenorphine or methadone for the use disorder before he disappears. Start buprenorphine when withdrawal is present so you do not precipitate it. A moral objection is not a contraindication. Naltrexone is an option only after a full detox. Clonidine eases symptoms. It is not the disease treatment.

**Vignette.** His friend gave one dose of naloxone. He woke up and wants to leave. The opioid was a long-acting pill.
**Reasoning.** Observe. The pill may outlast the naloxone. Offer medication for the use disorder. The diagnosis is an opioid overdose that has been transiently reversed. The trap is a discharge from the ambulance bay because he is awake and annoyed.
**NBME trap.** They require a punitive detox before buprenorphine. That delay is how people die. Withdrawal is the moment buprenorphine can start, not a character test.
**Hook.** Awake after naloxone is not the end. The opioid and the use disorder are both still there.
**CCS.** Airway, naloxone repeated as needed, observation, offer buprenorphine or methadone, a naloxone kit for later, do not advance to the parking lot.

### Tobacco is a drug offer, not a lecture

Varenicline or combination nicotine replacement, plus support. USPSTF recommends pharmacotherapy and behavioral support for adults who smoke. Bupropion is another option and is a poor choice in a seizure disorder or bulimia. Confirm labeling. Do not invent a dose. A patient who is not ready is a motivational conversation, not a hidden prescription and not a speech.

**Vignette.** He smokes a pack a day and is ready to try. He has bulimia. Someone offers bupropion because it might help the appetite too.
**Reasoning.** Bupropion and bulimia are a bad pair because of seizures. Varenicline or combination nicotine replacement. The diagnosis is tobacco use disorder in a person with a bupropion contraindication. The trap is one drug chosen because it treats two problems on a mnemonic.
**NBME trap.** They stop at “I advised cessation” in a person who asked for a medication. Advice plus a drug is the offer.
**Hook.** Ready: a drug and support. Bulimia or seizures: not bupropion.
**CCS.** The medication, a quit date, follow-up, no lecture as the only order.

### NMS, serotonin syndrome, and catatonia

Lead-pipe rigidity, fever, autonomic instability, bradyreflexia, and a high creatine kinase on a dopamine blocker is neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Stop the drug. Cool. Fluid. A monitored bed. Bromocriptine or dantrolene is specialist care. Confirm before you use either. Clonus, hyperreflexia, and diarrhea over hours after a serotonergic combination is serotonin syndrome. Stop the drugs. A benzodiazepine. Cyproheptadine if it is not settling. Do not give bromocriptine for serotonin syndrome. Mutism, waxy flexibility, and posturing are catatonia. A benzodiazepine challenge. An antipsychotic can worsen it. Electroconvulsive therapy is the step for malignant or refractory catatonia.

**Vignette.** Haloperidol was increased three days ago. He is rigid, febrile, and confused. Reflexes are dull. Creatine kinase is in the thousands.
**Reasoning.** Stop every dopamine blocker, including metoclopramide. Cool him and fill the tank. The diagnosis is neuroleptic malignant syndrome. The trap is benztropine, which treats acute dystonia, or cyproheptadine, which treats serotonin syndrome.
**NBME trap.** They see clonus and hyperreflexia and call it neuroleptic malignant syndrome because a fever is present. Brisk reflexes with clonus are serotonin. Dull reflexes and lead-pipe rigidity are the dopamine-blocker emergency.
**Hook.** Dull reflexes and rigidity: stop the blocker. Clonus: stop the serotonergic drugs.
**CCS.** Stop the offending drug, monitor, cooling and fluid for NMS, benzodiazepine for serotonin syndrome, benzodiazepine challenge for catatonia, do not add another antipsychotic.

### Psychosis in an older adult, and the antipsychotic follow-up

New psychosis in an older adult is a medical workup. Not a new label of schizophrenia. Delirium, a drug, a mass, a seizure, and dementia with a cause you can find come first. The follow-up of a person who is correctly on an antipsychotic is weight, glucose, lipids, and a movement exam. Clozapine adds a neutrophil schedule. Do not invent the ANC cutoff. Open the REMS program. Severe constipation on clozapine is an ileus until proven otherwise, not a stool softener and a wave.

**Vignette.** He is 68. Two weeks of hearing voices. No psychiatric history. He takes a new anticholinergic for the bladder. The family says yesterday was worse than this morning.
**Reasoning.** This is delirium or another medical cause until you have looked. Stop the new drug and do the medical evaluation. The diagnosis is new-onset psychosis in an older adult, presumed secondary. The trap is risperidone and a schizophrenia label on the first visit.
**NBME trap.** They skip the metabolic lab and the movement exam on a young adult who has been on olanzapine for a year because “the psychiatry is stable.” Stable psychosis still gains weight and still gets diabetes. The visit is the labs.
**Hook.** New voices at 68: a medical workup. A year of an antipsychotic: weight, glucose, lipids, and the movements.
**CCS.** The medical workup, medication review, no new lifelong antipsychotic as the first order, follow-up labs if a drug is continued.

### Eating disorders and the heart rate in the 30s

Restriction, a markedly low weight, and bradycardia, hypothermia, syncope, or an electrolyte abnormality is inpatient care. Refeed on a protocol and watch the phosphate. Do not send her to weekly therapy as the only plan while the heart rate is in the 30s. An SSRI does not restore weight. Fluoxetine has a role in bulimia, which is a different weight and a different emergency: the hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis from purging.

**Vignette.** She feels fine. Heart rate is 38. Temperature is 35.5. Potassium is 2.9. She has been purging.
**Reasoning.** Admit. Replete the potassium and watch the phosphate when you feed her. The diagnosis is a medically unstable eating disorder. The trap is outpatient therapy because her mood seemed all right and she minimized the symptoms.
**NBME trap.** They start fluoxetine for the low weight of anorexia and call it the treatment. Fluoxetine does not restore weight. It is a bulimia conversation, after she is medically safe, and only when that is the diagnosis.
**Hook.** A heart rate in the 30s is a hospital. The therapy comes after the phosphate is being watched.
**CCS.** Monitor, electrolytes, a refeeding protocol, phosphate, admission, psychiatry. Do not advance to a weekly clinic.

### Panic is a diagnosis you earn

Recurrent abrupt surges of palpitations and fear, peaking in minutes, plus a month of worry about the next one, after a medical look that fits the risk, is panic disorder. Cognitive behavioral therapy, an SSRI, or both. A benzodiazepine can abort a severe attack and is not the five-year plan. Exertional chest pain in a 60-year-old smoker is not panic. An electrocardiogram comes first.

**Vignette.** She is 26. Spells of pounding heart and doom, at rest, lasting minutes, for two months. She worries between them. The exam is normal. No exertional pain.
**Reasoning.** This pattern is panic disorder. Therapy, an SSRI, or both. The diagnosis is panic disorder. The trap is a benzodiazepine prescription with five refills as the disease treatment, or a missed thyroid and a missed stimulant.
**NBME trap.** They diagnose panic from a single exertional chest pain in an older smoker and skip the electrocardiogram. Medical first when the story is medical.
**Hook.** Rest, minutes, worry between attacks: panic treatment. Exertional, older, vascular risk: the heart first.
**CCS.** The medical look the risk requires, then therapy or an SSRI, a short follow-up, no chronic benzodiazepine as the plan.

### Trauma is a therapy first

More than a month of intrusion, avoidance, negative mood, and hyperarousal after a traumatic event is post-traumatic stress disorder. Less than a month is acute stress disorder. VA/DoD puts trauma-focused psychotherapy first. Sertraline, paroxetine, and venlafaxine are the medication names those guidelines discuss. Confirm the file. A benzodiazepine does not treat the disorder and can worsen it.

**Vignette.** Three months after an assault she cannot sleep, avoids the street it happened on, and startles at the door. She asks for “something to knock me out.”
**Reasoning.** Offer a trauma-focused therapy. If a medication is used, an SSRI or venlafaxine, not a benzodiazepine as the treatment. The diagnosis is post-traumatic stress disorder. The trap is a standing benzodiazepine because sleep was the loudest symptom.
**NBME trap.** They call three weeks of the same picture PTSD and start a long drug plan that ignores the acute-stress timeframe. The treatment posture is similar. The name follows the month. Do not refuse care because the month is not complete.
**Hook.** Therapy first. A benzodiazepine is not the disease treatment.
**CCS.** The therapy referral, an SSRI if a drug is chosen, safety screen, follow-up, no benzodiazepine monotherapy.

### A second set of stems

**Vignette.** He is on lithium. A new NSAID was started for a knee. He is vomiting and ataxic.
**Reasoning.** Stop the lithium and the NSAID. Check the level and the creatinine. Hydrate. The diagnosis is lithium toxicity provoked by an NSAID. The trap is a neurology referral for a new ataxia while both drugs continue.
**Hook.** Lithium plus an NSAID, a thiazide, an ACE inhibitor, or dehydration: think toxicity before a new diagnosis.

**Vignette.** She has schizophrenia, understands the antipsychotic, and refuses it. She is not dangerous. She can explain the relapse risk.
**Reasoning.** Capacity is decision-specific. She can refuse. The diagnosis is a capacitated refusal. The trap is a hold because the diagnosis is schizophrenia. Danger is a separate, local legal path. Do not invent it.
**Hook.** Understands and refuses: honor it. Imminent danger: a different, local process.

**Vignette.** A tremor and a twisted neck two hours after the first dose of haloperidol. He is not febrile. Reflexes are normal.
**Reasoning.** Acute dystonia. Diphenhydramine or benztropine. The diagnosis is an acute dystonic reaction. The trap is the neuroleptic malignant syndrome protocol for a twisted neck without fever.
**Hook.** Hours and a twisted posture: an anticholinergic. Days, fever, and rigidity: stop the drug and cool.

**Vignette.** He cannot stop pacing a week after an antipsychotic increase. He is not psychotic in a new way. He says the body will not sit.
**Reasoning.** Akathisia. Lower the dose or add a beta blocker. Do not add more antipsychotic. The diagnosis is akathisia.
**Hook.** Pacing, not psychosis: akathisia. More of the drug makes it worse.

**Vignette.** Months of lip smacking on a long-standing antipsychotic. He is otherwise at his baseline.
**Reasoning.** Tardive dyskinesia. Reassess the drug. The diagnosis is tardive dyskinesia. The trap is benztropine, which does not treat this and adds anticholinergic harm.
**Hook.** Late and choreiform: not an acute dystonia. Do not reach for benztropine.

**Vignette.** Grief six weeks after a spouse’s death. She is sad, she is eating, she has no wish to die, and she is functioning at a funeral and then at work in short pieces.
**Reasoning.** This is grief. Sit with her. Do not start an SSRI because sadness appeared. The diagnosis is grief. The trap is a major-depression label at week two without a function collapse, or a missed depression when she stops eating and develops a wish to die. The fork is function and suicidality, not the fact of tears.
**Hook.** Grief can look like depression. A wish to die or a collapse of function moves it.

**Vignette.** He wants to stop drinking and is still in moderate withdrawal, diaphoretic, tonight.
**Reasoning.** The benzodiazepine and thiamine come first. Naltrexone is not the only order on this night. The diagnosis is acute withdrawal, with a use disorder you will treat after. The trap is a maintenance drug as the sole order while he is still shaking.
**Hook.** Shaking: benzodiazepine. The next week: naltrexone or acamprosate.

**Vignette.** A positive urine for opioids at a buprenorphine visit. He is otherwise engaged and not sedated.
**Reasoning.** The urine is information. It is not an automatic discharge from treatment. The diagnosis is an opioid use disorder that is not yet in remission. Adjust the care. The trap is stopping the medication as punishment.
**Hook.** A positive urine is a clinical fact. It is not a moral verdict.

## Checklist

- [ ] Ask about suicide in plain language.
- [ ] Screen for mania before an antidepressant.
- [ ] Offer medication for opioid and alcohol use disorders.
- [ ] Naloxone observation outlasts the dose.
- [ ] Metabolic labs for the antipsychotic you prescribed.

## Common traps

- “Follow up in three months” after a new antidepressant and a passive death wish.
- Withholding buprenorphine until some moral milestone.
- Haloperidol for catatonia or for severe withdrawal.
- A feed card that treats every insomnia with a benzodiazepine. Look at the cause, the age, and the dependence risk.
