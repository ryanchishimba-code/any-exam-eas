# CCS case patterns

> **Study aid only.** Patterns, not scripts to memorize word for word. If a real person would need a different order, the real person wins. Confirm drug names against a current label when you practice.

## What this chapter covers

The first minutes of the complaints that recur: chest pain, shortness of breath, abdominal pain, altered mental status, and pregnancy. The framework chapter is the order. This chapter is the content you drop into that order.

## The framework

For each pattern, the first line is the site. The second is what you do before the diagnosis is pretty. The third is the test that changes the next hour. The fourth is disposition and counseling.

| Complaint | First minutes | Do not forget |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chest pain | Emergency department if it could be ischemic, dissecting, or embolic. Monitor, access, ECG, aspirin if no contraindication. | A chest film if the lung is in the story. Do not send them for an outpatient stress test from the office. |
| Shortness of breath | Oxygen if hypoxic, exam of lungs and heart and legs, peak flow or a gas if they are tiring. | Bronchodilator and steroid for a tight asthmatic. Noninvasive ventilation for acidemic COPD. A chest film. Think embolus, failure, and pneumonia, not just the inhaler. |
| Abdominal pain | Pregnancy test in anyone who could be pregnant, before the radiograph. Exam for peritonitis. | Bilious vomiting in a baby and a rigid abdomen in an adult are surgical. Cholangitis and a bleeding ulcer are not clinic problems. |
| Altered mental status | Glucose, oxygen, naloxone if opioid is possible, a focused neuro exam. | Then infection, withdrawal, sodium, and the medicine list. Do not lead with a sedative. |
| Fever | Site, cultures when you will give an intravenous antibiotic, the antibiotic if the person is unstable or neutropenic. | A source you can drain. A travel or exposure history after the antibiotic is in, not before, when they are septic. |
| Headache, sudden or with deficits | Emergency imaging. Glucose. | Temporal arteritis gets a steroid if the story is classic and vision is at risk. Migraine without flags does not get a CT to be kind. |
| Prenatal visit | Blood pressure, symptom screen, the lab that trimester needs. | Move to the hospital for bleeding, severe headache, or reduced movement that worries you. |
| Depression or substance use | Ask about suicide and withdrawal. | A monitored bed for significant withdrawal. Naloxone for overdose. Do not end the case without a follow-up. |
| Trauma or a procedure complication | Airway, breathing, bleeding. | Tension pneumothorax is clinical. Stop a transfusion that is causing a reaction. |
| Diabetes, sick | Fluids, potassium, insulin when it is DKA, hold the SGLT2 inhibitor. | Find the trigger. A glucose that is not extreme does not exclude SGLT2-associated ketoacidosis. |

Counseling that belongs on a stable case: tobacco, the medicine they must not stop (an antiepileptic, a pillar of heart failure), a follow-up day, and when to return. Counseling that does not belong: a dietary lecture during a resuscitation.

When the case advances and the person improves, de-escalate. Stop oxygen that is no longer needed. Narrow antibiotics if a culture returns. Convert intravenous medicine to oral. Move them down a level of care. Continuing the emergency orders into day three is a different kind of error.

## High-yield atlas

The first minutes of the complaints that recur. Site, the treatment that cannot wait, the test that changes the next hour, then disposition.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Office. Pressure in the chest for twenty minutes, sweat, a normal oxygen. You have not ordered an ECG.
**Reasoning.** Emergency department. ECG and aspirin before a long history. A normal first troponin does not send them home. The trap is a proton-pump inhibitor trial for a twenty-minute pressure.

**Vignette 2.** Emergency department. Wheezing, speaking in words, a normal CO2, and a quiet chest.
**Reasoning.** A normal CO2 in a severe asthma attack is a warning. Oxygen, repeated bronchodilator, steroid, and a monitored bed. The trap is a prescription and a discharge because the number looks normal.

**Vignette 3.** Known COPD, sleepy, a low pH, a high CO2.
**Reasoning.** Noninvasive ventilation if nothing contraindicates it, in a monitored bed. High-flow oxygen as comfort can make the CO2 worse. The trap is intubating before a trial of noninvasive ventilation when the airway is still protected, or the opposite trap of leaving them on a cannula.

**Vignette 4.** Fever, neck stiffness, and a CT scanner that is busy.
**Reasoning.** Antibiotics before a delayed lumbar puncture. The trap is a perfect cerebrospinal fluid that arrives after the hour you lost. Dexamethasone timing follows the current IDSA file.

**Vignette 5.** Third-trimester headache and a blood pressure of 170/110. Vision is blurred.
**Reasoning.** Hospital. Magnesium. This is not the adult PREVENT threshold. Delivery planning is ACOG. The trap is a clinic recheck tomorrow.

**Vignette 6.** A child with green vomit.
**Reasoning.** Surgical emergency until malrotation with volvulus is excluded. The trap is a formula change. Pyloric stenosis is non-bilious and a slightly older infant.

**Vignette 7.** Postoperative day one, a fever of 39, and a wound that is bronze.
**Reasoning.** Open the wound. This can be clostridial or streptococcal. The trap is a new antibiotic and a dressing.

**Vignette 8.** A glucose of 500, an anion gap, and ketones. The patient is on an SGLT2 inhibitor and the glucose is only 180 in a second stem.
**Reasoning.** Fluids, potassium plan, insulin for ketoacidosis, and hold the SGLT2 inhibitor. Euglycemic ketoacidosis still counts. The trap is a normal glucose used to exclude ketosis.

**Vignette 9.** A suicidal patient with a plan and a means, in the office at 4 p.m.
**Reasoning.** Emergency evaluation. You do not contract for safety and send them to a clinic next week. Ask directly. The trap is a bus pass.

**Vignette 10.** A stable migraine, no red flags, three attacks a week, and daily as-needed pills.
**Reasoning.** A preventive and a stop to medication overuse. No CT to be kind. The trap is imaging a chronic headache that has no flag, or refilling the as-needed drug that is now the disease.

## Second atlas

The framework chapter is the order. These are the cases, written as the orders you place and the moment you advance the clock. Each one is a pattern the outline keeps returning to. None of them has a secret point value. The Bulletin’s statement is the only weight this book will claim.

### Shortness of breath, three different beds

A person who speaks in sentences, saturates well, and has a known asthma history can be treated and often sent home with a steroid and a controller. A person who speaks in words or has a normal carbon dioxide while tiring stays, gets oxygen, repeated bronchodilator, ipratropium, and a steroid, and is not ended. A person with COPD who is acidemic and still protecting the airway gets noninvasive ventilation in a monitored bed, oxygen toward the COPD saturation target rather than “as much as possible,” a steroid, a bronchodilator, and an antibiotic when the sputum is purulent. Advance the clock twenty or thirty minutes and read the gas again. A six-hour jump is how the mask fails off-screen.

**Vignette.** The case opens in the office. Oxygen saturation is 88 percent. He is sleepy. pH is 7.27. You prescribe an inhaler and end.
**Reasoning.** Move him. Noninvasive ventilation, a monitored bed, the exacerbation medicines. The diagnosis is a hypercapnic COPD exacerbation. Ending is the miss. The trap is treating the saturation with high-flow oxygen and no gas.
**Hook.** Sleepy and acidemic: the mask and a short clock. Sentences and a rising peak flow: home, with the controller.
**CCS.** Location change, oxygen to the COPD target, blood gas, noninvasive ventilation, steroid, nebulizer, antibiotic if purulent, recheck the gas, counsel tobacco when he can talk.

### Chest pain that is a clot, not a treadmill

Pleuritic pain, hypoxia, and a swollen leg are a pulmonary embolism pathway. A hypotensive patient is resuscitated where he is and reperfused if there is no contraindication. A stable confirmed clot is anticoagulation. A filter is not the first idea if he can take a drug. Do not advance him to a stress test. Do not give aspirin as the antithrombotic and feel finished.

**Vignette.** Pressure 78/50, clear lungs, distended neck veins, sudden pleuritic pain. You order a CT two floors away and advance the clock an hour.
**Reasoning.** He may not survive the trip. Bedside ultrasound, thrombolysis if there is no contraindication, and a monitored bed. The diagnosis is a massive pulmonary embolism. The trap is a perfect scan and a dead patient.
**Hook.** Pressure in the 70s: reperfuse where you are. Normal pressure: anticoagulate and choose the bed.
**CCS.** Two lines, oxygen, monitor, bedside ultrasound, the reperfusion drug if crashing, anticoagulation if stable, no long clock jump to radiology.

### Abdominal pain, the four that do not stay in the office

Right-upper-quadrant pain, fever, and jaundice are cholangitis: antibiotics and drainage, not a clinic ultrasound tomorrow. A nonreducible hernia with vomiting is a surgeon today. Painless third-trimester bleeding is ultrasound and no digital exam. One-sided pain, dizziness, and a positive pregnancy test with an empty uterus is an ectopic pathway, and hypotension is the operating room. Everything else can be a slower abdominal pain evaluation. These four cannot.

**Vignette.** Fever, jaundice, and a tender right upper quadrant. Blood pressure is falling. You schedule an outpatient ultrasound and start an oral antibiotic.
**Reasoning.** Hospital, intravenous antibiotics, and drainage. The diagnosis is acute cholangitis. The trap is the oral plan.
**Hook.** The Charcot picture goes to a drain. A hernia that will not reduce goes to a surgeon. Late painless bleeding does not get a finger. A dizzy pregnant patient with an empty uterus does not get a next-day hCG.
**CCS.** Location, fluid, antibiotics, the procedure or the surgeon, pregnancy test before radiation, no digital exam for previa.

### Altered mental status, the fingerstick and the three lookalikes

Glucose first. Naloxone if the breathing is slow and the pupils are small, then observe because the drug may outlast the antidote. A benzodiazepine and thiamine if this is alcohol withdrawal, and do not use phenytoin as the plan. A stroke pathway if the deficit is focal: glucose, a non-contrast CT, last known well, and do not drop the blood pressure into a tidy range. A lumbar puncture if the neck is stiff, with the antibiotic before a delayed tap. Advance the clock to the fingerstick immediately. It is not a morning lab.

**Vignette.** Found down. You order a CT, a neurology consult, and a toxicology send-out, and you advance two hours. No glucose was ordered.
**Reasoning.** The fingerstick would have changed the next minute. The diagnosis is hypoglycemia until a number exists. The other orders can follow. They do not lead.
**Hook.** Glucose, pupils, oxygen. Then the scan. Withdrawal gets a benzodiazepine. A focal deficit gets a CT before anyone treats the pressure for sport.

### The prenatal case that hides a lab

Every prenatal case: blood pressure, symptoms of preeclampsia, the lab that is due, fetal movement when it is time. A positive HIV or syphilis test is treatment now. Rh-negative and bleeding, and she is not already sensitized: anti-D. Severe headache and a high pressure on day 5 after birth: magnesium, not a migraine pamphlet. Do not end the case on “routine prenatal counseling” while a positive result sits unread.

**Vignette.** You advanced two weeks for a routine visit. The syphilis test from the first visit is positive. You counsel on diet and end.
**Reasoning.** Treat the syphilis. The diagnosis is untreated syphilis in pregnancy. Diet counseling was not the result you were handed.
**Hook.** Read the lab before you end. Pregnancy does not defer HIV or syphilis. Postpartum hypertension is still preeclampsia.

### The child case

Bilious vomiting: surgeon, nothing by mouth, fluid, no formula change. Fever in a neonate: the current evaluation, not a virus speech. Bronchiolitis that is drinking and saturating: support, and no routine albuterol-steroid-antibiotic bundle. A femur fracture in a child who cannot roll: treat the bone and report. Loss of a skill: refer. A weight is required before a drug, and this book still will not list the dose. Do not end a pediatric case in the office when the child belongs in a hospital.

**Vignette.** A four-month-old with a femur fracture. The story is that he rolled off a couch. He cannot roll. You splint and end.
**Reasoning.** The splint is half. The report is the other half. The diagnosis is suspected nonaccidental trauma. Ending without the report is the miss.
**Hook.** Development that cannot cause the injury is a report. Green vomit is a surgeon. A febrile neonate is not a cold.

### The psychiatric emergency inside a medical case

Ask about suicide directly when the mood is low or the visit is odd. A plan, the means, and the intent stay. A contract does not discharge them. Alcohol withdrawal that includes a prior seizure stays for a benzodiazepine protocol. An opioid overdose that just woke up stays because the opioid may outlast the naloxone, and you offer buprenorphine or methadone before the case ends. Do not end on “counseled to stop using.”

**Vignette.** He says he will shoot himself tonight and the gun is in the car. You contract for safety and advance to a clinic visit next week.
**Reasoning.** He stays. The means are addressed. The diagnosis is suicidal intent with a plan and access. The contract is the trap.
**Hook.** Plan, means, intent: do not end. Offer the medication for the use disorder on the overdose case. A pamphlet is not a treatment.

### The prevention case, last, and only when he is stable

When the person is safe, order the grade A and B service that matches the age, offer a tobacco drug if he smokes and is ready, ask the depression question, and place the vaccine that is due. Do not do this block first on a hypotensive patient. Do not order a decorative chemistry panel and a chest radiograph and call it a physical. End only after the counseling orders exist.

**Vignette.** Pneumonia treated, saturating well, eating, going home. You end without a follow-up, a vaccine, or a smoking order. He smokes.
**Reasoning.** Those three orders are the end of a stable case. The diagnosis is a correct medical plan with the discharge undone. Add them, then end.
**Hook.** Unstable: do not end. Stable: counsel, then end. The end button is the last order, not an escape.

### A last set of clocks

**Vignette.** Neutropenic fever. The absolute neutrophil count is pending. You advance four hours to wait for hematology to call back. No antibiotic is in.
**Reasoning.** The antibiotic is now. The count can arrive whenever it arrives. The diagnosis is febrile neutropenia. Waiting is the harm.
**Hook.** Fever after chemotherapy: the drug, then the clock.

**Vignette.** One hot knee. You start antibiotics and advance a day. No tap was ordered. Minutes were available.
**Reasoning.** Tap first. The diagnosis is a septic joint until the fluid says otherwise, and gout can share it. Antibiotics before the needle, when you had minutes, throw away the culture.
**Hook.** Minutes exist: the needle, then the drug.

**Vignette.** Warfarin, a sudden headache, a CT that shows blood. You give vitamin K and advance a day.
**Reasoning.** A factor product is the fast reversal. Vitamin K makes it last. The diagnosis is an anticoagulated intracranial hemorrhage. Vitamin K alone is the trap.
**Hook.** Name the anticoagulant. Warfarin’s fast reversal is not a vitamin.

**Vignette.** The case is a well-child visit. You order a CT of the head because the parent is anxious about a normal fontanelle, and you give an antibiotic for a clear runny nose.
**Reasoning.** Plot the growth, give the vaccine that is due, and counsel. The diagnosis of the error is harm dressed up as thoroughness. Anxiety is not an indication.
**Hook.** A well visit is vaccines, growth, and the milestone. It is not a scan.

**Vignette.** Heart failure, ejection fraction 30 percent, dry, on furosemide only. You increase the diuretic and end.
**Reasoning.** Add a pillar he is missing. The diuretic treats fluid he does not have today. The diagnosis is under-treated heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. The trap is the bottle he brought.
**Hook.** Dry heart failure: the pillars. Wet heart failure: unload. Do not confuse the bottles.

**Vignette.** You are about to CT the abdomen of a woman of childbearing age. No pregnancy test.
**Reasoning.** The test is an order. It takes a minute. The diagnosis you might be about to irradiate is a pregnancy. Place the test, read it, then decide.
**Hook.** Pregnancy test before radiation and before a teratogenic drug. Every time the stem allows it.

**Vignette.** The consultant button is available for a straightforward decision you can make. You press it and advance a day waiting for the note.
**Reasoning.** Make the decision. Consult when you would call in the hospital: a surgeon for a surgical abdomen, a cardiologist for a reperfusion, an obstetrician for a severe preeclampsia. The trap is consulting to avoid an antibiotic you already know is right.
**Hook.** The consultant is a tool. It is not a substitute for the order.

**Vignette.** Results return: a critical potassium of 6.8 and a note that the ECG shows a wide QRS. You had advanced to tomorrow.
**Reasoning.** See the patient now. Calcium, the shift, and the cause. The diagnosis is hyperkalemia with ECG changes. Tomorrow is the error the clock already made. Do not make it twice.
**Hook.** A critical result cancels the clock. Treat, then advance a little, then read it again.

## Checklist

- [ ] Pregnancy test before imaging when pregnancy would change the test or the drug.
- [ ] Glucose and naloxone early in altered mental status.
- [ ] ECG early in chest pain.
- [ ] Site of care changes when the story declares itself.
- [ ] De-escalate when they improve.

## Common traps

- The same panel of labs on every case, including a TSH and a cortisol for a sprained ankle.
- Forgetting the pregnancy test and ordering a CT.
- Ending the case in the office with untreated chest pain.
- Leaving a person on a ventilator setting, a pressor, or nothing-by-mouth with no reassessment after the clock jumps to the next morning.
