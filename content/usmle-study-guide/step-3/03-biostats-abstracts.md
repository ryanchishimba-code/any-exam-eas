# Biostatistics, abstracts, and ads

## Framework

Day 1 gives you a paper or an advertisement and asks what it can support. Read the design before the p-value.

| Design | What you may say | What you may not say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Randomized trial | The assignment can support a causal claim, if the trial was actually randomized and you respect intention-to-treat. | A subgroup that was not planned is not a new indication. |
| Cohort | Relative risk, if exposure came first. | It is not free of confounding. |
| Case-control | Odds ratio. | Not a relative risk, and not an incidence. |
| Cross-section | A prevalence association. | Not "this exposure caused that." |
| Case series | A description. | Not a treatment effect. |

**The ad**

A pharmaceutical ad is marketing plus selected numbers. Ask: compared with what, in whom, for which outcome, and with what harm. A significant p-value on a surrogate (a lab) is not proof patients lived longer. A "number needed to treat" printed in large type still needs the time horizon and the baseline risk. If the control was a placebo and a standard drug exists, the ad has not shown the new drug is the one you should start.

**The abstract**

1. What was the question.
2. Who was excluded. Exclusions are where the result stops applying.
3. Primary endpoint, prespecified.
4. Absolute difference, not only the ratio.
5. Harms.
6. Who paid.

Lead-time bias, length-time bias, and confounding mean the same things they meant on earlier steps. Precision is a narrow interval. Accuracy is hitting the truth. A huge trial can be precisely wrong if the design is biased.

## Checklist

- [ ] Name the design in one word before you believe the conclusion.
- [ ] Intention-to-treat stays with the assigned group.
- [ ] Absolute difference before relative fanfare.
- [ ] A surrogate endpoint is a lab until it is a patient outcome.
- [ ] An ad is not a guideline.

## Common traps

- Treating a p-value under 0.05 as clinical importance.
- Applying a trial of healthy 50-year-olds to a frail 85-year-old without saying the limit.
- Calling a case-control result a risk.
