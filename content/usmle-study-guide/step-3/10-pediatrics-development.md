# Pediatrics and development

> **Study aid only.** A generalist sees children. The AAP and the CDC schedule are the sources. Human development is a small MCQ row and a real part of continuing care. Weight-based errors are how children are harmed. This page does not list doses.

## What this chapter covers

The well-child visit, the newborn problems that are surgical, and the adolescent visit that is actually confidential care. Step 3 expects you to function without a pediatric specialist in the hallway for the common problems, and to know when that is no longer true.

## The framework

Well-child care is growth, development, vaccines, safety, and one disease the age suggests. Plot the growth. A single point is not failure to thrive. A falling percentile is. Loss of a skill is not a variant. Refer.

| Age or complaint | Do not miss |
| --- | --- |
| Newborn, bilious vomiting | Malrotation until proven otherwise. Surgery. |
| Young infant, projectile non-bilious vomiting | Pyloric stenosis. Fix the electrolytes. Then operate. |
| Neonatal jaundice | Timing and the bilirubin fraction. Hemolysis and sepsis are the dangerous early causes. Phototherapy thresholds come from the current AAP chart, not from a color. |
| Bronchiolitis | Support. The AAP recommends against routine albuterol, steroids, and antibiotics. |
| Fever in a neonate | A real evaluation. Not reassurance. Use the current febrile-infant guideline. |
| Asthma or croup | See the Step 2 pediatrics chapter for the acute drugs. Disposition is whether the child is tiring. |
| Suspected abuse | Report. A mismatched story and a developmentally impossible injury are the clues. |
| Adolescent | Confidential time. HEADSS-style questions. Vaccines including HPV as the CDC schedule says. Contraception and STI care without a parent in the room when the law allows. Know your jurisdiction. Do not guess it. |
| Vaccines | The CDC schedule. Live vaccines are not for severe immunosuppression. Pertussis vaccine in every pregnancy protects the newborn who is too young to be vaccinated. |
| Dehydration | Oral rehydration if the child drinks. Intravenous if not. |

Development you should be able to defend, then confirm against the current CDC milestones: social smile early, sitting around the middle of the first year, walking and a few words by about a year and a half, and any loss of skill at any time. The exact month on a revised checklist beats the month in an old lecture.

## High-yield atlas

A generalist on Step 3 sees children. Weight-based errors are how they are harmed, and this page does not list doses. Plot the growth. A single point is not failure to thrive. A falling percentile is. Loss of a skill is not a variant. The CDC schedule and the AAP policy are the sources, and both are revised. CCS pediatric cases fail when a bilious newborn is sent home, when a neonate with fever is called a virus, and when a falling growth curve is labeled a picky eater.

### The newborn who needs a surgeon

Bilious vomiting is malrotation with volvulus until proven otherwise. The operating room, not a formula change. Projectile nonbilious vomiting in a young infant, after a period of normal feeds, is pyloric stenosis. Correct the hypokalemic hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, then a pyloromyotomy. Do not take a stable baby to the operating room before the potassium is safe, and do not send an alkalotic baby home to “try a different nipple.”

| What you see | Where the child sleeps | The order and the source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bilious emesis in an infant | Operating room | Pediatric surgery. Volvulus until proven otherwise |
| Projectile nonbilious emesis, a young infant, an alkalosis | Correct electrolytes, then operate | Not a crash laparotomy if the baby is stable and depleted |
| A groin bulge that comes and goes | Surgical referral. Incarcerated is not elective | A nonreducible, miserable infant is an emergency |
| One hip that clunks | Ultrasound in the young infant, referral | Developmental dysplasia. Do not wait for a limp |

**Vignette.** A three-week-old has vomited green fluid twice. The abdomen is flat. He is otherwise “a good baby.” The family wants to try a sensitive formula.
**Reasoning.** Green emesis is bilious until someone proves it is not. He needs a surgical evaluation now, not a formula aisle. The diagnosis is suspected malrotation with midgut volvulus. The trap is reassurance because the belly is soft. Early volvulus can be soft.
**NBME trap.** They operate immediately on a stable pyloric stenosis before correcting the alkalosis, or they do the reverse and delay a bilious baby for a full electrolyte panel and a nap. Read the color of the vomit.
**Hook.** Green: surgery now. Projectile and alkalotic: fix the salt, then the muscle.
**CCS.** For bilious emesis: surgical consult, imaging the surgeons want, fluid, nothing by mouth, do not advance the clock to a formula change. For pyloric stenosis: electrolytes, fluid, surgery when he is resuscitated.

### Jaundice is a timing problem

Early jaundice, the first day, is hemolysis or sepsis until proven otherwise. It is not “a little yellow.” Physiologic jaundice has a timing and a height that the current AAP chart owns. This chapter does not print a bilirubin number. Open the chart. Direct hyperbilirubinemia is a different disease, including biliary atresia, and it does not get a “it will fade” speech. Pale stools and dark urine are the clue.

**Vignette.** A 36-hour-old is jaundiced. Mom’s blood type is O and the baby’s is A. He is feeding poorly.
**Reasoning.** Jaundice on day one plus a setup for ABO incompatibility is hemolysis until the bilirubin fraction and the hematocrit say otherwise, and poor feeding is not a reason to skip the evaluation. The diagnosis is suspected hemolytic jaundice. The trap is a “physiologic, see you at two weeks” plan on the first day of life.
**NBME trap.** They treat prolonged direct hyperbilirubinemia with phototherapy advice and no workup of the duct. Phototherapy is for unconjugated bilirubin. A direct fraction is a liver and a biliary emergency, not a light.
**Hook.** Day one: hemolysis or infection. Direct fraction or pale stools: the ducts, not a week of sunshine.
**CCS.** Fractionated bilirubin, the AAP threshold if it is unconjugated, feeding support, and a surgical path if the direct fraction is the story. Do not invent the light level.

### Fever in a neonate is not a virus

A febrile neonate gets a real evaluation. Do not apply the “viral syndrome, go home” script. The current AAP febrile-infant guideline has replaced the single cutoff many people memorized. Follow that guideline rather than a day-of-life number from a senior’s pocket card. This chapter will not print a day cutoff it might have wrong. The spirit that must survive every revision is: the youngest infants do not go home on reassurance.

**Vignette.** A 10-day-old has a rectal temperature of 38.4 and is “mostly himself.” The parents prefer not to traumatize him with tests.
**Reasoning.** This is a hospital evaluation. The diagnosis is fever in a neonate. The trap is a viral diagnosis because he looks only a little off. Neonates do not have to look terrible to be bacteremic.
**NBME trap.** They apply an older-infant “low risk, home” pathway to a neonate because the white count was normal. A normal white count is not a discharge.
**Hook.** The youngest febrile infants stay for a real evaluation. A well appearance is not a culture.
**CCS.** The evaluation the current febrile-infant guideline requires, antibiotics when that guideline says to give them before the results, admission for the neonate, do not advance to home.

### Breathing: bronchiolitis, croup, and the tired child

Bronchiolitis is support. The AAP recommends against routine albuterol, routine steroids, and routine antibiotics. Oxygen and hydration if he needs them, and a hospital if he is tiring, hypoxic, or not drinking. Croup gets a steroid. Nebulized epinephrine if the stridor is at rest, then watch for rebound. Do not discharge from the epinephrine in the first minutes. Epiglottitis is a toxic child, drooling, leaning forward. Do not examine the throat in a way that upsets him. The airway is controlled, in a place that can secure it. The Hib vaccine made this rare. It did not make it impossible.

**Vignette.** A six-month-old has a runny nose, wheezing, and is drinking well. Saturation is 96 percent. Someone orders albuterol, a steroid, and azithromycin “to cover it.”
**Reasoning.** Support and discharge teaching if he keeps drinking. The diagnosis is bronchiolitis. The trap is the three-drug reflex the AAP recommends against.
**NBME trap.** They send home a croupy child five minutes after nebulized epinephrine because the stridor vanished. Rebound is why you watch.
**Hook.** Bronchiolitis drinks and goes home with nothing routine. Rest stridor gets epinephrine and a watch. Drooling and toxic: do not touch the throat.
**CCS.** Oxygen if low, hydration, no routine bronchodilator for bronchiolitis, steroid for croup, observation after epinephrine, a controlled airway for epiglottitis.

### Dehydration, asthma, and the child who is tiring

Oral rehydration if the child drinks. Intravenous fluid if not. Replace what is lost, then maintain. A child with asthma who speaks, saturates well, and turns around can go home with a steroid and a controller plan. A child who is tiring, silent, or hypoxic stays. Drowning is ventilation. The water is not the mystery. The airway is.

**Vignette.** A toddler with gastroenteritis is alert and drinking oral rehydration solution. The diaper count is coming back. A grandparent demands a bolus.
**Reasoning.** He is drinking. Oral rehydration is the treatment. The diagnosis is mild to moderate dehydration that is correcting. The trap is an intravenous line as kindness.
**NBME trap.** They send home a wheezing child who is too breathless to speak because the oxygen saturation blipped up to 93 percent for one reading. Work of breathing is the disposition.
**Hook.** Drinking: the mouth. Not drinking, or tiring: a line and a stay.
**CCS.** Oral rehydration trial, intravenous fluid if it fails, weight when you can, asthma treatment as in the lung chapter, disposition by work of breathing.

### Growth, development, and the milestone you refer

Social smile early, sitting around the middle of the first year, walking and a few words by about a year and a half. Confirm the month against the current CDC checklist. The revised checklists beat the month in an old lecture. Loss of any skill, at any age, is a referral. It is not a late bloomer. A falling percentile across visits is failure to thrive until you have looked at intake, a chronic disease, and the caregiver. One low point on a first visit is a point.

**Vignette.** He sat and babbled at nine months. At sixteen months he does not babble and no longer sits well. The parents say boys are slower.
**Reasoning.** Loss of skill is the referral. The diagnosis is developmental regression until a cause is found. The trap is “watch and wait” because the family is calm.
**NBME trap.** They diagnose failure to thrive from a single plot at the 10th percentile in a child who has always lived there and is developing. A percentile is a position. A fall is a diagnosis.
**Hook.** Lost a skill: refer. A curve that falls: look. A single point on his own curve: plot the next one.
**CCS.** Plot growth, the developmental history, referral for regression, feeding history for a falling curve, do not advance past “he will catch up” when he has lost a skill.

### Vaccines and the adolescent who needs the room

Live vaccines are not for severe immunosuppression. Routine MMR is not given before the first birthday because maternal antibody interferes. An outbreak exception exists. Read it. Do not invent the outbreak age. Pertussis vaccine in every pregnancy protects the newborn. The adolescent visit is confidential time. HEADSS-style questions. HPV as the CDC schedule says. Contraception and care for sexually transmitted infections without a parent in the room when the law allows. Know the jurisdiction. Do not guess a state’s age. You cannot promise secrecy when abuse must be reported.

**Vignette.** A 15-year-old asks for contraception and a chlamydia test and asks that her mother, who is in the waiting room, not be told. She is not in danger. There is no abuse disclosure.
**Reasoning.** In the usual exam frame, provide that confidential care. The diagnosis is a request for confidential adolescent reproductive care. The trap is calling the mother in to cosign, or promising secrecy you would have to break if she then disclosed abuse.
**NBME trap.** They give MMR at 8 months for a routine well-child visit because the family is traveling. Routine MMR waits for the first birthday. An outbreak or a travel exception is a CDC page, not a guess.
**Hook.** Confidential care when the law allows. Live vaccines and severe immunosuppression do not mix. Routine MMR after the first birthday.
**CCS.** Private interview, the vaccine that is due, the test she asked for, no parent in the room for that part, report if abuse appears.

### Injury that does not match the child

A story that cannot produce the injury, or an injury a child of that development cannot cause, is a report. Reporting is the action. You do not need to know who did it, and you do not need certainty. Examine for other injuries. A bucket-handle fracture, bruises in a pattern, or a skull injury that the story cannot explain are the classics. Do not hand the child back to the only historian and “keep an eye on it.”

**Vignette.** A four-month-old has a femur fracture. The caregiver says he rolled off a sofa. He cannot roll.
**Reasoning.** The development does not match the story. Report, treat the fracture, and look for other injuries. The diagnosis is suspected nonaccidental trauma. The trap is a social-work “chat” instead of a report, or a lecture to the caregiver as the only action.
**NBME trap.** They delay the report until they have proof. Reasonable suspicion is the threshold. Proof is an investigator’s job.
**Hook.** Cannot roll, and a femur: report. Treat the bone and report the suspicion. Both.
**CCS.** Treat the injury, skeletal survey as the child-abuse evaluation directs, report, do not discharge to the same unwitnessed plan.

### A second set of stems

**Vignette.** Intermittent severe abdominal pain every twenty minutes in a toddler. He is well between waves. A sausage-shaped mass. No currant jelly yet.
**Reasoning.** Intussusception. An air enema is often both the diagnosis and the treatment. The diagnosis is intussusception. The trap is waiting for currant jelly, which is late, or a laxative for “constipation.”
**Hook.** Waves of pain and a well interval: air contrast, not a stool softener.

**Vignette.** A two-year-old suddenly coughs and then wheezes on one side. He was eating peanuts.
**Reasoning.** Foreign body. A bronchoscopy, not an albuterol trial as the whole plan. The diagnosis is suspected airway foreign body.
**Hook.** Sudden, one-sided, a peanut: look in the airway.

**Vignette.** Five days of fever, red eyes, a rash, red lips, and swollen hands. He is four.
**Reasoning.** Kawasaki disease. Intravenous immunoglobulin and aspirin. The diagnosis is Kawasaki disease. Coronary arteries are why this is not “a virus on day five.” Confirm the current aspirin dose rather than inventing one. The trap is antibiotics for a presumed scarlet fever and no immunoglobulin.
**Hook.** Five days of fever plus the mucocutaneous signs: immunoglobulin, not a longer observation.

**Vignette.** A child after a viral illness has petechiae. Hemoglobin and white count are normal. Platelets are 12,000. He is running around the room.
**Reasoning.** Immune thrombocytopenia. Many children with only skin findings are observed. The diagnosis is immune thrombocytopenia. The trap is a platelet transfusion for petechiae, or a leukemia treatment for a single-line drop. Still look at the smear. More than one line down is a different disease.
**Hook.** One line, a well child, skin only: often observe. Sick, or more than one line: do not.

**Vignette.** Periorbital edema in the morning, heavy proteinuria, low albumin. Blood pressure is normal. The urine is not tea-colored.
**Reasoning.** Nephrotic syndrome. The diagnosis is nephrotic syndrome, often minimal change disease in a young child. The trap is treating it as acute glomerulonephritis. Tea-colored urine, hypertension, and a little blood are the nephritic picture.
**Hook.** Low albumin and protein, a well pressure: nephrotic. Tea urine and hypertension: nephritic.

**Vignette.** A limp and a fever and a refusal to bear weight. The hip is held still.
**Reasoning.** Septic arthritis until a tap says otherwise. The diagnosis is suspected septic hip. The trap is a toddler fracture pathway and ibuprofen. Kocher-type features support the concern. Do not invent a score cutoff. The tap and the orthopedist are the action.
**Hook.** Fever and a hip that will not move: tap. A painless limp without fever is a different afternoon.

**Vignette.** A newborn has not passed meconium and the exam shows no patent anus, or a meconium plug story with cystic fibrosis in the family.
**Reasoning.** This is an obstruction until surgery or the specific diagnosis is made. Hirschsprung and cystic fibrosis are on the list when the anus is patent and the meconium never came. The diagnosis is distal intestinal obstruction of the newborn. The trap is “babies take a while” past the first day or two without an exam of the anus.
**Hook.** No meconium and no anus you can see: surgery. No meconium and a patent anus: still not a watchful week.

**Vignette.** Anaphylaxis after a peanut. Hives and wheezing. Someone reaches for diphenhydramine first.
**Reasoning.** Intramuscular epinephrine. The antihistamine is not the rescue. The diagnosis is anaphylaxis.
**Hook.** Wheezing and hives: epinephrine in the thigh. Diphenhydramine is not the first syringe.

## Another pass on the stems

### A febrile seizure is not meningitis until you have looked

A short generalized seizure with a fever, in a child who returns to baseline, between about 6 months and 5 years, is often a simple febrile seizure. The work is the source of the fever and a history that the seizure was short and generalized. A seizure that is focal, lasts a long time, or recurs in the same illness is complex, and the evaluation is larger. A child who does not return to baseline, has a stiff neck, or looks toxic is meningitis until the evaluation says otherwise. Do not call every fever-plus-shaking a febrile seizure and skip the exam. Confirm the age band against the current AAP statement rather than tattooing it.

**Vignette.** A 2-year-old had one minute of whole-body shaking during a fever. He is playing. The neck is supple. The ears are red.
**Reasoning.** Treat the otitis if that is the source. Counsel the family. The diagnosis is a simple febrile seizure with a source. The trap is a lumbar puncture because a fever and a seizure were in the same sentence, or a missed stiff neck because you were reassured by the word simple.
**NBME trap.** They send home a child who is still postictal and lethargic as “post-ictal from a febrile seizure.” Baseline means baseline.
**Hook.** Back to baseline, short, generalized, a source: counsel. Not back to baseline, or a stiff neck: this is not the simple row.
**CCS.** Glucose, the fever source, observation until he is himself, no routine imaging for a simple febrile seizure. Do not end the case on a lethargic child.

### Diabetic ketoacidosis in a new child is fluids, insulin, and potassium you watch

A thin child, vomiting, deep breathing, and a glucose that is high. The first orders are fluid, a careful insulin plan, and a potassium you measure before and during the insulin, because insulin drives potassium in and the total body potassium is already low. Do not bolus insulin. Cerebral edema is the complication: headache, a falling heart rate, a rising pressure, or a change in mentation. That is mannitol or hypertonic saline and a reduced fluid rate, and a pediatric intensive-care conversation. Confirm the current protocol. This page does not list a dose or a milliliter-per-kilogram rule.

**Vignette.** A 7-year-old with two weeks of thirst and a day of vomiting. Glucose is 480. He is breathing deeply. Potassium is 5.2 before treatment. Someone wants a large intravenous insulin bolus.
**Reasoning.** Fluid and a controlled insulin infusion. The potassium will fall. Recheck it. The diagnosis is diabetic ketoacidosis, often new type 1 diabetes. The trap is the bolus, or forgetting potassium because the first value was normal-high.
**NBME trap.** They give bicarbonate because the pH is low. Bicarbonate is not routine. It is a special case you confirm, not the first order.
**Hook.** High glucose, deep breathing, vomiting: fluids and insulin you control. Watch the potassium and the mental status.

### A limp with a fever is a septic hip until a tap says otherwise

Refusal to bear weight, fever, and a hip held still. The tap and orthopedics are the action. Do not send him home on ibuprofen because the film is normal. Films are late for osteomyelitis and useless for an early septic joint. A painless limp without fever in an older child can be a different afternoon, including a slipped capital femoral epiphysis in an adolescent, which is a surgical hip, not a sprain. Knee pain can be the hip. Examine the hip when the knee film is normal.

**Vignette.** An 8-year-old will not walk. Fever of 39. The hip is held flexed and still. The plain film is normal.
**Reasoning.** Tap the hip. The diagnosis is a septic hip until the fluid says otherwise. The trap is ibuprofen and a next-day clinic because the radiograph was clean.
**NBME trap.** They ultrasound a classic slipped capital femoral epiphysis in a 13-year-old with a limp and no fever, then send him to physical therapy. That hip is non-weight-bearing and a surgical referral the same day. Knee films that are normal do not clear it.
**Hook.** Fever and a hip that will not move: tap. An adolescent limp: examine the hip, and do not walk him on a slip.

### The iron-deficient toddler and the lead question

A toddler on a milk-heavy diet with a low hemoglobin and a low mean cell volume is iron deficiency until proven otherwise. Limit the milk, add iron, and confirm the response. A lead level belongs in the workup when the history or the local guideline says so. Do not invent a capillary cutoff. Open the current CDC or AAP chart. Pica and an old house are the clues. Iron and lead can coexist. Treating iron does not replace a lead level the guideline wants.

**Vignette.** A 2-year-old drinks a quart of milk a day. Hemoglobin 9. Mean cell volume is low. He lives in a house built in 1920 and mouths the windowsills.
**Reasoning.** Iron, less milk, and a lead level. The diagnosis is iron deficiency, with lead exposure that has to be measured. The trap is iron alone and a speech about vegetables.
**NBME trap.** They diagnose thalassemia trait from one low mean cell volume and never ask about milk or the house. Trait is a later thought, after iron and the smear.
**Hook.** Too much milk: iron. An old house and mouthing: a lead level on the current chart.

### Asthma at home and asthma that stays

A child who speaks, saturates well, and turns around after a bronchodilator and a steroid can go home with a controller plan if he is using a reliever every day. A child who is tiring, hypoxic, or too breathless to speak stays. Work of breathing outranks one saturation number. Teach the inhaler with a spacer. A parent who cannot say the plan back does not have the plan yet.

**Vignette.** A 6-year-old uses albuterol every day. Today he speaks in sentences and the oxygen saturation is 96 percent after two treatments. The peak flow has climbed. He has a ride home.
**Reasoning.** Discharge with a steroid burst if you gave the attack one, and an inhaled corticosteroid. The diagnosis is an asthma exacerbation that has turned, on top of untreated persistent asthma. The trap is another albuterol canister.
**NBME trap.** They discharge a child who is still retracting because one saturation read 94 percent. Look at the work.
**Hook.** Sentences and a rising peak flow: home, with a controller. Retracting or tiring: he stays.
**CCS.** The treatment, reassessment, the controller, teach-back, return precautions. No routine antibiotic.

## Common traps

- Adult fluid and drug math.
- Aspirin for a viral fever in a child.
- Reassurance for a falling growth curve because today’s weight is “on the chart.”
- HPV vaccine skipped because the adolescent is “not sexually active.” The point is to give it before exposure. Follow the schedule.
