# Pulmonary disease and acute care

> **Study aid only.** Disposition is the Step 3 skill. GOLD, GINA, and IDSA/ATS own the details. Confirm them when a number will touch a patient.

## What this chapter covers

Where the person with lung disease sleeps tonight, and what you start before the bed is assigned. Respiratory content is about 8–10% of Step 3 MCQs. CCS cases of shortness of breath are common enough to practice on purpose.

## The framework

Office dyspnea in a known patient is an exacerbation of their disease, a new disease, or a cardiac impersonator. Emergency dyspnea is the ABCs and a saturation. Do not send an unstable person home with an inhaler sample.

| Situation | Disposition and the drug principle |
| --- | --- |
| Asthma, not on an inhaled steroid | Start one. SABA-only treatment is not GINA care. |
| Asthma exacerbation, talks in sentences, saturation acceptable, peak flow mildly reduced | Steroid, bronchodilator, observe, home with a plan if they turn around. |
| Asthma, silent chest, tiring, normal or high CO2 | This is a ventilatory emergency. A normal CO2 in a severe attack is not reassuring. |
| COPD, more sputum and dyspnea, looks well | Bronchodilators, a steroid burst, an antibiotic when GOLD’s exacerbation features are met, and follow-up. Oxygen to the saturation GOLD and the oxygen guideline actually want, not “as much as possible.” |
| COPD, acidemic and tiring | Noninvasive ventilation if no contraindication, in a monitored bed. |
| Community pneumonia, low severity, reliable follow-up | Home, with the IDSA/ATS oral regimen for that severity. |
| Pneumonia, hypoxic, hypotensive, or confused | Hospital. ICU if the severity tool or the shock says so. |
| Possible PE, stable, low probability | PERC or D-dimer as in the Step 2 pulmonary chapter. |
| Possible PE, hypotensive | This is a resuscitation and a reperfusion decision, not a clinic note. |
| Chronic cough, normal chest film, ACE inhibitor | Stop the drug. The cough can take weeks to leave. Do not add a cough suppressant and continue the drug. |
| Solitary lung nodule | Compare with old films before you biopsy. The Fleischner-style follow-up depends on size and risk. Open the current table. Do not invent an interval. |

Smoking cessation is the intervention with the largest effect in COPD. Offer medication. A pamphlet is not the whole answer.

Oxygen for chronic hypoxemia has criteria. Breathlessness with a normal saturation is not one of them. Confirm the saturation and the blood gas rules before you sign a home-oxygen order.

## High-yield atlas

Step 3 pulmonary disease is a bed and a list for tomorrow. Step 2 taught the diagnosis. This page asks where the person sleeps tonight, which order is part of stabilizing rather than part of the write-up, and what still has to be true in the morning if they went home or if they stayed. The computer-based case uses one sequence: stabilize, a focused exam, the test that changes management, treatment, monitoring, counseling, and disposition. A treatment that is dangerous to delay is stabilizing. You do not hold a nebulizer, oxygen to a target, a systemic steroid, or noninvasive ventilation while you finish a family history.

![Where the person sleeps tonight](visuals/disposition-ladder.svg)

The ladder is the question. Office, emergency department, ward, monitored bed, intensive care. A person who speaks in sentences, keeps a safe saturation on the right amount of oxygen, and can drink may leave when the follow-up is real. A person who is tiring, silent in the chest, confused, or hypotensive does not leave because a score looked kind. Smoking-cessation medicine, the vaccines this lung disease earns, and return precautions are orders. A pamphlet is not the counseling.

Read every dyspnea stem twice. Once for the disease you already know (asthma, chronic obstruction, a clot, a pneumonia, a effusion, a pneumothorax, the heart impersonating the lung). Once for the bed. The same disease has two dispositions. Mild airway narrowing that turns around after treatment goes home with a controller. The same airway, silent and tired, is an intensive-care problem. A confirmed clot in a walking person is anticoagulation and a reliable plan. The same clot with a blood pressure that will not hold is resuscitation and a reperfusion decision.

### Asthma that is not an attack today

Airway inflammation is the disease. The reliever treats the hour. GINA does not accept a short-acting beta agonist as the only drug a person with asthma lives on. The preferred paths put an inhaled corticosteroid in the regimen, either as the reliever partner or as daily maintenance, including maintenance-and-reliever therapy with an inhaled corticosteroid plus formoterol where that is the track you are using. You do not need to recite both tracks from memory on a case. You need to refuse the plan that is a reliever alone.

Ask about night waking, how many days a week the reliever fires, whether school or work is being missed, and whether an oral steroid was needed since the last visit. Those answers are the control assessment. Spirometry when the diagnosis is still soft, or when the course is not what you expected, separates asthma from obstruction that will not reverse, from a vocal-cord problem, and from a heart that is wheezing because the pressure in the left atrium is high. A peak flow is for the action plan and for the attack. It is a weak way to make the diagnosis the first time.

**Vignette.** A 24-year-old uses albuterol every day and wakes twice a week. She plays on a recreational team and has started sitting out the second half. No controller is on the list. She speaks in full sentences today. Saturation is comfortable on room air.
**Reasoning.** Daily reliever use and night waking are uncontrolled asthma. The missed second half is impairment, not a reason for a new brand of reliever. GINA wants an inhaled corticosteroid in the regimen and a written plan. The diagnosis is uncontrolled asthma on reliever-only treatment. She sleeps at home. Tomorrow she is on a controller, she knows what to do if peak flow falls, and she has a follow-up that is soon enough to see whether nights improved.
**NBME trap.** Another albuterol inhaler as the whole plan, or a standing oral steroid for symptoms that are chronic rather than an attack.
**Hook.** No short-acting beta agonist as sole maintenance.
**CCS.** Stabilize: she is already stable; do not invent oxygen. Focused exam: speech, saturation, chest, peak flow. Tests that change management: spirometry if the diagnosis was never confirmed; a film only if the story is not pure asthma. Treatment: start the inhaled corticosteroid regimen and teach inhaler technique before she leaves. Monitoring: she does not need a monitor. Counseling: trigger avoidance that is real (a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug that has caused wheeze, a workplace), the action plan, and when to return. Disposition: home.

What continues tomorrow is the controller, not a leftover oral steroid. An oral corticosteroid is for an exacerbation. It is not maintenance. If she is pregnant, asthma treatment continues. Uncontrolled asthma is worse for the pregnancy than the inhaled drugs GINA uses. Confirm the current pregnancy note in the strategy report before you stop a controller out of fear.

### A severe asthma attack

The person who is well enough to go home after an attack speaks in sentences, moves air you can hear improving, and has a peak flow that rose and stayed up through an observation period. The person who is not well enough speaks in words, sits forward, uses accessory muscles, and then gets quiet. Quiet is not better. A silent chest means air is barely moving. Fatigue, a falling heart rate after tachycardia, confusion, and a normal or rising arterial carbon dioxide are the same message: the usual hypocapnia of a severe attack has been lost because the pump is failing.

GINA’s emergency path is oxygen, a repeated short-acting beta agonist, ipratropium, and a systemic corticosteroid. Intravenous magnesium is for the severe attack, not for every wheeze that responded to the first nebulizer. Epinephrine intramuscularly is for anaphylaxis that is wearing an asthma costume: urticaria, a new food or drug, and shock. A beta blocker on the medication list can make the wheeze harder to treat and can make epinephrine more likely to be the drug you needed. Heliox and other adjuncts are not the first orders.

An arterial blood gas is the test that changes management when you think they are tiring. You do not need one in a person who is speaking and improving. A chest film is the test that changes management when the breath sounds are asymmetric, when subcutaneous air is present, or when the attack will not turn: pneumothorax and pneumonia both impersonate a stubborn attack. A routine film in a straightforward improving attack does not earn its radiation. Pregnancy testing comes before that film when pregnancy is possible.

**Vignette.** She can say one word at a time. The chest is quiet. The arterial carbon dioxide is 40 mm Hg. She was acidotic on paper last year during a milder attack and someone told her “40 is normal.”
**Reasoning.** A severe attack should blow carbon dioxide down. Forty in this chest is a failing pump. The old reassurance was about a different day. The diagnosis is life-threatening asthma. She does not go to the pharmacy. She goes to a monitored bed, with the GINA drugs running, and with someone who can intubate if the mask and the nebulizer lose.
**NBME trap.** Discharge because the carbon dioxide “is normal,” or a sedative for the anxiety of air hunger.
**Hook.** Normal carbon dioxide in bad asthma is bad.
**CCS.** Stabilize: oxygen to the GINA adult target you confirm (commonly 93 to 95 percent), sit her up, continuous pulse oximetry. Focused exam: speech, accessory muscles, symmetry, mental status. Tests that change management: a gas if she is tiring; a film if air entry is unequal. Treatment: repeated short-acting beta agonist, ipratropium, systemic corticosteroid now, magnesium if she is severe. Monitoring: a monitored bed, reassess after each round of bronchodilator, not at the end of the shift. Counseling waits until she can talk. Disposition: she stays. If she later speaks in sentences and the peak flow holds, the disposition can change. It does not change in the first ten minutes of a silent chest.

Intubation in asthma is a high-risk procedure because the lungs are overinflated. It is still the right procedure when consciousness is falling or the gas is worsening despite treatment. After the tube, the trap is a respiratory rate and a tidal volume that stack breaths. Permissive hypercapnia is the ventilator idea. You confirm the settings with the current emergency-airway practice. You do not “normalize” the carbon dioxide in the first hour by hyperventilating a trapped chest.

**Pair.** Anaphylaxis gets intramuscular epinephrine and still gets the bronchodilator. Asthma without urticaria or shock does not get epinephrine as the opening drug. Both can wheeze. The skin and the blood pressure separate them.

What continues tomorrow, if she earned a discharge after observation, is a steroid burst she can swallow, a controller she did not have, inhaler teaching, a peak-flow plan, and a follow-up measured in days. Return precautions are written in ordinary words: words that will not come out, lips that change color, a reliever that stops working. If she did not turn, tomorrow is still the monitored bed, and the question is whether the attack is actually pneumonia, a pneumothorax, vocal-cord dysfunction, or a heart that was never bronchospasm.

### COPD on a day that is not an exacerbation

Destroyed elastic recoil and a chronically inflamed airway. The cigarette is the exposure that changes the slope. Long-acting bronchodilators treat symptoms and reduce exacerbations. An inhaled corticosteroid is added when exacerbations are the story and the blood eosinophil count in the current GOLD table says the person is likely to benefit. A person who gets repeated pneumonia may be harmed by that steroid. Do not put an inhaled corticosteroid on every former smoker with dyspnea, and do not withhold it from the person who is in the hospital twice a winter if the eosinophil guidance supports it. Open the count. It has been expressed as ranges, and a memorized cutoff from an undated card is how you get the year wrong.

GOLD revises the letters it uses for groups. Learn symptoms plus exacerbations so a renamed group does not break the habit. FEV1 still describes severity and still matters for prognosis and for some drug labels. It is not the prescription by itself. A person with a low FEV1 who is walking a mile and never exacerbates is not the same prescription problem as a person with a moderate FEV1 who was admitted twice.

Smoking cessation is the intervention with the largest effect. Offer varenicline or combination nicotine replacement, or bupropion when the seizure and psychiatric history allow it. Combination means a long-acting nicotine product plus a short-acting one, not a pamphlet plus a lozenge you hope they buy. Behavioral support is added to the drug. It does not replace the drug. Pregnancy changes the medication. Confirm the agent. Do not leave the sentence at “I advised cessation” on a computer-based case. The order is the medicine and the follow-up.

Vaccines are part of the same visit. Influenza, COVID-19 as the current season directs, pneumococcal vaccine on the adult schedule, and RSV when the person’s age and conditions match the current CDC line. A pertussis-containing booster if it is due. You open the schedule. You do not guess the product from a card printed before the conjugate vaccines changed.

Pulmonary rehabilitation after an exacerbation is a GOLD recommendation with a real effect on the next months. Offer it. It is not a substitute for the inhaler, and it is not the disposition for the person who is acidemic today.

**Vignette.** He stops walking at one block. He was treated for two exacerbations last year. He is on albuterol only and still smokes a pack a day. Saturation at rest today is 96 percent. He asks for home oxygen because his brother has it.
**Reasoning.** Symptoms plus exacerbations pick a long-acting bronchodilator, and the exacerbation history puts an inhaled corticosteroid into the conversation once you have looked at the eosinophil guidance. Saturation of 96 percent is not chronic hypoxemia. Home oxygen is for chronic hypoxemia that meets criteria, measured when he is stable, not for breathlessness and not for a family member’s concentrator. The diagnosis is undertreated COPD. He also gets a cessation drug. Tomorrow he is on a long-acting bronchodilator, a quit medicine, and a rehabilitation referral, not on oxygen.
**NBME trap.** An oxygen prescription for a normal saturation, or an FEV1 percent used as the only reason to choose the inhaler.
**Hook.** Symptoms and exacerbations. The cigarette is the disease-modifying exposure.
**CCS.** Stabilize: none; he is at baseline. Focused exam: chest, edema, saturation at rest and after a walk if you are deciding about oxygen. Tests that change management: spirometry if never done; a film if this dyspnea is new in character; eosinophils if you are deciding on an inhaled corticosteroid; an arterial gas only if you are qualifying oxygen and the saturation is in the range the criteria care about. Treatment: long-acting bronchodilator, cessation medicine, vaccines that are due. Monitoring: clinic follow-up. Counseling: no smoking, inhaler technique, what an exacerbation feels like and when to start the action plan. Disposition: home.

**Pair.** Cardiac dyspnea from a weak left ventricle can look like his one-block limit. A gallop, a displaced apex, orthopnea, and a raised venous pressure are the heart. COPD does not forbid heart failure. Both can be present. The test that changes management is the one that finds the second disease, not a third inhaler.

### The COPD exacerbation and the mask

An exacerbation is an acute worsening of dyspnea, cough, or sputum beyond the usual day. The common triggers are a virus, a bacterial bronchitis, and a clot or a heart that you will miss if you call every worsening “the COPD.” Look at the legs, the rhythm, and the film. A pneumothorax in a person with bullae is an exacerbation until the film, and then it is a tube.

Bronchodilators, a short course of systemic corticosteroid, and oxygen to a target. GOLD’s antibiotic indication is the one to memorize as features, not as a vibe: sputum purulence plus increased dyspnea or increased sputum volume. A need for ventilatory support is its own reason. A mild increase in clear sputum in a person who is walking does not automatically earn a fluoroquinolone. Open the current GOLD paragraph before you expand the antibiotic to every cough.

The oxygen target in a person at risk of hypercapnic failure is the saturation GOLD and the British Thoracic Society oxygen guideline converge on, often taught as about 88 to 92 percent. Confirm before you tattoo 88. The harm of unrestricted oxygen is a rising carbon dioxide and sleepiness, then an arrest you caused with a nasal cannula you thought was kind. Recheck the gas after you change the oxygen. A target is a range you hit, not a minimum you exceed for comfort.

Noninvasive ventilation is for respiratory acidosis when the person is still protecting the airway and can cooperate with the mask. Contraindications are practical: apnea, vomiting, copious secretions, facial trauma or recent upper-airway surgery, an inability to cooperate, and a pneumothorax that is not drained. If the pH is extreme, the mental status is falling, or a real trial of the mask fails, intubate. Open the current pH window rather than memorizing a single threshold from a lecture. The bed for a mask is a monitored bed. A ward that cannot see the person is not noninvasive ventilation. It is a hope.

**Vignette.** Known COPD. He is sleepy but rousable, still swallowing, not vomiting. The pH is 7.28 and the carbon dioxide is 70 mm Hg. Saturation is 98 percent on a non-rebreather mask that a well-meaning person applied in the ambulance. The film has no pneumothorax and no new lobar consolidation.
**Reasoning.** This is respiratory acidosis with an airway he still owns. The non-rebreather is the wrong dose of oxygen. Turn the oxygen down toward the retainer target, give bronchodilators and a systemic corticosteroid, and start noninvasive ventilation in a monitored bed. An antibiotic is reasonable because he needs ventilatory support. The diagnosis is a severe COPD exacerbation. Immediate intubation of a person who can still try a mask is the other trap, equal and opposite to leaving him on the non-rebreather for a nap.
**NBME trap.** Unlimited oxygen for comfort, or a blood gas you never repeat after you change the oxygen.
**Hook.** Acid and sleepy, airway protected: the mask, in a monitored bed.
**CCS.** Stabilize: controlled oxygen, sit him up, monitors, access. Focused exam: mental status, airway protection, chest symmetry, edema, calves. Tests that change management: the arterial gas, a chest film, an electrocardiogram because the heart is the impersonator. Treatment: nebulizers, systemic corticosteroid, noninvasive ventilation, antibiotic for ventilatory support. Monitoring: repeat the gas, watch the pH and the mentation, stop the mask trial if he vomits or tires further and intubate. Counseling is for later: cessation medicine before discharge, vaccines, an action plan. Disposition: monitored bed tonight. Tomorrow you ask whether the mask can come off, whether the oxygen can fall, and whether he can swallow the steroid. You do not discharge him from the emergency department on the non-rebreather.

A person who is walking, talking, purulent, and not acidemic can complete the same drugs and go home if someone can see them and they can use an inhaler. Give return precautions in plain language: sleepiness, lips changing color, inhalers that stop working, chest pain. Schedule the follow-up in days. Send the cessation prescription with them. The exacerbation is the moment they will hear it.

What you do not continue tomorrow is a month of prednisone, a standing fluoroquinolone between flares, and theophylline added because the list looked short. Maintenance is the long-acting bronchodilator. A phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitor exists for a selected chronic-bronchitis phenotype with frequent exacerbations. It is not an acute bronchodilator. Open the GOLD line. Azithromycin prevention exists for selected former smokers who still exacerbate on proper inhalers. It costs hearing, QT interval, and resistance. It is not the first prescription on the day you met him.

### Two oxygen sentences that are not the same sentence

People tattoo one number and use it for every lung. There are two decisions.

The acute decision, during a flare, in a person who may be a chronic retainer: target the saturation GOLD and the oxygen guideline want, often taught as about 88 to 92 percent. Confirm before you tattoo 88. Recheck the gas. A person with asthma is not that person. GINA’s adult target in an attack is higher, commonly 93 to 95 percent. Confirm. A young person with a pneumothorax or a clot and previously normal lungs gets enough oxygen to correct hypoxemia. You do not restrict them to a retainer target they do not need.

The chronic decision: home oxygen for chronic hypoxemia that meets long-term criteria, confirmed when the person is stable and on optimal treatment, not during the flare and not because they are breathless with a normal saturation. Do not invent the arterial oxygen tension cutoff. Confirm the long-term oxygen criteria, including the situations in which cor pulmonale or erythrocytosis change the line. Do not sign the home order from the emergency department. If they still meet criteria weeks later, at a stable visit, that is the prescription. It is for hours of use that are long enough to matter. A few minutes of comfort are not the therapy the trials tested.

**Vignette.** Three weeks after a flare he feels back to baseline. Resting saturation is low enough that you are in the range where long-term oxygen is even a conversation, and a gas confirms hypoxemia while he is stable and on his inhalers. He is not sleepy. He does not have a new infiltrate.
**Reasoning.** This is the chronic decision, made while stable. The diagnosis is chronic hypoxemia that may qualify. You confirm the current criteria and you prescribe if he meets them. You also ask about the cigarette, because oxygen and a lit cigarette are a fire, and the cessation drug is still the higher-yield order.
**NBME trap.** Signing home oxygen on the night of the exacerbation because the saturation dipped, in a person whose baseline saturation is normal.
**Hook.** Acute target and chronic qualification are two sentences. Confirm both.

### Pneumonia and the bed

Community-acquired pneumonia is a new infiltrate plus a clinical illness. Older adults may not mount a fever. Hypothermia counts. The white count can be low and that is worse, not reassuring. The organism list (pneumococcus, atypicals, Haemophilus, a virus, less often staphylococcus after influenza) matters for the drug. The bed matters more for the first hour of a Step 3 case.

IDSA/ATS separates site of care from the antibiotic menu, and then lets the site pick the menu. Low severity and reliable follow-up, able to drink, oxygenating, thinking clearly, pressure holding: an oral regimen and home. Hypoxia, hypotension, or confusion: hospital. Intensive care if shock or respiratory failure is present. Those are the major criteria. The guideline also lists minor criteria. Open the current table rather than memorizing how many minor criteria tip the bed. A person who meets a major criterion does not need a minor-criteria argument.

CURB-65’s elements — confusion, urea, respiratory rate, blood pressure, age 65 — are a memory aid you can use on paper. The score supports disposition. It does not send home a person who cannot keep water down, lives alone and is already confused, or looks much sicker than the points. Do not treat a point total as law.

The oral and intravenous menus have a logic you should know and a dose you should confirm. A healthy adult who is going home is not the same as an adult with chronic heart, lung, liver, or kidney disease, diabetes, alcohol use disorder, or a recent antibiotic. The healthy outpatient starts with a beta-lactam such as amoxicillin, or with doxycycline, or with a macrolide only where local pneumococcal resistance is low enough that the guideline still allows macrolide monotherapy. Confirm that local fact. Do not invent it. The outpatient with comorbidity gets a broader oral combination or a respiratory fluoroquinolone. The ward gets a beta-lactam plus a macrolide, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone. The intensive care gets a beta-lactam plus either a macrolide or a fluoroquinolone. You add coverage for MRSA or for Pseudomonas only when the person has a prior respiratory culture of that organism or another risk the current IDSA/ATS paragraph names, such as recent hospitalization with intravenous antibiotics. Open the look-back window. Routine anaerobic coverage for suspected aspiration is not the default.

Draw blood cultures when the disease is severe, if the draw does not delay the first antibiotic. A sputum culture is useful when you can get a real specimen and the disease is severe or the person is at risk for a resistant organism. A procalcitonin does not decide whether to start antibiotics when the clinical diagnosis is pneumonia. Duration is guided by stability. The IDSA/ATS file uses a minimum measured in days, about five, once the person is afebrile and stable. Confirm. Do not reflex a long course, and do not stop on the morning the fever breaks if that morning is day two.

**Vignette.** A 74-year-old has a new lobar infiltrate, a fever, and a saturation of 94 percent on room air. He is eating, joking, and his daughter lives with him. Blood pressure is 128/76. He is not confused. Respiratory rate is 18.
**Reasoning.** This is low-severity disease with reliable follow-up and no hypoxia that needs oxygen, no hypotension, and no confusion. The diagnosis is community-acquired pneumonia that can be treated at home on an IDSA/ATS oral regimen matched to whether he has comorbidity. The trap is admission because he is 74, or discharge with no follow-up and no return precautions. Age 65 is one CURB-65 element. It is not a hospital by itself.
**NBME trap.** Vancomycin plus piperacillin for a stable lobar pneumonia with no MRSA risk, or a macrolide-only plan in a place where you have no idea what the resistance is.
**Hook.** Low severity and a real follow-up can go home. Hypoxia, hypotension, or confusion stays.
**CCS.** Stabilize: oxygen only if hypoxic; he is not. Focused exam: mental status, work of breathing, pressure, a listen for a complicated effusion. Tests that change management: the film you already have; labs that feed the severity picture; cultures if you are about to admit him for severe disease. Treatment: the oral regimen, and a first dose now if you are not sure he will fill the prescription tonight. Monitoring: a phone or a visit soon. Counseling: finish the antibiotic, return for a worse breath, confusion, or a pressure that feels faint, and take the pneumococcal and influenza vaccines when this acute illness allows if he is behind. Disposition: home.

**Vignette.** The next person is 68, respiratory rate 34, blood pressure 86/50, confused, with the same lobar film. He is not yet on a ventilator.
**Reasoning.** Hypotension and confusion are already a hospital. Shock is an IDSA/ATS major criterion for intensive care even before the ventilator arrives. The diagnosis is severe community-acquired pneumonia. Cultures if they do not delay the drug, an intravenous regimen appropriate to severe disease, fluid if he is hypoperfused, and a vasopressor if fluid is not enough. That last sequence is sepsis care living inside a pneumonia case. The lung guideline and the sepsis guidance are both true.
**NBME trap.** An oral antibiotic and a clinic appointment because the white count “is not back yet.”
**Hook.** Shock or respiratory failure is the intensive care unit.

A parapneumonic effusion changes the case. If the fluid is free-flowing and small and the person is improving, you may watch it. If the person is not improving, if the fluid is loculated, or if you tap and find pus, a low glucose, or a low pH, the fluid needs a tube. Open the current pleural-fluid pH line rather than guessing it. Antibiotics without drainage do not treat an empyema. Empyema is source control. It is the same idea as an abscess anywhere else.

Influenza that worsens after a brief improvement, especially with a cavity or a pleural process, is staphylococcal superinfection until proven otherwise. A pure viral illness in a low-risk adult does not get an antibiotic. A hypoxic pregnant person or an older adult with influenza is a treatment and disposition problem on the CDC antiviral file, and often a hospital.

### Pulmonary embolism, stable and not stable

A clot is two diseases. One is a stable diagnostic problem. The other is obstructive shock.

Stable and low probability: use PERC or a D-dimer the way Step 2 taught. PERC is a set of negative findings. It applies only when your probability was already low. A single positive element, or a gestalt that was never low, means you do not clear the person with PERC. D-dimer is for the unlikely person, because a positive dimer in a high-probability person was never going to send them home and a negative dimer is not how you exclude a high-probability clot. High probability goes to a CT pulmonary angiogram, or to a ventilation-perfusion scan when contrast is a bad idea and the chest film is readable. A pregnant person often starts with leg ultrasound if the legs are swollen, because a positive study lets you treat without a lung dose of radiation. Do not invent a casual CT as the kind thing.

Hypotensive, or suddenly collapsed, with clear lungs and a right-heart story: resuscitation and a reperfusion decision. Fluids are cautious. A huge fluid bolus can worsen a pressure-overloaded right ventricle. Norepinephrine is the pressor if you need one. Thrombolysis if nothing contraindicates it. A catheter-based or surgical embolectomy if lysis is contraindicated or fails. Do not send this person home. Do not send them to the scanner without a monitor and a person who can run a code. A confirmed clot on bedside ultrasound of a dilated, failing right heart, in a person you are about to lose, can be enough to treat. You do not need a beautiful CT report to give a lytic to a dying person when the story is this loud.

A confirmed stable clot gets anticoagulation. A direct oral anticoagulant for most people. Low-molecular-weight heparin if pregnant, and often the start of the conversation in active cancer, confirming the current cancer-associated plan. Warfarin is still the drug for a mechanical valve and for antiphospholipid syndrome in many practices. The duration is a finite course for a clearly provoked clot and a longer conversation for an unprovoked clot or a clot with cancer. Open the current CHEST duration. Do not invent the month.

An inferior vena cava filter is for a person who cannot be anticoagulated because they are bleeding, or for the uncommon recurrence on adequate anticoagulation. It is not the first idea if they can take a drug. Filters thrombose, migrate, and get forgotten. If you place one, the plan includes retrieving it when anticoagulation becomes possible.

Right-heart strain, a positive troponin, or both, with a normal blood pressure, is intermediate risk. Those people are anticoagulated and watched in a monitored bed. Routine thrombolysis is not their plan. Rescue reperfusion is for the one who deteriorates. Sending them home from the scanner because “the pressure is normal right now” is how the deterioration happens in a car.

**Vignette.** Pleuritic pain, heart rate 110, saturation 91 percent on room air, a swollen left leg, blood pressure 118/74. He had pelvic surgery three weeks ago.
**Reasoning.** Probability is not low. PERC was never available to you. A D-dimer will be positive and will not help. The test that changes management is a CT pulmonary angiogram. If it confirms a clot, he is anticoagulated. Saturation of 91 percent and a recent operation mean he is not a casual discharge even after the first dose. The diagnosis is pulmonary embolism until the scan says otherwise. He sleeps in a hospital bed tonight.
**NBME trap.** A D-dimer in a high-probability person, or a filter because surgery was recent even though he can take anticoagulation now.
**Hook.** Low probability earns PERC or a dimer. This story earns the scan.
**CCS.** Stabilize: oxygen for the hypoxemia, monitors. Focused exam: pressure, legs, lungs, a look for a wound that is bleeding. Tests that change management: CT pulmonary angiogram. Treatment: anticoagulation once confirmed, or immediately if the delay is long and the probability is high and bleeding risk is acceptable. Monitoring: saturation and pressure. Counseling: bleeding precautions, why the drug must not stop early, and a follow-up to decide the duration. Disposition: hospital tonight because he is hypoxic. A different person, confirmed, saturating on room air, reliable, and on a formal low-risk path, can go home. This one does not.

**Vignette.** Blood pressure is 78/40. He is sweaty. The neck veins are up. The lungs are clear. He was in a cast until yesterday.
**Reasoning.** This is obstructive shock until you prove otherwise. The diagnosis you act on is massive pulmonary embolism. Reperfusion, a pressor if needed, cautious fluid, a monitored intensive-care bed. The trap is a liter of crystalloid and a ride to CT with no one in attendance, or a heparin drip as the only plan while the pressure stays 78.
**NBME trap.** A D-dimer, or a discharge with “follow-up Doppler on Monday.”
**Hook.** Hypotensive is resuscitation and a reperfusion decision. Do not send a hypotensive person home.

### The nodule, the old film, and the screening scan

A solitary nodule found on a scan done for another reason is not a hospital admission and not a same-day biopsy. The first test that changes management is an old image. A nodule that has been the same size for years is not cancer you need to stick a needle in. A nodule that was not there, or that has grown, is a different object. After you have compared, open the current Fleischner table. Solid nodules and subsolid nodules are different rows. Risk (smoking, age, the look of the edge, the lobe) changes the interval. Do not invent a millimeter cutoff. Do not invent a three-month or a twelve-month rule from memory. A clearly suspicious large nodule in a heavy smoker is PET or tissue, not a long wait. Fleischner does not govern people with known cancer, people who are immunocompromised, or low-dose screening scans.

Screening is a different program. USPSTF recommends annual low-dose CT for adults 50 to 80 years old with at least a 20 pack-year history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years. A chest radiograph is not the screening test. A symptom is not screening. Hemoptysis, a new cough that persists, weight loss, or a focal finding is a diagnostic workup even if the person is outside the screening frame, and even if a screening scan was recent. Do not wait for the next annual low-dose CT because the calendar says so. Lung-RADS reads the screening study. Do not apply Fleischner intervals to it.

**Vignette.** A 63-year-old former 40-pack-year smoker has a 9 mm solid nodule mentioned on a CT done for kidney stones. No old films are in the packet. He has no cough, no fever, no weight loss.
**Reasoning.** Nine millimeters is a size you will look up. It is not a size you biopsy because the number feels medium, and it is not a size you ignore. The first move is the old film. Then the current Fleischner row for a solid nodule at that size and his risk, which is not low. The diagnosis is an incidental pulmonary nodule, unresolved until the comparison. He goes home with the follow-up the table names. He does not go home with “repeat sometime.”
**NBME trap.** Immediate biopsy before comparison, or a one-year shrug, or a Fleischner interval applied to a screening examination.
**Hook.** Old films before a needle. Then the current table. No invented millimeter.

The 9 mm in that stem is a story detail so you can feel the temptation. It is not a threshold this book is teaching. The table has the thresholds. They have moved before.

**Vignette.** A 58-year-old who currently smokes, 30 pack-years, feels well, and asks for “the chest X-ray that finds cancer early.”
**Reasoning.** He is inside the USPSTF age and pack-year frame if he is between 50 and 80, which he is, and he currently smokes. The test is annual low-dose CT, not a radiograph. The diagnosis is a screening conversation, plus a cessation drug. If he had coughed blood, this would no longer be screening.
**NBME trap.** A chest radiograph as the screening test, or a low-dose CT for a 30-year-old with a five-pack-year history because a relative had cancer.
**Hook.** Screening has a frame. A symptom breaks the frame and becomes diagnosis.

### Pleural fluid and air under pressure

A new effusion is tapped unless the cause is obvious heart failure that is already responding to diuretic. Send protein and LDH in the fluid and in the serum. Light’s criteria call the fluid an exudate if the fluid-to-serum protein ratio is greater than 0.5, or the fluid-to-serum LDH ratio is greater than 0.6, or the fluid LDH is greater than two-thirds of the upper limit of normal for serum LDH. An exudate needs a cause: infection, cancer, clot, inflammation, tuberculosis. Heart failure, cirrhosis, and nephrosis are the usual transudates. Diuretic therapy can nudge a heart-failure fluid toward the exudate line. Look at the person, not only the ratio.

A parapneumonic fluid that is an exudate with a low pH, a low glucose, or pus is a drainage problem. Tuberculosis in the pleural space is a lymphocytic exudate and an adenosine-deaminase conversation, plus the public-health duties of active disease if you are treating it as such. A bloody exudate in a smoker is cancer until the cytology and the scan say otherwise. Do not start a diuretic and a clinic visit in six months for a unilateral bloody effusion.

**Vignette.** Bilateral effusions, a known weak ventricle, a weight up four pounds, and crackles. He is speaking. Saturation improves with a modest amount of oxygen.
**Reasoning.** This is transudate from heart failure until he fails to respond. The diagnosis is cardiogenic edema. Tap if he does not turn, or if the fluid is unilateral and he is febrile. Diuretic, the heart plan, a ward if he needs oxygen. Home only if he is reliable, oxygen is not a new chronic need you have not arranged, and someone can weigh him tomorrow.
**NBME trap.** A chest tube for bilateral heart-failure fluid.
**Hook.** Tap a new effusion unless obvious heart failure is already responding.

Air in the pleural space is a disposition based on stability and on reserve. Tension physiology is clinical: hypotension, distended neck veins, absent breath sounds, trachea pushed away. Decompress. Do not wait for a film. A small primary spontaneous pneumothorax in a healthy person who is saturating and comfortable can be observed, with the size cutoffs read from the current British Thoracic Society or American College of Chest Physicians statement. Those two statements have not always used the same centimeter. Do not invent one. A secondary pneumothorax, which means underlying lung disease, is a hospital. The reserve was already spent. A pneumothorax on positive-pressure ventilation is a tube, because the air will grow.

**Vignette.** After a central line, saturation falls and blood pressure falls. One side of the chest is silent. The trachea is away from the silent side.
**Reasoning.** This is tension pneumothorax. The diagnosis is clinical. The procedure is decompression and then a chest tube. The film is what you do for the next person, who is stable. The trap is a stat radiograph while the pressure falls, or a needle on the wrong side because you did not listen.
**NBME trap.** Intubation as the first move for tension physiology, which can worsen the pressure until the air is out. Support the airway if you must, but decompress.
**Hook.** Tension is a clinical diagnosis. The film is for the stable cap.

### When the ventilator is the treatment

Respiratory failure is a pump problem, a gas-exchange problem, or both. Hypercapnia with a tiring person is the pump. Hypoxemia that will not correct is the lung parenchyma, a shunt, or a clot. Noninvasive ventilation fits the pump failure of a COPD exacerbation and of cardiogenic edema when the airway is protected. High-flow nasal oxygen is a tool for hypoxemic failure. It is not a substitute for noninvasive ventilation in an acidemic retainer. Intubation is for an airway that is not protected, a trial of noninvasive support that is failing, apnea, or a trajectory you can already see (airway burn, swelling, a massive aspiration).

The acute respiratory distress syndrome is acute bilateral opacities not fully explained by left-heart failure, with hypoxemia despite positive end-expiratory pressure. The Berlin definition has severity bands. Open the current ratios rather than reciting a number you might swap. The treatment that has stayed is the one to remember. Tidal volume is about 6 mL per kilogram of predicted body weight, not actual body weight. Predicted body weight comes from height and sex. A short obese person ventilated on the scale weight gets a tidal volume that overdistends a small lung. Plateau pressure is limited to 30 cm of water or less. Prone positioning is for severe hypoxemia that is not turning. Fluid is conservative once shock has been resuscitated. Those are ARDSNet principles and the practice that followed. Confirm the current ventilator order set. Do not chase a normal carbon dioxide. Do not use a high tidal volume because the saturation looks prettier for an hour.

**Vignette.** Intubated for pneumonia and shock. On day two the film is white on both sides. The left ventricle is not the explanation on ultrasound. Tidal volume is set at 12 mL per kilogram of actual body weight. Height was never entered.
**Reasoning.** This is the acute respiratory distress syndrome until a cardiogenic story appears. The ventilator is set on the wrong weight and at a volume the trials showed was harmful. Change to predicted body weight and a volume near 6 mL per kilogram, and watch the plateau pressure against 30 cm of water. The diagnosis is ventilator-induced overdistension on top of an injured lung. The trap is diuresis alone, which may also be right later, while the tidal volume stays at 12.
**NBME trap.** Actual body weight as the ventilator input, or a tidal volume chosen to normalize the carbon dioxide.
**Hook.** Predicted body weight. About 6 mL per kilogram. Plateau pressure at or under 30 cm of water.

Sedation on a ventilator is a daily holiday, not a coma. A spontaneous breathing trial when the reason for the tube has improved is how people leave the ventilator. Continuing full support because no one asked is a disposition error inside the intensive care unit. So is extubating a person who cannot protect the airway because the gas looks fine.

### The office lung: cough, sleep, and tuberculosis

A cough of a few days with a clear exam is bronchitis. No antibiotic. No film. Colored sputum is inflammation. It is not a culture result. A cough that is violent, with post-tussive vomiting, in a person whose pertussis vaccine is remote, is the exception: test when you can, and give a macrolide because it reduces spread. It will not instantly stop a cough that is already in the paroxysmal phase. Tell them they are contagious. That is the counseling.

Chronic cough is a cough lasting more than 8 weeks. Acute is under 3 weeks. The gap is subacute. Those are American College of Chest Physicians frames, not disease definitions. The chronic-cough workup starts with a film and with the drug list. An angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor causes cough. Stop it. The cough can take weeks, sometimes a couple of months, to leave. Do not add a suppressant and continue the drug. Switch the blood-pressure medicine. After that, the common causes are upper-airway cough syndrome, asthma including cough-variant asthma, nonasthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis, and reflux. Treat the likely one and see. Do not order a bronchoscopy for a normal film and a classic postnasal story you have not treated.

**Vignette.** A dry cough began three weeks after lisinopril. The film is normal. He wants a codeine prescription and to keep the blood-pressure pill because “it is working.”
**Reasoning.** The drug is the disease. Stop the angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor. The diagnosis is drug-induced cough. Three weeks is enough time for the drug to be guilty and not enough time for you to call the cough chronic and start a long workup. Give him a different pressure medicine. Tell him the cough may linger for weeks after the stop.
**NBME trap.** A cough suppressant added to the offending drug, or an antibiotic.
**Hook.** Stop the angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor. Do not cover the cough and continue the drug.

Obstructive sleep apnea is snoring, witnessed pauses, daytime sleepiness, a thick neck, and hypertension that will not move. Atrial fibrillation likes it. The test is a sleep study. The treatment is positive airway pressure. Weight loss helps and is part of tomorrow, not the only order. A stimulant is not first. Counseling you do not skip: do not drive while sleepy. That sentence belongs in the case. Before elective surgery, anesthesia needs to know. Many cases can proceed with a plan. Proceeding in silence is the error. Acute respiratory acidosis in a very obese person is obesity hypoventilation until proven otherwise and is noninvasive ventilation tonight, not a referral to the sleep lab as the only order.

Latent tuberculosis is a positive interferon-gamma release assay or a positive skin test, a normal film, and no symptoms, after you have actually looked for symptoms. Treat it so it does not become active. Open the current CDC short-course regimen. Do not start a single drug if the film shows a cavity or the symptoms say active disease. Active disease is airborne isolation, a public-health report, and combination therapy. Never one drug. A health-care worker’s positive test is the same medicine. It is not a chest film you skip because they feel well, and it is not treatment you start before the film.

**Vignette.** Night sweats, weight loss, and an upper-lobe cavity. He has been in a shelter. Someone offers isoniazid alone because “that is the tuberculosis drug.”
**Reasoning.** This is active disease. Isolation first, then combination therapy, then the public-health department. The diagnosis is active tuberculosis. Isoniazid alone breeds resistance. He does not go home on a bus tonight while you wait for the culture, unless a public-health plan you would sign says the setting is safe. Most of these people are admitted to an airborne room at the start.
**NBME trap.** A single drug for a cavity, or a regular floor with no airborne precaution.
**Hook.** Active disease is never one drug. Latent disease is not a cavity.

### More stems, the name withheld

**Vignette.** A 19-year-old collapses at the bus stop, then is pleuritic and tachypneic. Blood pressure is 100/70. The chest film is clear. The left calf is swollen. She started a combined oral contraceptive two months ago.
**Reasoning.** Clear film, pleuritic pain, a leg, and a new estrogen. Probability is not low. The diagnosis to chase is pulmonary embolism. Scan, then anticoagulate if confirmed. She is not hypotensive, so she is not a lytic. She is also not a D-dimer. Estrogen is a clue, not a diagnosis by itself, and it comes off the list once a clot is real.
**NBME trap.** A proton-pump inhibitor for “chest pain,” or PERC applied to a swollen leg.
**Hook.** A clear film does not clear a clot.

**Vignette.** A 70-year-old with COPD is more breathless. Sputum is purulent and heavier. He speaks in sentences. pH is 7.41. Carbon dioxide is 46 mm Hg, near his baseline. Saturation is 90 percent on two liters, which is his home rate.
**Reasoning.** The GOLD features for an antibiotic are present. He is not acidemic and he is talking. Bronchodilators, a short steroid course, an antibiotic, and his usual oxygen. The diagnosis is a moderate exacerbation. If he can eat and has follow-up, he can go home. If this oxygen rate is new today, he stays. The stem says it is his home rate, so you do not turn him up to 98 percent for comfort.
**NBME trap.** Noninvasive ventilation for a normal pH, or oxygen at a non-rebreather because he has COPD.
**Hook.** Antibiotic when purulence meets more dyspnea or more volume. The mask is for acidosis.

**Vignette.** A 40-year-old has sudden chest pain and mild dyspnea. Tall and thin. The film shows a small apical cap and no shift. Pressure and saturation are normal. No lung disease.
**Reasoning.** Primary spontaneous pneumothorax, stable, small. Observation is allowed if the current size criteria agree. You look up the centimeter. You do not aspirate every cap, and you do not send him to a wedding at altitude tomorrow. The diagnosis is a small primary pneumothorax. Counseling is no smoking, and return if the breath shortens. A different centimeter, or any centimeter in a person with COPD, is a different bed.
**NBME trap.** A tension-pneumothorax needle for a normal blood pressure and a small cap.
**Hook.** Stable and small can be watched. Look up the size. Do not invent it.

**Vignette.** After influenza improves, a 55-year-old worsens on day 6 with a high fever and a cavity on the film. Pressure is low.
**Reasoning.** Post-influenza deterioration with a cavity is Staphylococcus aureus until proven otherwise, including MRSA in the empiric drug when he is this sick. The diagnosis is a bacterial superinfection, severe. Intensive care, cultures, source control if an empyema is forming. The trap is another course of oseltamivir as the only new order.
**NBME trap.** Oseltamivir alone for a cavity and shock.
**Hook.** A second worsening after influenza is bacterial until proven otherwise.

**Vignette.** A 66-year-old has a unilateral effusion, smokes, and has lost 15 pounds. The fluid is bloody. Light’s criteria make it an exudate. He is saturating and afebrile.
**Reasoning.** A bloody exudate and weight loss in a smoker is cancer until cytology and imaging say otherwise. The diagnosis is a malignant effusion until proven otherwise. He does not need a chest tube tonight if he is comfortable and not accumulating under pressure. He does need a diagnostic path this week, not a diuretic trial. Dyspnea relief by thoracentesis is reasonable if he is short of breath. Tell him what you are worried about. That counseling is part of the visit.
**NBME trap.** Furosemide for a unilateral bloody exudate.
**Hook.** Exudate needs a cause. Bloody and unilateral in a smoker is cancer until it is not.

**Vignette.** A 33-year-old wheezes, has hives, and vomited after a new antibiotic. Blood pressure is 84/50. Someone hands you albuterol.
**Reasoning.** This is anaphylaxis. Intramuscular epinephrine now, then the airway adjuncts, fluids, and a period of observation because biphasic reactions exist. Albuterol is an adjunct. The diagnosis is anaphylaxis. She stays through the observation window your emergency practice uses. She leaves with epinephrine in hand and a reason to see an allergist. Tomorrow’s counseling is the drug she must never take again, written down.
**NBME trap.** A nebulizer as the only drug, or a discharge the moment the hives fade.
**Hook.** Urticaria, a new drug, and shock: epinephrine.

### A full computer-based case, played in order

The case opens in the office. A 67-year-old with COPD is sleepy, using accessory muscles, saturation unread. The nurse asks if you want the past surgical history first.

Move him before the past surgical history. Emergency department or a monitored bed. That move is the disposition, done early, and you can change it later if you were wrong. It is cheaper to move a well person than to code a person in a clinic chair.

Stabilize: oxygen only up to the retainer target, monitors, access, a glucose because altered mentation is not always carbon dioxide. Focused exam: can he protect the airway, is the chest symmetric, are the neck veins up, is a calf swollen, is there a fever. You do not need a complete review of systems to start a nebulizer.

Tests that change the next hour: arterial gas, chest film, electrocardiogram. A dimer is not one of them while he is sleepy and hypoxic. Treatment that is already running because it was stabilization: nebulized bronchodilator, systemic corticosteroid. If the gas shows respiratory acidosis and he is still protecting the airway, noninvasive ventilation. If the film shows a lobar pneumonia, add the antibiotic the severity demands and reconsider the bed. If the film shows a pneumothorax, the mask is the wrong machine until the air is drained.

Monitoring: repeat the gas. Reexamine. Advance the clock to that result with a reason. If he is pinker and awake, de-escalate the oxygen before you cause the next acidosis. If he is vomiting on the mask, intubate. Do not leave the emergency orders running into the next morning out of superstition. Convert intravenous steroid to oral when he swallows. Narrow an antibiotic if a culture returns. Ask about the cigarette and order the cessation drug when he can talk. Vaccines before discharge if he is due. A follow-up date. Return precautions.

The case fails in predictable ways. A complete physical exam while the saturation is unread. High-flow oxygen and a nap. A beautiful panel of send-out labs and no nebulizer. Discharge while the pH is still falling. A dietary lecture during the resuscitation, or, on the other end, an end to the case the moment the oxygen looks better, with no quit medicine and no follow-up. Both ends are graded as medicine, because both are medicine.

### What tomorrow should look like, by disease

Asthma that went home: a controller, a burst if you gave one, a peak-flow plan, follow-up in days, return precautions. Asthma that stayed: the same list waiting at discharge, plus a review of why this attack was life-threatening.

COPD that went home: long-acting bronchodilator, a short steroid course with an end date, an antibiotic only if the features were met, cessation medicine, vaccines, rehabilitation referral, follow-up in days. COPD that stayed: a daily question about whether the mask, the oxygen, and the steroid route can step down.

Pneumonia that went home: the oral drug, a number of days you confirmed, return precautions, vaccines when he can take them. Pneumonia that stayed: narrow the drug when the culture allows, look for an effusion that needs a tube, a five-day-style minimum you confirm rather than an open-ended intravenous habit.

A clot: the anticoagulant he will actually take, bleeding precautions, a duration conversation on the calendar, a filter-retrieval date if one was placed, no discharge while hypotensive.

A nodule: the comparison film and the Fleischner or Lung-RADS interval written as an order, not as advice. A screening-eligible smoker: the annual low-dose CT and the cessation drug.

Every one of them: tobacco, if it is in the story, is a medicine you offer. Every one of them: a sentence about when to come back. That is the Step 3 pulmonary case.

## Common traps

- High-flow oxygen as comfort in a chronic CO2 retainer who is sleepy. Target the saturation. Recheck the gas.
- Oral steroids forever for COPD. The maintenance plan is bronchodilators. Steroids are for the exacerbation and for a selected inhaled-steroid group.
- A d-dimer in a high-probability or an unstable patient.
- Sending pneumonia home because the CURB-65 was low, when the person cannot keep down water or lives alone and is confused. The tool is not the patient.
