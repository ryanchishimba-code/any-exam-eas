# Gastrointestinal and liver disease

> **Study aid only.** The next step in bleeding, biliary pain, and liver disease. A current ACG, AGA, or AASLD guideline outranks this summary for a real patient.

## What this chapter covers

The decisions that show up constantly: upper versus lower bleeding, who needs an urgent endoscopy, gallstone complications, and the outpatient liver problems. Gastrointestinal content is about 5–10% of Step 2 CK.

## The framework

Gastrointestinal bleeding is resuscitation first. Two large-bore access lines, type and screen, and a transfusion strategy. For a stable patient with upper bleeding, a restrictive threshold around 7 g/dL is the usual teaching from transfusion guidelines, and you transfuse sooner for instability, active ischemia, or ongoing massive loss. Do not quote 7 as a law for the crashing patient. Then ask where the blood is. Hematemesis or melena is upper until proven otherwise. Hematochezia is usually lower, and is sometimes a brisk upper bleed.

| Problem | Next decision |
| --- | --- |
| Suspected variceal bleed | Resuscitation, a vasoactive drug such as octreotide, and an antibiotic. Urgent endoscopy. Nonselective beta blocker is prevention, not the resuscitation. |
| Peptic ulcer disease | A proton-pump inhibitor, endoscopy for risk stratification, and a test-and-treat plan for Helicobacter pylori when an ulcer is present. |
| Gallstone, biliary colic | Pain that comes and goes, often after meals. Elective surgery when episodes recur and the patient is a surgical candidate. |
| Acute cholecystitis | Persistent pain, fever, a gallbladder wall that is inflamed. Antibiotics and an early surgical plan, not weeks of “cool down” as the default. |
| Cholangitis | Fever, jaundice, and pain is the classic triad and it is often incomplete. This is a duct under pressure plus infection. Resuscitation, antibiotics, and biliary drainage. It does not wait for a convenient weekday. |
| Ascites, new | Diagnostic paracentesis before you assume the cause. A neutrophil count at or above 250 cells/mm³ in ascites is spontaneous bacterial peritonitis until proven otherwise, and you treat. |

Cirrhosis outpatient care is a bundle, not a diuretic. Screen for varices as the hepatology guideline directs. Watch for hepatocellular carcinoma with the imaging interval AASLD currently recommends (ultrasound with or without alpha-fetoprotein, on a schedule you confirm rather than guess). Restrict sodium. Treat alcohol use as the disease. Hepatic encephalopathy is a search for a trigger (bleed, infection, constipation, medication) plus lactulose. Rifaximin is add-on therapy in recurrent encephalopathy, not the first move in a first episode you have not investigated.

Inflammatory bowel disease items want the complication: toxic megacolon, primary sclerosing cholangitis with ulcerative colitis, and cancer surveillance. Do not start by naming a brand of biologic. Name the emergency.

## Checklist

- [ ] Resuscitate before you locate the bleed.
- [ ] Restrictive transfusion is for the stable patient.
- [ ] Cholangitis is a drainage emergency.
- [ ] New ascites gets a tap. Neutrophils at or above 250 mean treat.
- [ ] Encephalopathy means look for the trigger.

## Common traps

- A beta blocker during an active variceal bleed. That drug is for prevention after the bleed is controlled, in the right patient.
- Skipping the tap because “it looks like cirrhosis.” Infection changes the hour.
- Oral antibiotics and a follow-up visit for cholangitis.
- Treating every elevated ALT as a statin injury. Alcohol, viral hepatitis, fatty liver, and obstruction are still more often the story. Check the pattern and the person.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Bleeding items: stability, access, transfusion stance, then source. Biliary items: colic versus cholecystitis versus cholangitis, because the clock is different. Feed cards that list antibiotic names for SBP or cholangitis should be checked against a current ID and hepatology source. Resistance patterns move.
