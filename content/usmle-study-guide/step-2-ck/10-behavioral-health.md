# Behavioral health

> **Study aid only.** Diagnosis and the first safe step. Full medication management belongs to a current APA or VA/DoD guideline and to the clinician who will follow the person.

## What this chapter covers

The diagnoses Step 2 actually tests, the emergencies (suicide, catatonia, severe withdrawal, neuroleptic malignant syndrome), and the drugs whose harms are the question. Behavioral health is about 5–10% of the form, and psychiatry as a clinical science is about 10–15%. The ranges overlap. Take them as “this is not optional.”

## The framework

A behavioral stem is medical until you have looked. Delirium, substances, thyroid, and a mass can look like a primary psychiatric illness. New psychosis in an older adult is a medical workup. Then the diagnosis is a time course plus function, not a single sad week.

| Situation | The safe move |
| --- | --- |
| Suicidal, with a plan, means, and intent | Do not discharge to “follow up.” This is emergency containment and a full assessment. Ask directly. Asking does not plant the idea. |
| Alcohol withdrawal, at risk of seizure or delirium | A benzodiazepine protocol, thiamine, and a monitored setting. Outpatient detox is for the mild, supported case. |
| Opioid overdose | Naloxone, airway, and a plan that outlasts the naloxone, because the opioid may outlast it. |
| Opioid use disorder | Buprenorphine or methadone saves lives. Withholding them for a moral reason is not a guideline. |
| Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | Rigidity, fever, autonomic instability on a dopamine blocker. Stop the drug. Cool. Support. A dopamine agonist or dantrolene is specialist care. |
| Serotonin syndrome | Clonus and hyperreflexia, often after a drug combination. Stop the drug. Benzodiazepine. Different from NMS, which is rigid and bradyreflexic. |
| Catatonia | Benzodiazepine challenge and look for the cause, including malignant catatonia. An antipsychotic can worsen it. |
| Depression, first medication | An SSRI is a common start. Screen for bipolar history first so you do not precipitate mania. Follow for suicidal behavior in the young, as the FDA warning describes. |
| Schizophrenia, maintenance | An antipsychotic. Clozapine for treatment-resistant disease and it requires ANC monitoring. Long-acting injectables when adherence is the failure point. |
| Lithium | Levels, kidney, thyroid. Teratogenic (Ebstein association is the classic teaching). Not a casual drug in pregnancy. |

Substance use is a chronic disease. The exam tests withdrawal timelines and the drug that treats the disease (naltrexone or acamprosate for alcohol, buprenorphine or methadone for opioids, varenicline or combination nicotine replacement for tobacco). Motivational interviewing is the skill. A lecture is not.

Capacity still applies. A person with schizophrenia who understands this decision can refuse a drug. Danger to self or others is a different legal path and it is jurisdiction-specific. Do not invent a hold law.

## High-yield atlas

Behavioral items on Step 2 CK are a medical emergency wearing a psychiatric face, or a drug whose harm is the question. Ask the time course, screen for mania before an antidepressant, and do not discharge the patient who has a plan, the means, and the intent.

| --- | --- | --- |
| The patient says he wants to die, has a plan, has access to the means, and intends to carry it out. A prior attempt raises the stakes further. He may still be calm. | Ask directly. Keep him safe now. Do not discharge to a clinic next week. Remove access to the means, especially a firearm. Complete a full assessment. | APA practice: asking does not plant the idea. A no-harm contract is not a discharge plan. Emergency containment and a clinician assessment come before any outpatient follow-up. Jurisdiction rules decide an involuntary hold. Do not invent a statute. |
| Tremor, sweating, tachycardia, and anxiety hours to a few days after the last drink. A history of a withdrawal seizure or delirium tremens raises the level of care. The patient can look acceptable at hour 8. | A benzodiazepine protocol in a monitored setting if the withdrawal is moderate or severe or the history is high risk. Thiamine. Do not discharge because the current vital signs are quiet. | VA/DoD and ASAM: symptom-triggered benzodiazepines for the syndrome that needs them. Outpatient detox is only the mild, supported case. Thiamine before or with glucose. Phenytoin is not the treatment of a withdrawal seizure. Haloperidol is not monotherapy. It lowers the seizure threshold. Seizures tend to come before delirium. Looking well early does not retire the risk. |

Offer medication for the use disorder before he disappears. | SAMHSA: naloxone is the antidote and it is temporary. Methadone and long-acting or potent synthetic opioids often outlast a single dose. Repeat as the protocol directs. Do not invent the milligrams. A prescription for naloxone without a treatment offer is incomplete. Buprenorphine or methadone is the disease treatment after the airway is safe. |

A moral objection is somewhere in the note. | Offer buprenorphine or methadone. Start buprenorphine when withdrawal is present so you do not precipitate it. Do not require a punitive detox before the medication. | SAMHSA: buprenorphine and methadone save lives. Methadone for this indication is dispensed through an opioid treatment program. Naltrexone is an option only after a full detox, and it blocks opioid analgesia. Clonidine eases symptoms. It is not the disease treatment. A positive urine is information, not a reason to withhold the medication as punishment. |
| A patient who has stopped or wants to stop drinking and is past the acute withdrawal. Liver tests and the need for opioid pain medicine are the branch point. | Offer naltrexone or acamprosate plus a psychosocial treatment. Match the drug to the liver and the kidney and to whether opioids are required. | VA/DoD: naltrexone is avoided when the patient needs opioids and is used cautiously in acute liver failure. Acamprosate depends on the kidney and is the option when the liver forbids naltrexone. Confirm the current file and the renal cutoff. Disulfiram is not first line and is dangerous if alcohol use is hidden. Do not slip it into food. |
| A patient who smokes and is willing to make an attempt. The history includes seizures or an eating disorder, or it does not. | Offer varenicline or combination nicotine replacement, plus behavioral support. Do not stop at advice if he is ready for a drug. | USPSTF recommends pharmacotherapy and behavioral support for adults who smoke. Combination nicotine replacement is a patch plus a short-acting form. Bupropion is another option and is a poor choice in a seizure disorder or bulimia. Confirm the current labeling. Do not invent a dose. |
| Lead-pipe rigidity, high fever, autonomic instability, and a quiet or confused mental state. The reflexes are decreased. The creatine kinase is high. A dopamine blocker was started or increased days ago, or a dopaminergic drug was stopped. | Stop the dopamine blocker. Cool. Fluid. Monitor in a high-acuity bed. A dopamine agonist or dantrolene is specialist care. Do not give an anticholinergic and call it dystonia. | This is a drug emergency, not a side effect to push through. Supportive care is the core. Bromocriptine or dantrolene is added in specialist hands. Confirm before you use either. Restarting an antipsychotic later is a careful rechallenge, not the next hospital day. Metoclopramide counts as a dopamine blocker. |
| Clonus, hyperreflexia, tremor, and diarrhea, often with fever, starting over hours after a serotonergic combination. Ocular clonus is a clue. The reflexes are brisk, not dull. | Stop the serotonergic drugs. A benzodiazepine for agitation. Cyproheptadine if the syndrome is moderate or severe and not settling. Do not give bromocriptine. | Support and the benzodiazepine are the start. Cyproheptadine is the serotonin antagonist used when the picture is more than mild. Confirm it with the current toxicology approach. The classic combinations include an SSRI plus an MAOI, linezolid, methylene blue, tramadol, or a triptan. |
| Mutism, immobility, waxy flexibility, posturing, negativism, or echolalia. The patient is not simply refusing. Vital signs can become unstable if it is malignant. A psychiatric or medical cause is in the background. | A benzodiazepine challenge. Look for the cause, including mood disease and a medical illness. Do not give haloperidol as the treatment. Prepare for electroconvulsive therapy if it is malignant or it does not break. | APA practice: lorazepam is the challenge and often the treatment. An antipsychotic can worsen catatonia and can precipitate a malignant, neuroleptic-malignant-like state. Electroconvulsive therapy is the definitive step for malignant or refractory catatonia. Find the driver. |
| At least two weeks of low mood or anhedonia, with sleep, interest, guilt, energy, concentration, appetite, and psychomotor changes, and a drop in function. There is no mania history yet, or there is and nobody asked. | Screen for a bipolar history before the first antidepressant. An SSRI is a common first medication. Psychotherapy is also first line for mild to moderate disease. Arrange close follow-up. | APA and VA/DoD: an SSRI is a standard start when a medication is chosen and bipolar disease has been excluded. The FDA warns of suicidal behavior in children, adolescents, and young adults early in treatment. Follow them. Do not refuse to treat because of the warning. Do not start an antidepressant the same week as an MAOI. |
| A week or more of elevated or irritable mood, less need for sleep, pressured speech, grandiosity, and risky behavior. Any duration counts if he is already hospitalized or psychotic. Function is down or the behavior is dangerous. | Safety, a medical look (stimulant, steroid, thyroid), and a mood stabilizer, an antipsychotic, or both. Do not start an antidepressant. | APA: acute mania is a mood stabilizer, an antipsychotic, or the combination the severity requires. Lithium and valproate are classic stabilizers. Confirm the current choice and check a pregnancy test before valproate. Neither is a casual drug in pregnancy. Sleep loss is a symptom and a trigger. Do not treat it with an SSRI. |
| A current major depressive episode in a patient with a prior manic or hypomanic episode. The stem may bury the prior mania in one clause about a week without sleep. | A mood-stabilizing regimen. Do not start an antidepressant as the only drug. Ask about mixed features (racing thoughts inside the depression). | APA and VA/DoD: evidence-based options include lithium, lamotrigine, and several antipsychotics with evidence in bipolar depression. Confirm the current list before you name one as the only choice. An antidepressant is added only to a mood stabilizer, and not as the sole drug. Quetiapine, lurasidone, and olanzapine-fluoxetine are among the regimens those documents discuss. Confirm before prescribing. |
| Persistent psychosis after two adequate antipsychotic trials, taken at a real dose for a real duration. Adherence was actually assessed. The patient is not simply missing every dose. | Clozapine, with the absolute neutrophil count on the schedule the current program requires. If the failures were missed doses, use a long-acting injectable instead of calling it resistance. | APA: clozapine is for treatment-resistant schizophrenia and it requires neutrophil monitoring. Do not start it outside that program. Severe constipation and ileus, myocarditis, seizures, and metabolic disease are the other harms to know. A third ordinary antipsychotic is not the resistant-disease step. Confirm the monitoring schedule. It changes. |
| A patient on lithium who is confused, ataxic, or vomiting, often after a new NSAID, an ACE inhibitor, a thiazide, or dehydration. The tremor is coarse. The kidneys may be failing. | Stop lithium. Check the level, the creatinine, and the electrolytes. Hydrate. Do not start an NSAID or an ACE inhibitor and recheck in three months. | Hold the drug. Supportive care and saline for volume loss. Hemodialysis is for severe toxicity (seizures, severe neurologic findings, or a level and a kidney that cannot clear it). Confirm the dialysis criteria with toxicology. Chronic use still needs a level, a creatinine, and a TSH on a schedule. Lithium is associated with Ebstein anomaly and is not a casual drug in pregnancy. Do not stop it abruptly in a stable patient without a plan. Rebound mania is real. |
| Acute, fluctuating confusion with inattention. Worse at night. A medical or drug cause is available: postoperative state, infection, an anticholinergic, a benzodiazepine, urinary retention. The family says this is not his baseline. | Find and treat the cause. Orient him, mobilize him, and fix sleep, pain, retention, and constipation. An antipsychotic is only for dangerous agitation, at the lowest dose, for the shortest time. | Do not use a benzodiazepine to help a non-withdrawal delirium sleep. It worsens the confusion. Do not diagnose a new primary psychosis in an older adult and stop the workup. The American Geriatrics Society and standard delirium practice put nonpharmacologic care first. |
| Restriction, a markedly low weight, and a danger sign: bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, syncope, or an electrolyte abnormality. The patient may say she feels fine. | Admit. Refeed on a protocol and watch the phosphate. Do not send her to weekly therapy as the only plan while the heart rate is in the 30s. | APA eating-disorder guidance: medical instability is inpatient care. Refeeding syndrome is a fall in phosphate, potassium, and magnesium. Monitor them. Psychotherapy is the disease treatment once she is medically safe. An SSRI does not restore weight. Fluoxetine has a role in bulimia, which is a different weight and a different emergency. |
| Exposure to a traumatic event, then more than a month of intrusion, avoidance, negative mood or thoughts, and hyperarousal. Function is down. If the same picture has lasted less than a month, it is acute stress disorder. | Offer a trauma-focused psychotherapy. If a medication is used, an SSRI or venlafaxine. Do not make a benzodiazepine the treatment. | VA/DoD: trauma-focused psychotherapy (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, or EMDR) is first line. Sertraline, paroxetine, and venlafaxine are the medication names those guidelines discuss. Confirm the current file. Benzodiazepines do not treat the disorder and can worsen it. Do not make a single nightmare drug the whole plan. |
| Recurrent abrupt surges of palpitations, chest pain, dyspnea, and fear, peaking in minutes, plus a month of worry about the next one. A medical workup that fits the risk has already been done. The attacks are not exertional. | Cognitive behavioral therapy, an SSRI, or both. A benzodiazepine can abort a severe attack and is not the five-year plan. If this is the first episode, or the pain is exertional, or the patient is older with vascular risk, an ECG and a medical look come before the psychiatric label. | APA: an SSRI and cognitive behavioral therapy are the disease treatments. Rule out the mimics that the story suggests: arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, hyperthyroidism, stimulant use. Do not diagnose panic from a single exertional chest pain in a 60-year-old smoker. |

| Pair | How you tell them apart |
| --- | --- |
| Suicide with a plan, means, and intent versus the neighbor | Chronic suicidal thoughts without intent, plan, or means can be managed with a close outpatient plan after a real assessment. That is a different stem. Homicidal intent with a named person is a duty-to-protect problem and is also not a pamphlet. |
| Alcohol withdrawal versus the neighbor | Wernicke encephalopathy is confusion, ataxia, and eye signs and is thiamine, which this patient also needs. Intoxication is slurred and sedated now, not tachycardic and tremulous as the alcohol level falls. Opioid withdrawal is miserable and not a seizure syndrome. |
| Opioid overdose versus the neighbor | Benzodiazepine overdose sedates without pinpoint pupils and is not reliably reversed by naloxone. Alcohol intoxication smells of alcohol and is not an opioid. Opioid withdrawal is the opposite picture: dilated pupils, diarrhea, yawning, and a normal respiratory rate. |
| Opioid use disorder versus the neighbor | Overdose is respiratory depression and gets naloxone first. Withdrawal without any offer of medication is the undertreated state. Alcohol use disorder has its own medications and its own withdrawal seizures. |
| Alcohol use disorder, maintenance treatment versus the neighbor | Acute withdrawal still needs a benzodiazepine and thiamine, not a maintenance drug as the only order. Opioid use disorder is buprenorphine or methadone. Naltrexone would block those drugs and cannot be combined casually. |
| Tobacco use disorder versus the neighbor | Alcohol and opioid use disorders have different drugs. A patient who is not ready is a motivational-interviewing conversation, not a lecture, and not a hidden prescription. |
| Neuroleptic malignant syndrome versus the neighbor | Serotonin syndrome is clonus, hyperreflexia, and often diarrhea, over hours, after a serotonergic combination. Malignant hyperthermia is during anesthesia with a volatile agent or succinylcholine. Acute dystonia is a few hours of a twisted neck and responds to an anticholinergic. |
| Serotonin syndrome versus the neighbor | Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is lead-pipe rigidity, bradyreflexia, and a high creatine kinase over days. Anticholinergic toxicity is dry, delirious, and tachycardic without clonus. Malignant hyperthermia is the operating room. |
| Catatonia versus the neighbor | Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is rigidity and fever caused by a dopamine blocker. Malignant catatonia can look similar and is a reason to avoid that blocker and to move toward electroconvulsive therapy. Elective mutism in a child is a different, non-medical picture and is not waxy flexibility. |
| Major depressive disorder, first medication versus the neighbor | Bipolar depression gets a mood-stabilizing regimen, not an antidepressant alone. Grief without the depressive syndrome does not need a drug. Hypothyroidism and a substance mimic the mood and are still on the medical list. |
| Acute mania versus the neighbor | Major depression has the opposite energy and sleep story and can take an SSRI after a negative bipolar screen. Stimulant intoxication is a shorter, drug-linked picture. Hypomania does not markedly impair function and is not this hospitalization. |
| Bipolar depression versus the neighbor | Unipolar major depression has no manic history and can start with an SSRI. Acute mania is the high pole and is not treated with an antidepressant either. Mixed depression feels down and wired at the same time and is even more reason to avoid an antidepressant alone. |
| Treatment-resistant schizophrenia versus the neighbor | Missed oral doses are nonadherence. A long-acting injectable comes before the resistance label. First-episode schizophrenia starts with an ordinary antipsychotic, not clozapine. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a reason to stop a dopamine blocker, not a reason to push the dose. |
| Lithium toxicity versus the neighbor | Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is rigidity and fever on a dopamine blocker. Serotonin syndrome is clonus. A stable patient on lithium who feels well still needs the level, the kidney, and the thyroid checked. That maintenance visit is not toxicity. |
| Delirium versus the neighbor | Dementia is insidious and the family describes a year, not a night. Primary psychosis usually starts young and does not wax over hours with inattention. Alcohol withdrawal delirium gets a benzodiazepine, which is the exception. |
| Anorexia nervosa, medically unstable versus the neighbor | Bulimia is often a normal weight, with bingeing and purging, hypokalemia, and enamel erosion. Fluoxetine is an evidence-based adjunct there, after the potassium is safe. Major depression can coexist with either and does not explain a heart rate of 38. |
| Post-traumatic stress disorder versus the neighbor | Acute stress disorder is three days to one month. Major depression can overlap and has its own SSRI path, still after a bipolar screen. Panic disorder is abrupt surges without a required trauma cluster. |
| Panic disorder versus the neighbor | Acute coronary syndrome is exertional or persistent pressure and an ECG emergency. Pulmonary embolism is pleuritic pain and a risk factor. Hyperthyroidism has a low TSH and weight loss. A single attack with a normal workup is not yet the disorder. |

### Suicide with a plan, means, and intent

Intent plus a plan plus access is the short-term risk that changes disposition. Passive wishes without a plan are still asked about, and they are not this same discharge decision.

**Vignette.** A 40-year-old says he will shoot himself tonight. There is a firearm at home. He has a date and a place in mind. He is speaking quietly and his affect is flat. A follow-up appointment in seven days and a written promise not to harm himself are offered as the plan.
**Reasoning.** Plan, means, and intent are already present. Calm speech does not lower that. He stays, the firearm question is acted on, and a contract is not the intervention. Asking the question was correct. Discharging on the answer is not. The diagnosis is Suicide with a plan, means, and intent.
**NBME trap.** A safety contract and a next-week appointment, or avoiding the question because it might plant the idea.
**Hook.** Plan, means, intent: he does not go home. Asking does not plant the idea.

### Alcohol withdrawal

Autonomic rebound and a lowered seizure threshold after a GABA-ergic drug is removed. Delirium tremens is the later confusional state.

**Vignette.** A man whose last drink was this morning has a tremor and a heart rate of 110. He had a withdrawal seizure two years ago. At hour 8 he says he feels fine and asks to leave. No benzodiazepine has been given. Thiamine has not been given.
**Reasoning.** The prior seizure makes this a high-risk withdrawal even though he is polite at hour 8. He needs a monitored benzodiazepine protocol and thiamine. Sending him out is how the seizure arrives at hour 24. The diagnosis is Alcohol withdrawal.
**NBME trap.** Discharge at hour 8, phenytoin as the seizure drug, or haloperidol alone.
**Hook.** Hour 8 can look calm. The seizure is later. Benzodiazepine and thiamine.

### Opioid overdose

Mu-receptor agonism stops the respiratory drive. Pupils and the respiratory rate are the exam. Naloxone displaces the agonist and then wears off.

**Vignette.** A man is found barely breathing with pinpoint pupils. After one dose of naloxone he sits up and asks to go. He used what he believes was heroin. No observation period is planned, and no one mentions treatment.
**Reasoning.** The airway was the emergency and it may not be over. He needs observation because the agonist can return after the naloxone fades. The moment he is awake is also the moment to offer buprenorphine or methadone, not only a lecture. The diagnosis is Opioid overdose.
**NBME trap.** Sidewalk discharge the minute he speaks, or naloxone as the entire plan with no offer of treatment.
**Hook.** Naloxone wears off. Watch him, then offer real treatment.

### Opioid use disorder

A chronic use disorder with a high risk of death on return to use. The partial agonist must not be started while a full agonist still occupies the receptor, or withdrawal is precipitated.

**Vignette.** A man in moderate withdrawal after stopping fentanyl asks for help. He is tachycardic, yawning, and in clear withdrawal. The plan offered is a clonidine taper, a pamphlet, and buprenorphine only if next week's urine is negative.
**Reasoning.** He is in withdrawal now, which is the window for a buprenorphine start, or he can be referred for methadone. Clonidine does not treat the disorder. A clean urine as a ticket is a moral rule, not a SAMHSA rule. The diagnosis is Opioid use disorder.
**NBME trap.** Clonidine-only detox as definitive care, or buprenorphine given while the patient is still comfortable on a full agonist.
**Hook.** Offer buprenorphine or methadone. Do not make him earn it.

### Alcohol use disorder, maintenance treatment

A chronic disease with medications that reduce return to heavy drinking. Detox without a maintenance plan is why the patient is back next month.

**Vignette.** A woman completed a benzodiazepine taper yesterday. She does not use opioids. Her liver enzymes are near normal and her kidneys are normal. She is handed a meeting list and no medication. Disulfiram is mentioned as something the family could give secretly.
**Reasoning.** She is a candidate for naltrexone or acamprosate and for a real psychosocial plan. A meeting list alone leaves the medication benefit behind. Secret disulfiram is not a regimen. The diagnosis is Alcohol use disorder, maintenance treatment.
**NBME trap.** Naltrexone in a patient on methadone, or disulfiram given without the patient's knowledge.
**Hook.** After detox, offer naltrexone or acamprosate. Match the organ that clears it.

### Tobacco use disorder

Nicotine dependence. Counseling plus a drug beats either lecture alone. The drug is chosen around the comorbidities.

**Vignette.** A 50-year-old smokes a pack a day and asks for medicine to quit. He has bulimia and a past seizure. Bupropion is prescribed because it also causes weight loss. No nicotine replacement and no varenicline are discussed.
**Reasoning.** He asked for pharmacotherapy, which is correct. Bupropion is the wrong member of the list given the seizure and the eating disorder. Varenicline or combination nicotine replacement fits. Advice alone would also have been incomplete. The diagnosis is Tobacco use disorder.
**NBME trap.** Bupropion in bulimia or epilepsy, or willpower as the only offer when he asked for a drug.
**Hook.** Varenicline or combination nicotine replacement. Skip bupropion if he seizes or purges.

### Neuroleptic malignant syndrome

Dopamine blockade in the hypothalamus and the striatum. Onset is days, not hours. The muscle is rigid and the reflexes are dull. That is how you separate it from serotonin syndrome.

**Vignette.** Three days after haloperidol was started, a man has a fever of 40 C, a blood pressure that swings, lead-pipe rigidity, and a creatine kinase in the thousands. Reflexes are hard to elicit. Cyproheptadine is ordered, and the haloperidol is continued.
**Reasoning.** Rigidity, fever, autonomic chaos, and a dopamine blocker are this syndrome. The drug stops. Cyproheptadine treats serotonin syndrome, which would be clonus and hyperreflexia over hours, not lead-pipe rigidity over days. The diagnosis is Neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
**NBME trap.** Continuing the antipsychotic, or cyproheptadine because the patient has a fever and a psychiatric drug.
**Hook.** Rigid and bradyreflexic over days: stop the dopamine blocker.

### Serotonin syndrome

Excess serotonin at the synapse. Onset is hours. The neuromuscular finding is clonus, not lead pipe. Hunter criteria put clonus at the center.

**Vignette.** A woman on sertraline is given linezolid for a soft-tissue infection. Within hours she has fever, tremor, inducible clonus, hyperreflexia, and diarrhea. Bromocriptine is suggested because a recent lecture covered rigid fevers.
**Reasoning.** The time course is hours, the reflex finding is clonus, and the combination is serotonergic. Stop both drugs and give a benzodiazepine. Bromocriptine is the specialist drug for the rigid dopamine-blocker syndrome, which this is not. The diagnosis is Serotonin syndrome.
**NBME trap.** Bromocriptine, or continuing linezolid because the SSRI seems like a small drug.
**Hook.** Clonus and hyperreflexia over hours: stop the combination. Benzodiazepine.

### Catatonia

A motor and behavioral shutdown that cuts across diagnoses. Negativism looks like refusal and is not a character problem. Malignant catatonia adds fever and autonomic instability.

**Vignette.** A man with bipolar disorder sits immobile, mute, with an arm held in the air. He does not eat. A note says he is being manipulative. Haloperidol is ordered for presumed psychosis. No benzodiazepine is given.
**Reasoning.** Posturing, mutism, and immobility are catatonia. The challenge is a benzodiazepine. Haloperidol can make this worse. Calling it manipulation delays the drug that treats it. The diagnosis is Catatonia.
**NBME trap.** Haloperidol for the mutism, or a behavioral contract for negativism.
**Hook.** Mute and posturing: a benzodiazepine. Not haloperidol.

### Major depressive disorder, first medication

A depressive episode of sufficient duration and breadth. Two sad days are not an episode. Grief can coexist with an episode when the criteria are actually met.

**Vignette.** A 20-year-old has six weeks of anhedonia, insomnia, poor concentration, and a failing semester. She has no plan to die. Her father has bipolar disorder and she once went four days without sleep, talking rapidly, which no one asks about. An SSRI is started and the next visit is in two months.
**Reasoning.** The depressive syndrome is real, and the missed four days of no sleep is a bipolar screen that has to happen before the SSRI. If that history holds, an antidepressant alone can precipitate mania. The young-adult follow-up also has to be soon, not in two months. The diagnosis is Major depressive disorder, first medication.
**NBME trap.** An SSRI with no bipolar screen, or no follow-up in a young adult because of a misread FDA warning that was used to cancel treatment.
**Hook.** Ask about mania first. Then an SSRI, and see the young patient soon.

### Acute mania

A bipolar I episode. Antidepressant monotherapy can prolong it or precipitate it. The duration criterion shortens if the patient is already in a hospital or psychotic.

**Vignette.** A 24-year-old has slept two hours a night for ten days, spent his rent, and is speaking in an unstoppable stream. He is not suicidal. A relative asks for an antidepressant because he seems happy. Urine drug screen and thyroid tests are not ordered.
**Reasoning.** Ten days of decreased sleep, pressured speech, and harmful spending is mania. An antidepressant is the wrong class. He needs a mood stabilizer or an antipsychotic, and a quick look for a stimulant or a steroid so you do not miss a mimic. The diagnosis is Acute mania.
**NBME trap.** An SSRI because the mood is high, or valproate started without a pregnancy test.
**Hook.** Less sleep and more plans: a stabilizer or an antipsychotic. Not an antidepressant.

### Bipolar depression

The depressive pole of bipolar illness. It looks like ordinary depression if you do not ask about the prior high. Antidepressant monotherapy is how the next mania starts.

**Vignette.** A woman with a documented manic episode three years ago is now depressed for a month. She is started on sertraline alone. No mood stabilizer is on the list. She is told this is the same plan as her sister's unipolar depression.
**Reasoning.** The prior mania changes the regimen. Sertraline alone is the unipolar plan and the bipolar error. She needs a mood-stabilizing regimen from the current APA or VA/DoD list. The diagnosis is Bipolar depression.
**NBME trap.** An antidepressant as the only new drug.
**Hook.** Prior mania plus today's depression: do not use an antidepressant alone.

### Treatment-resistant schizophrenia

Two failed adequate trials. Nonadherence is a different failure and a long-acting injectable is the tool. Clozapine is also the antipsychotic with evidence when suicidal behavior persists in schizophrenia.

**Vignette.** A man has taken risperidone and then olanzapine, each at a documented dose for months, with pill counts and levels that show he took them. He is still hearing voices that command him. A switch to aripiprazole is proposed. No neutrophil plan is mentioned.
**Reasoning.** Two adequate, adhered-to trials have failed. The next drug is clozapine, inside the neutrophil program. Another first-line antipsychotic repeats a strategy that has already failed. The diagnosis is Treatment-resistant schizophrenia.
**NBME trap.** A third ordinary antipsychotic after two real failures, or clozapine with no neutrophil monitoring.
**Hook.** Two real failures: clozapine and a neutrophil count. Missed doses: an injectable.

### Lithium toxicity

A narrow-index drug cleared by the kidney. NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, and thiazides raise the level. Toxicity is neurologic and gastrointestinal before it is a number you like.

**Vignette.** A man on lithium for years starts ibuprofen and lisinopril in the same week after a sprain and a high blood-pressure reading. He is now vomiting and confused, with a coarse tremor. The lithium level is not ordered. The new drugs are continued.
**Reasoning.** The new NSAID and the ACE inhibitor plus confusion and tremor are lithium toxicity until the level says otherwise. Stop the lithium and the interacting drugs, check the level and the creatinine, and hydrate. A routine refill is the error. The diagnosis is Lithium toxicity.
**NBME trap.** Adding an NSAID or an ACE inhibitor without a new level, or abrupt cessation of a therapeutic lithium with no mood plan.
**Hook.** NSAID, ACE inhibitor, or a dry week: check the level and stop the combination.

### Delirium

An acute brain failure from a medical insult. Attention is the exam finding. Dementia is slow and attention is spared until late. The two can coexist, and an acute change is still delirium.

**Vignette.** An 82-year-old is lucid in the morning and picking at the air at night on postoperative day 2. He cannot say the months backward. Lorazepam is ordered for sleep, and the bladder is not checked.
**Reasoning.** Acute fluctuating inattention is delirium. The benzodiazepine will deepen it. Look at drugs, oxygen, infection, electrolytes, pain, and a full bladder. A new diagnosis of schizophrenia at 82 is the miss. The diagnosis is Delirium.
**NBME trap.** Lorazepam for sleep, or haloperidol as the treatment of the cause.
**Hook.** Inattention that fluctuates: fix the cause. A benzodiazepine is for withdrawal.

### Anorexia nervosa, medically unstable

Starvation with a risk of arrhythmia and of refeeding shifts. Insight is often preserved in the sense that she can talk, and it is impaired about the weight. Capacity around the food refusal is a separate, careful assessment. Do not invent a hold law.

**Vignette.** A 19-year-old has a heart rate of 38, a temperature of 35.5 C, and dizziness on standing. Her body mass index is very low and she restricts tightly. She is cheerful and asks to start outpatient therapy next Thursday. Phosphate is borderline.
**Reasoning.** Bradycardia and hypothermia are instability. Thursday is too late. She is admitted and refed with phosphate watched. Cheerfulness does not change the vital signs. An antidepressant will not replace the calories. The diagnosis is Anorexia nervosa, medically unstable.
**NBME trap.** Outpatient therapy for a bradycardic, hypothermic patient, or a rapid refeed with no phosphate checks.
**Hook.** Unstable vital signs: admit and watch the phosphate. Therapy waits on safety.

### Post-traumatic stress disorder

A trauma-linked syndrome past the one-month line. Acute stress disorder is the earlier window and is not a reason to start a benzodiazepine either.

**Vignette.** Four months after an assault, a woman has nightmares, avoids the neighborhood, feels numb, and startles easily. She is not sleeping. Alprazolam is started as the only treatment. No therapy is offered.
**Reasoning.** Duration past a month and the four symptom clusters are the disorder. The VA/DoD first line is a trauma-focused therapy. A benzodiazepine as the sole drug is the harm, not the treatment. An SSRI or venlafaxine is the medication class if a drug is added. The diagnosis is Post-traumatic stress disorder.
**NBME trap.** A standing benzodiazepine as the plan, or no offer of a trauma-focused therapy.
**Hook.** Trauma-focused therapy first. An SSRI or venlafaxine if you use a drug. Not a benzodiazepine.

### Panic disorder

A primary anxiety disorder of unexpected surges. The first medical look is proportional to the risk. After that look is negative, more testing is not the treatment.

**Vignette.** A 27-year-old has had six identical unexpected attacks in two months, each peaking within minutes, with palpitations and fear of dying. Between attacks she avoids exercise class because she fears another surge. The ECG, the exam, and the thyroid test are normal. Alprazolam is prescribed as the only long-term drug.
**Reasoning.** The attacks are recurrent, unexpected, and already medically cleared. This is panic disorder. The long-term treatments are therapy and an SSRI. A benzodiazepine as the only maintenance drug is the trap. Had the story been one exertional pressure in an older smoker, the ECG would have been the whole first step and this diagnosis would have been premature. The diagnosis is Panic disorder.
**NBME trap.** Alprazolam as the only long-term drug, or a panic label on the first exertional chest pain without an ECG.
**Hook.** Clear the dangerous mimic, then therapy and an SSRI. A benzodiazepine is not the maintenance plan.

## Common traps

- An antidepressant alone for bipolar depression. You can flip the person into mania.
- Discharging alcohol withdrawal because the person “seems fine” at hour 8. Seizures come later.
- Haloperidol for catatonia.
- A feed card that says “SSRI and you are done” with no bipolar screen and no follow-up.
