# Ethics, patient safety, and evidence

> **Study aid only.** The published social-science range on Step 2 CK is about 10–15%. That is larger than several organs. This is not legal advice for your state.

## What this chapter covers

The safety behaviors the exam treats as answers, the ethics sequence from Step 1 applied to a supervised physician, and the evidence skills that read a paper in the stem.

## The framework

Safety items have a correct action that feels socially awkward. Do it anyway.

| Situation | The action |
| --- | --- |
| You are about to make a wrong-site or wrong-patient error | Stop the line. A time-out exists for this. Hierarchy is not a contraindication. |
| A colleague is impaired | Report through the path your institution uses. Covering the shift forever is not the treatment. |
| A patient does not speak the language | A professional interpreter. Not the child in the room. Not your semester of the language. |
| Bad news | A private setting, a warning, the news in plain words, silence, then a plan. Do not start with a pile of pathophysiology. |
| An error reached the patient | Tell them what happened, what it means, and what you will do. Hiding it is the ethics failure. |
| A surrogate asks you not to tell the patient | Capacity first. If the patient has capacity, the information is theirs unless they have asked not to know. |
| A gift or a drug-rep meal would change the drug you name | Decline the influence. A cheap pen is not the issue. The decision is. |
| Discharge | Teach-back, the medicines reconciled, and a follow-up that exists. “Any questions?” is not teach-back. |

Evidence items in a stem are abstracts and ads. Read the outcome first. A surrogate marker (a lab) is not a patient-important outcome (a stroke, a death, a hospitalization) unless the question is about the lab. Absolute risk matters more than relative risk when you counsel. A significant p value with a tiny effect in a huge trial may not be a reason to treat. A nonsignificant p value in a tiny trial is not proof of no effect.

Conflict of interest in a paper is a reason to read harder, not an automatic reason to discard a well-run trial. Funding does not invent a confidence interval. It does change how you look at the outcome the authors chose.

## High-yield atlas

Safety and ethics items have a correct action that feels socially awkward. Use a qualified interpreter, tell patients about errors that reached them, and judge capacity before you honor a refusal or turn to a surrogate. Evidence items ask what was measured and how large the effect is in absolute numbers. These are principles for the exam, not legal advice for a state.

A child or a family member is about to interpret. | A qualified interpreter, in person or by a professional video or phone service. Not the child. A capacitated adult may refuse blood, surgery, or chemotherapy. Assess capacity first if the refusal seems inconsistent with the patient's own stated values or if delirium is present. This is not a suicide plan you assist, and it is not a reason to provide a method. |

Involve ethics or a court for disagreements that are not emergencies. Do not delay resuscitation for a signature. | Pediatric emergency ethics. Parents decide for children except when refusal poses immediate serious harm. A capacitated adult may refuse blood for themselves and may not refuse it for a hemorrhaging child. Elective disputes can go to ethics and, if needed, a court. This describes the duty. It is not a state code section. |

Right patient, right procedure, right site. Anyone can stop it. Hierarchy is not a contraindication. | Joint Commission Universal Protocol. Site marking happens before the time-out. A wrong-site event is a never event. Speaking up is the action. Do not proceed and file a note later as a substitute. |
| A colleague is about to care for patients and is impaired: alcohol on the breath, slurred speech, or a pattern of errors from substance use or illness. | Remove them from patient care now if they are unsafe. Report through the physician health or credentialing path your institution uses. Do not cover the shift forever. Do not announce it in the waiting room. | AMA ethics opinion on impaired physicians. The duty is to the patients, not to a quiet favor and not to a punishment. A single bad day is not the same as impairment, and a pattern that risks patients is not a loyalty test. This is a reporting path, not a criminal complaint you draft. |
| A serious result is about to be given in a corridor, standing, in jargon, with no pause. | A private setting, what the patient already knows, a warning, the news in plain words, silence, the emotion, then a plan. | The SPIKES framework is a communication method used in oncology and palliative care, not a law. Do not start with a pile of pathophysiology. Do not deliver a first devastating result by ambush in the hallway. A phone call is for distance, with the patient's agreement, not for convenience. |
| A family member asks you not to tell a capacitated patient the diagnosis. The patient is asking what the test showed. | Ask the patient how much they want to know. If they want the information, tell them. A family request does not override a capacitated patient's right to their own result. | AMA ethics on truth-telling. If the patient has already asked not to know, honor that and speak with the person they name. Therapeutic privilege, withholding news because you predict it will harm, is narrow and is not a family's convenience. Capacity comes first. This is not a cultural veto you invent. |
| A patient is going home on a high-risk medicine. The nurse asks if there are any questions. The patient nods. Nobody has heard the plan in the patient's words. | Teach-back. The patient explains the medicine, how to take it, and the warning signs. Reconcile the list. Make a follow-up that exists. | AHRQ health-literacy guidance. Any questions is not teach-back. Use plain language and the language the patient uses, with an interpreter if needed. A nod is not understanding. Do not invent a regimen the guideline does not own. |
| A headline says a drug cuts events in half. The table shows 2 events per 100 without it and 1 per 100 with it. | Counsel with the absolute difference. Offer the number needed to treat. Do not lead with the relative headline. | Standard evidence-based medicine. Absolute risk reduction is the control event rate minus the experimental event rate. Relative risk reduction divides that difference by the control rate. Number needed to treat is 1 divided by the absolute risk reduction. In this table the absolute reduction is 1 per 100, the relative reduction is half, and the number needed to treat is 100. Use the absolute number when you talk to a patient. |
| A trial or an advertisement shows a lab moving. Heart attacks, strokes, deaths, admissions, or quality of life were not the result. | Say what was measured. Do not tell the patient the lab change prevents a clinical event the trial did not count. | Standard evidence-based medicine. A surrogate marker is a lab or a radiograph. A patient-important outcome is something the patient feels or survives. Conflict of interest and industry funding are reasons to look harder at the endpoint the authors chose. Funding does not invent a confidence interval, and it does not let you discard a well-run trial automatically. |
| A small trial reports a p value above the usual threshold and a confidence interval that runs from a large benefit to a large harm. | Say the trial was too small to answer the question. Do not conclude the treatment has no effect. | Standard evidence-based medicine. Failure to reject the null is not proof of the null. A confidence interval that includes the null, and also includes a clinically large effect, is uninformative. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A large trial with a tight interval around a tiny effect is a different problem: statistically detectable and possibly not worth giving. |
| A patient names a person and describes a credible plan to harm that person. This is not a vague statement of anger. | Act to protect the identifiable person. That can include warning them and notifying police. Document. Do not keep this particular secret. | The exam principle is a duty to protect an identifiable third party from a credible threat. State law varies, and this is not a statute. A wish to be dead is a suicide assessment, not a warning to a third party, and it is not a reason to discuss methods. Abuse of a child is a report even without a named future victim. |
| A reasonable suspicion of abuse or neglect. You do not have proof, and you may not know who did it. | Report to child protective services through the path your hospital uses. Treat injuries. Do not make the parent your only call. | The exam principle is mandatory reporting on reasonable suspicion. Reporting is not a confidentiality violation. You do not investigate to the point of certainty first. This is not a specific state's penal code. Adult intimate-partner violence has a different, often supportive path. Do not invent a reporting statute for competent adults. |
| A meal, a speaking fee, or a sample closet is attached to naming a particular drug, especially when a guideline or a generic equivalent would do. | Decline the influence. Choose the drug from the guideline and the patient. Disclose research conflicts if you are writing or teaching. | AMA ethics opinions on gifts from industry. A cheap pen is not the issue. The decision is. Funding of a paper is a reason to read the endpoint harder, not an automatic reason to discard a well-run trial. Do not accept a fee that picks the brand. |
| The patient has lost capacity. A directive, or a named agent, refuses a treatment. A newly arrived relative demands that treatment. | Follow the directive and the named agent. A new relative does not override a clear refusal. If there is no directive, the surrogate uses substituted judgment, then best interests. | AMA ethics on advance care planning. Substituted judgment asks what the patient would have wanted. Best interests are used when that is unknowable. A POLST or MOLST for a seriously ill patient is an order, not a vague conversation. Conflicts that are not emergencies can go to ethics. Do not invent a hierarchy that puts the loudest relative first. |
| An adolescent requests contraception, testing for a sexually transmitted infection, or pregnancy care, and asks that a parent not be called. | In the usual exam frame, provide that care confidentially. Break confidentiality if the adolescent is being abused or is in immediate danger. Do not call a parent to report a chlamydia result as the reflex. | The exam principle is confidential care for sensitive services. Age cutoffs and which services qualify vary by jurisdiction. Do not state this as a national statute, and confirm the law where you practice. You cannot promise secrecy when abuse must be reported or when the adolescent is in immediate danger. Emancipation and mature-minor rules exist and are not one number. |

### Language barrier without a qualified interpreter

Consent and teaching fail when the words are filtered through a child or a relative who edits. Ad hoc interpreting is how the wrong surgery is agreed to.

**Vignette.** A 7-year-old is asked to explain a consent for abdominal surgery to her mother, who speaks a language no one on the team speaks well. The resident says it will be faster than calling an interpreter, and he had a semester of the language in college.
**Reasoning.** The decision is high stakes, the child is not a qualified interpreter, and a semester of study is not one either. Speed is the usual excuse and the usual harm. The professional interpreter is the next step before the signature. The diagnosis is Language barrier without a qualified interpreter.
**NBME trap.** The child in the room, or the fastest bilingual bystander, for consent.
**Hook.** Interpreter, not the child, for a medical decision.

### Harmful error that reached the patient

The patient cannot make the next decision without the fact. Hiding a reached error is the ethics failure, separate from whether a lawsuit follows.

**Vignette.** A wrong dose of insulin was given overnight. The patient became hypoglycemic, was treated, and is now eating breakfast and asymptomatic. A colleague says not to mention it because the morning glucose is normal and a disclosure will only upset him.
**Reasoning.** The error reached him and caused hypoglycemia, even though he recovered. He is told what happened, what it means for the rest of the day, and what will change. The record states the facts. Silence because the glucose recovered is the failure. The diagnosis is Harmful error that reached the patient.
**NBME trap.** Hiding an error that reached the patient because the harm was reversed.
**Hook.** If it reached the patient, say what happened and what you will do.

### Absent decision-making capacity

You cannot take a signature from a person who cannot do the four things for this decision. A mini-mental score is not those four things.

**Vignette.** An older man with urosepsis agrees to everything, forgets the operation that was just explained, and cannot say what will happen if he refuses. His daughter is asked to witness a consent he does not understand. The note says he is oriented to person and therefore has capacity.
**Reasoning.** Orientation to person is not capacity. He cannot retain or apply the information, so he lacks capacity for this decision. The surrogate decides, using what he would have wanted. If the infection clears and he can reason, he decides the next question himself. The diagnosis is Absent decision-making capacity.
**NBME trap.** A cognitive score, or a yes to every question, used as proof of capacity.
**Hook.** Capacity is four abilities for this decision, not a point on a form.

### Informed refusal by a capacitated adult

Autonomy includes refusal. The duty is information and non-abandonment, not a forced signature.

**Vignette.** A capacitated adult with a bleeding ulcer refuses transfusion and explains that death is possible and that this refusal is consistent with long-held beliefs. The family demands that you transfuse anyway. He repeats the risk in his own words.
**Reasoning.** He has capacity, the refusal is informed, and the belief is his. The family does not override him. You document, you treat with the options he accepts, and you do not sneak the blood in. A delirious refusal would be a different entry. The diagnosis is Informed refusal by a capacitated adult.
**NBME trap.** Transfusing a capacitated adult in secret because the family insisted.
**Hook.** Capacity plus an informed no is a no.

### Parental refusal of emergency treatment for a child

The child's interest is the duty when the harm is immediate. Parental authority does not include letting a child die of a reversible emergency.

**Vignette.** A child is in hemorrhagic shock after trauma. The parent refuses blood products on religious grounds and asks you to wait. The child is hypotensive and pale. No other parent is available.
**Reasoning.** This is an emergency and the patient is a child. Transfusion proceeds. The conversation with the parent continues, and it does not pause the blood. The same parent could refuse blood for their own capacitated self. That right does not transfer to the child. The diagnosis is Parental refusal of emergency treatment for a child.
**NBME trap.** Withholding emergency blood from a child because a parent refused it.
**Hook.** Emergency care for a child proceeds. The parent's emergency refusal does not.

### A skipped surgical time-out

The time-out exists because capable people still operate on the wrong side when the room is busy. The social discomfort is the point.

**Vignette.** The consent says right knee and the mark is on the left. The attending says the family is waiting and the right side is obvious, and asks for the knife. A student sees the mismatch and stays quiet.
**Reasoning.** The mismatch is the reason the time-out exists. Anyone in the room stops the line, including the student. Late schedules do not pick the side. The case starts again when the patient, the procedure, and the site agree. The diagnosis is A skipped surgical time-out.
**NBME trap.** Staying quiet because of hierarchy.
**Hook.** Stop the line. The time-out beats the schedule.

### An impaired clinician

Impairment is a patient-safety problem. Covering it preserves the harm. Public shaming is not the treatment of the colleague either.

**Vignette.** An attending smells of alcohol and is slurring while consenting the first patient of the day. A resident offers to do the cases and say nothing, so the attending can sleep it off and the schedule survives.
**Reasoning.** He is unsafe today. He does not operate, and the report goes through the real channel so patients are protected and he can be evaluated. Quietly absorbing his list hides the impairment until the next patient is hurt. The diagnosis is An impaired clinician.
**NBME trap.** Covering the shift forever, or a public humiliation in place of a real report.
**Hook.** Impaired and about to touch a patient: relieve them and report.

### Bad news in the hallway

People cannot use information they received while standing in a hall. Plain words and a pause are the delivery.

**Vignette.** A biopsy shows cancer. The physician stops the patient in the hallway, reads the histology line, explains three pathways of metastasis, and walks on because clinic is behind. There is no chair and no pause.
**Reasoning.** The news is true and the delivery fails. Privacy, a warning shot, the word cancer in ordinary language, silence, and one next step are the action. Pathophysiology can follow after the patient has heard the sentence. The diagnosis is Bad news in the hallway.
**NBME trap.** Jargon in the hallway as the whole conversation.
**Hook.** Private, plain words, silence, then a plan.

### A surrogate demanding concealment

The information belongs to the patient who has capacity. Concealment by relatives is a request, not an order.

**Vignette.** A son asks you not to tell his father that the biopsy is cancer, because the news would be too heavy. The father is alert, has capacity, and asks what the biopsy showed.
**Reasoning.** Capacity is present and he is asking. You tell him, in the way bad news is told. The son's love is real and does not own the result. If the father had said he wanted his son to hear it instead, you would follow that. The diagnosis is A surrogate demanding concealment.
**NBME trap.** Promising the family you will lie to a capacitated patient.
**Hook.** If they have capacity and they ask, the information is theirs.

### Discharge without teach-back

People leave having agreed to a plan they cannot repeat. The repeat is the test of the teaching.

**Vignette.** A man is discharged on new insulin and a blood thinner. He nods when asked if he has questions. He cannot say what number would make him call, or which bottle is the evening dose. The ride is at the curb.
**Reasoning.** The nod was courtesy. Teach-back would have shown the gap before the curb. You sit back down, he explains the bottles and the warning signs, and the follow-up appointment is real. A pamphlet in the bag is not that conversation. The diagnosis is Discharge without teach-back.
**NBME trap.** Accepting any questions and a nod as proof of understanding.
**Hook.** Teach-back is the patient talking, not the patient nodding.

### Counseling with relative risk alone

Relative risk inflates small baselines. Patients decide with absolute risk. A significant p value does not pick the number you say out loud.

**Vignette.** A pamphlet says a drug cuts heart attacks in half. The table shows 2 heart attacks per 100 people without the drug and 1 per 100 with it, over the trial's duration. The patient asks if this means half of people like him are saved.
**Reasoning.** Half is the relative reduction from a small baseline. One fewer person per 100 is the fact he needs, and about 100 people take the drug for one to benefit over that period. You do not quote the headline as his personal chance. The diagnosis is Counseling with relative risk alone.
**NBME trap.** Telling the patient the risk fell by half when it fell from 2 per 100 to 1 per 100.
**Hook.** Absolute risk for counseling. Relative risk for headlines.

### A surrogate endpoint sold as a patient outcome

Drugs can move a number and still fail to help a person. The outcome in the methods is the outcome you may claim.

**Vignette.** An ad says a new lipid drug prevents heart attacks. The paper's primary result is a lower LDL cholesterol. Heart attacks, strokes, and deaths were not reported as outcomes. The funder is the manufacturer.
**Reasoning.** The measured result is the lab. You may say the cholesterol fell. You may not say heart attacks were prevented. The funding makes you check which endpoint was chosen. It does not, by itself, erase the cholesterol result or create a clinical one. The diagnosis is A surrogate endpoint sold as a patient outcome.
**NBME trap.** Counseling a lab result as if it were a prevented stroke or death.
**Hook.** A lab is not a stroke unless the trial counted strokes.

### An underpowered negative trial

Precision comes from size and from events. A wide interval means you do not know. A p value is not a measure of how useless the drug is.

**Vignette.** A trial of 40 patients reports p = 0.22 for a new drug against a symptom. The confidence interval runs from a large benefit to a large harm. A colleague says this proves the drug does not work and should never be studied again.
**Reasoning.** The interval includes both a useful drug and a harmful one. That is an unanswered question, not a negative proof. You do not tell a patient the drug was shown to fail. You say this study could not tell. The diagnosis is An underpowered negative trial.
**NBME trap.** Reading a nonsignificant small trial as proof of no effect.
**Hook.** A wide interval is not a verdict. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

### A credible threat to an identifiable person

Confidentiality yields to a specific, credible threat against a named person. It does not yield to ordinary anger, and it does not yield to curiosity.

**Vignette.** A patient says he has a gun in the car and will shoot a named coworker when the shift ends tonight. He describes how he will get in. He asks you to promise not to tell anyone.
**Reasoning.** The person is named, the plan is credible, and the time is now. You act to protect that person and you notify police. The promise of secrecy does not cover this. A statement that people at work are unfair, with no plan and no target, would not be this duty. The diagnosis is A credible threat to an identifiable person.
**NBME trap.** Keeping the secret because confidentiality was promised, or warning the whole city about a vague grudge.
**Hook.** Named person, credible plan: protect them. A vague grudge is not that duty.

### Suspected child maltreatment

The duty runs to the child. Uncertainty about the perpetrator is expected and is not a reason to wait.

**Vignette.** A 4-month-old has a bruise in the shape of a loop. The parent says the baby rolled onto a toy. You are not sure which adult was present. The note says you will mention it if it happens again.
**Reasoning.** A looped bruise in a non-mobile infant is reasonable suspicion. You do not need to know which adult, and you do not wait for a second injury. The report is the action, along with care of the bruise and a survey for other injuries. The diagnosis is Suspected child maltreatment.
**NBME trap.** Waiting for certainty or for the name of the perpetrator before reporting a child.
**Hook.** Suspicion of child abuse is a report. Proof can come later.

### A gift that would change the prescription

The prescription should be explainable without the lunch. Influence is the harm, not the caloric value of the meal.

**Vignette.** A representative offers a paid speaking tour if you start their branded drug for a condition that has an equivalent generic and a guideline recommending the generic class. The dinner is tonight. The samples are already in the closet.
**Reasoning.** The fee is tied to the drug you would name. You decline, and you prescribe from the guideline. A pen that does not touch the decision is not this case. A research grant disclosed at the start of a talk is a different, required transparency. The diagnosis is A gift that would change the prescription.
**NBME trap.** Letting the speaking fee pick the brand, or throwing out a solid trial only because a company paid for it.
**Hook.** The decision is the issue. Decline the influence.

### An advance directive the surrogate wants ignored

The patient's prior capacitated wishes outrank the family's present distress. The document is how those wishes survived the loss of capacity.

**Vignette.** A man with advanced lung disease is unconscious. His directive refuses intubation. A spouse he married last month demands the tube and says the directive is too depressing to follow.
**Reasoning.** Capacity is gone and the directive is clear. You do not intubate. The new spouse is heard, and she does not erase the refusal. Comfort care continues. If there were no directive, she might be the surrogate and would still owe substituted judgment rather than her own preference alone. The diagnosis is An advance directive the surrogate wants ignored.
**NBME trap.** Intubating because a new relative insisted, against a clear directive.
**Hook.** When capacity is gone, the directive still speaks.

### Confidential adolescent care

Adolescents forgo care they think will be reported home. Confidentiality is what makes the care happen, until safety overrides it.

**Vignette.** A 16-year-old asks for contraception and testing for a sexually transmitted infection, and asks you not to tell a parent. She is not describing abuse. A staff member is about to phone the parent with the plan before the visit starts.
**Reasoning.** In the usual exam frame the phone call does not happen, and the care is given. You say you will not promise silence if you learn she is being hurt or is in immediate danger. You do not quote a fake national age as if every state used it. The diagnosis is Confidential adolescent care.
**NBME trap.** Calling a parent about contraception or a sexually transmitted infection as the first move, or promising absolute secrecy when abuse is disclosed.
**Hook.** Sensitive adolescent care stays confidential. Abuse and immediate danger do not.

## Common traps

- A family member interpreting because it is faster. It is also how consent fails.
- Quoting a relative risk reduction of 50% when the absolute reduction is a fraction of a percent.
- Reporting a colleague to punish them. The standard is impairment and patient risk, through a real channel.
- Treating a practice-based-learning item as “read more.” The answer is often an audit, a protocol, or a specific question you look up.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Do these items in mixed blocks so you do not spot them by the chapter. When you miss one, name the person who was harmed or about to be. Feed cards on ethics that are slogans (“autonomy always”) are too crude. Autonomy loses to a lack of capacity and to a specific duty to protect a named third party. The card should say the exception.
