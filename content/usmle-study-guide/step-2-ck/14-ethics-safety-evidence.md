# Ethics, patient safety, and evidence

> **Study aid only.** The published social-science range on Step 2 CK is about 10–15%. That is larger than several organs. This is not legal advice for your state.

## What this chapter covers

The safety behaviors the exam treats as answers, the ethics sequence from Step 1 applied to a supervised physician, and the evidence skills that read a paper in the stem.

## The framework

Safety items have a correct action that feels socially awkward. Do it anyway.

| Situation | The action |
| --- | --- |
| You are about to make a wrong-site or wrong-patient error | Stop the line. A time-out exists for this. Hierarchy is not a contraindication. |
| A colleague is impaired | Report through the path your institution uses. Covering the shift forever is not the treatment. |
| A patient does not speak the language | A professional interpreter. Not the child in the room. Not your semester of the language. |
| Bad news | A private setting, a warning, the news in plain words, silence, then a plan. Do not start with a pile of pathophysiology. |
| An error reached the patient | Tell them what happened, what it means, and what you will do. Hiding it is the ethics failure. |
| A surrogate asks you not to tell the patient | Capacity first. If the patient has capacity, the information is theirs unless they have asked not to know. |
| A gift or a drug-rep meal would change the drug you name | Decline the influence. A cheap pen is not the issue. The decision is. |
| Discharge | Teach-back, the medicines reconciled, and a follow-up that exists. “Any questions?” is not teach-back. |

Evidence items in a stem are abstracts and ads. Read the outcome first. A surrogate marker (a lab) is not a patient-important outcome (a stroke, a death, a hospitalization) unless the question is about the lab. Absolute risk matters more than relative risk when you counsel. A significant p value with a tiny effect in a huge trial may not be a reason to treat. A nonsignificant p value in a tiny trial is not proof of no effect.

Conflict of interest in a paper is a reason to read harder, not an automatic reason to discard a well-run trial. Funding does not invent a confidence interval. It does change how you look at the outcome the authors chose.

## Checklist

- [ ] Interpreter, not a family member, for a medical decision.
- [ ] Tell patients about errors that affect them.
- [ ] Time-out beats hierarchy.
- [ ] Absolute risk for counseling. Relative risk for headlines.
- [ ] Teach-back on the discharge medicines.

## Common traps

- A family member interpreting because it is faster. It is also how consent fails.
- Quoting a relative risk reduction of 50% when the absolute reduction is a fraction of a percent.
- Reporting a colleague to punish them. The standard is impairment and patient risk, through a real channel.
- Treating a practice-based-learning item as “read more.” The answer is often an audit, a protocol, or a specific question you look up.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Do these items in mixed blocks so you do not spot them by the chapter. When you miss one, name the person who was harmed or about to be. Feed cards on ethics that are slogans (“autonomy always”) are too crude. Autonomy loses to a lack of capacity and to a specific duty to protect a named third party. The card should say the exception.
