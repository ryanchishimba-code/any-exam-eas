# Hematology and oncology

> **Study aid only.** The emergency and the screening decision. A regimen belongs to a current NCCN or ASH document and to the oncologist who will give it.

## What this chapter covers

Transfusion triggers, clotting emergencies, and the cancer presentations that cannot wait for a routine clinic. Blood and immune content is about 5–10% of Step 2 CK.

## The framework

Bleeding and clotting items are “which limb of hemostasis, and is this an emergency.” Mucosal bleeding is platelets or von Willebrand factor. Deep bleeding is factors. A new clot plus a low platelet count and a recent hospitalization is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia until you have another story: stop all heparin, including flushes, and do not start warfarin alone.

| Emergency | The move |
| --- | --- |
| Suspected TTP | Microangiopathic hemolysis, low platelets, neurologic or renal injury. Plasma exchange is the treatment. Platelet transfusion is not the treatment and can make it worse. Do not wait for ADAMTS13 to start the call. |
| Neutropenic fever | Prompt antipseudomonal antibiotics. See the infectious disease chapter. |
| Spinal cord compression from cancer | Steroid, MRI, and a same-day radiation or surgical plan. Back pain in a person with known cancer is this until imaging says no. |
| Hypercalcemia of malignancy | Fluids. A bisphosphonate or denosumab for the ongoing release. The calcitonin is a bridge, not a plan. |
| Tumor lysis | Hydration, uric acid control, and a watch on potassium, phosphate, and calcium. Start the prevention before the chemotherapy when the tumor is bulky and sensitive. |
| Febrile non-hemolytic versus acute hemolytic transfusion reaction | Stop the transfusion for any serious reaction. Hemolytic reactions are clerical until proven otherwise. Check the identity at the bedside. |
| Massive pulmonary embolism or limb-threatening clot | Thrombolysis or a procedure for the unstable patient. Anticoagulation alone for the stable one. |

Anemia in a hospitalized patient is often not a vitamin. It is bleeding, marrow suppression, renal disease, or inflammation. Transfuse for symptoms and for coronary disease at a higher threshold than you would use for a stable young adult. The AABB’s restrictive strategy is the default for the stable patient. It is not the default for the person in shock.

Anticoagulation choice is indication plus kidney plus bleeding risk. Do not stack an antiplatelet and an anticoagulant without a reason you can say out loud (a recent coronary stent plus a clot, for example). When the reason expires, drop the extra drug.

Cancer screening that belongs in this neighborhood: colorectal cancer screening for adults 45 to 75 (USPSTF; grade A from 50 to 75, grade B from 45 to 49), and lung cancer screening as in the pulmonary chapter. Breast and cervix are in the women’s health chapter so they are next to the exam that finds them. Cervical screening is not “oncology clinic.” It is primary care.

## Checklist

- [ ] Suspected TTP gets plasma exchange, not platelets as the plan.
- [ ] Cancer plus new back pain gets an MRI, not a clinic appointment next month.
- [ ] Stop the blood for a serious reaction. Then investigate.
- [ ] Restrictive transfusion for the stable patient. Earlier for shock or ischemia.
- [ ] Colorectal screening starts at 45 in the current USPSTF recommendation.

## Common traps

- Platelets for TTP or for HIT as a reflex because the count is low.
- Warfarin alone in acute HIT. Protein C falls and the clot extends.
- A single fecal occult test as “screening” in place of a program. USPSTF names strategies. A random smear of a glove is not one of them.
- Memorizing a chemotherapy schedule. The exam wants the emergency and the class toxicity, not the cycle length.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Heme items: name the emergency in one line before the drug. Oncology items: cord, calcium, lysis, fever. Feed cards on transfusion numbers should say “stable patient.” A card that says “transfuse under 10” for everyone is not the AABB strategy.
