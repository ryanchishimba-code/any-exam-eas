# Emergency and perioperative care

> **Study aid only.** Surgery is about 5–15% of Step 2 CK as a clinical science, and it punches above that weight because the question is “operate, now or never.” Perioperative medical care is an ACC/AHA and an anesthesia decision.

## What this chapter covers

The abdominal and traumatic emergencies, and the medicine you do before the operating room so the surgeon is not surprised.

## The framework

A surgical abdomen is peritonitis, ischemia, or obstruction with a consequence. Pain out of proportion with a quiet abdomen is mesenteric ischemia until proven otherwise. Free air is a perforated hollow viscus. A pulsatile mass and hypotension is a ruptured aneurysm on the way to the operating room, not to the CT scanner, if the person is unstable. A stable person with a suspected aneurysm can be imaged.

Trauma is the ATLS sequence: airway with cervical spine protection, breathing, circulation and bleeding, disability, exposure. A tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis. You do not wait for a film to decompress a crashing patient. Massive transfusion is a protocol, not a bag of red cells alone. Stop the bleed.

| Situation | The fork |
| --- | --- |
| Appendicitis | Score, image when the diagnosis is not clear (CT in many adults, ultrasound first in many children and in pregnancy), then surgery. Antibiotics are part of care, not a substitute, in the usual non-operative debate the stem will specify. |
| Cholecystitis and cholangitis | See the gastrointestinal chapter. Cholangitis is drainage. |
| Small-bowel obstruction | Many partial obstructions from adhesions settle with decompression. Complete obstruction, hernia at the gate, or peritonitis does not. |
| Testicular torsion | Do not wait for imaging if the story and exam are classic. Minutes matter for the testis. |
| Ectopic pregnancy, unstable | Surgery. See the obstetrics chapter. |
| Preoperative cardiac risk | The ACC/AHA perioperative guideline: emergency surgery does not wait for a stress test. For elective surgery, functional status and active cardiac conditions decide who needs more than an ECG. A revascularization “to get them through it” is not the plan when they would not need that revascularization on its own merits. |
| Medications | Hold some drugs, continue others. Antihypertensives are often continued, with care around ACE inhibitors. Antiplatelet therapy around a recent stent is a cardiology decision. Do not stop it because the form says “hold blood thinners.” |
| Postoperative fever | The timing suggests the cause. Immediate is the transfusion, the drug, or malignant hyperthermia. A day later is atelectasis versus a wind, water, wound, walking (clot), and wonder-drug list you should use as a prompt, not as a diagnosis. |
| Malignant hyperthermia | A trigger anesthetic, rigidity, a rising CO2. Stop the agent. Dantrolene. This is not “a bit of a fever.” |

Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis is the default after many operations unless bleeding risk forbids it. The caprini-style judgment is the hospital’s protocol. The exam wants you to remember that the clot is a postoperative disease, not an accident.

## Checklist

- [ ] Unstable ruptured aneurysm and tension pneumothorax do not go to radiology first.
- [ ] Pain out of proportion is ischemia.
- [ ] Torsion is a clinical emergency.
- [ ] Do not revascularize a heart only to “clear” someone for an elective hernia.
- [ ] Dantrolene for malignant hyperthermia.

## Common traps

- A CT of the abdomen in a patient too unstable to lie flat.
- Holding a statin or a beta blocker out of superstition. Most of these continue.
- Antibiotics as the treatment for an undrained abscess or a dead gallbladder.
- The five W’s as a substitute for looking at the wound and the lungs.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Surgical items: “operating room now, operating room today, or not an operation.” Perioperative items: “which medicine do you hold, and which test would change nothing.” Feed cards that say “type and cross everyone” are waste. The card should name the unstable pattern.
