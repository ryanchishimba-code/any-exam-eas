# Emergency and perioperative care

> **Study aid only.** Surgery is about 5–15% of Step 2 CK as a clinical science, and it punches above that weight because the question is “operate, now or never.” Perioperative medical care is an ACC/AHA and an anesthesia decision.

## What this chapter covers

The abdominal and traumatic emergencies, and the medicine you do before the operating room so the surgeon is not surprised.

## The framework

A surgical abdomen is peritonitis, ischemia, or obstruction with a consequence. Pain out of proportion with a quiet abdomen is mesenteric ischemia until proven otherwise. Free air is a perforated hollow viscus. A pulsatile mass and hypotension is a ruptured aneurysm on the way to the operating room, not to the CT scanner, if the person is unstable. A stable person with a suspected aneurysm can be imaged.

Trauma is the ATLS sequence: airway with cervical spine protection, breathing, circulation and bleeding, disability, exposure. A tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis. You do not wait for a film to decompress a crashing patient. Massive transfusion is a protocol, not a bag of red cells alone. Stop the bleed.

| Situation | The fork |
| --- | --- |
| Appendicitis | Score, image when the diagnosis is not clear (CT in many adults, ultrasound first in many children and in pregnancy), then surgery. Antibiotics are part of care, not a substitute, in the usual non-operative debate the stem will specify. |
| Cholecystitis and cholangitis | See the gastrointestinal chapter. Cholangitis is drainage. |
| Small-bowel obstruction | Many partial obstructions from adhesions settle with decompression. Complete obstruction, hernia at the gate, or peritonitis does not. |
| Testicular torsion | Do not wait for imaging if the story and exam are classic. Minutes matter for the testis. |
| Ectopic pregnancy, unstable | Surgery. See the obstetrics chapter. |
| Preoperative cardiac risk | The ACC/AHA perioperative guideline: emergency surgery does not wait for a stress test. For elective surgery, functional status and active cardiac conditions decide who needs more than an ECG. A revascularization “to get them through it” is not the plan when they would not need that revascularization on its own merits. |
| Medications | Hold some drugs, continue others. Antihypertensives are often continued, with care around ACE inhibitors. Antiplatelet therapy around a recent stent is a cardiology decision. Do not stop it because the form says “hold blood thinners.” |
| Postoperative fever | The timing suggests the cause. Immediate is the transfusion, the drug, or malignant hyperthermia. A day later is atelectasis versus a wind, water, wound, walking (clot), and wonder-drug list you should use as a prompt, not as a diagnosis. |
| Malignant hyperthermia | A trigger anesthetic, rigidity, a rising CO2. Stop the agent. Dantrolene. This is not “a bit of a fever.” |

Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis is the default after many operations unless bleeding risk forbids it. The caprini-style judgment is the hospital’s protocol. The exam wants you to remember that the clot is a postoperative disease, not an accident.

## High-yield atlas

Emergency items ask whether this patient goes to the operating room now, later today, or not at all. The primary survey and a tension pneumothorax do not wait on a film. Pain out of proportion with a quiet abdomen is mesenteric ischemia. Perioperative items ask which test would change nothing. Do not revascularize a coronary artery solely to clear a low-risk elective operation, and do not start a beta blocker at the door of the operating room.

Peritonitis, a fecalith, or a child is not that debate unless the stem builds it. Do not invent an antibiotic regimen. Scoring systems do not replace the exam or the pregnancy test. |

Do not observe a strangulated hernia or a peritonitic abdomen. CT shows a transition point and signs of ischemia. Do not invent a fluid quota. |

Do not wait for Doppler. If imaging is already done and flow is absent, that confirms. A normal flow does not erase a classic exam. | Urologic emergency. Minutes to a few hours matter. Explore and fix both sides, because the anatomic predisposition is often bilateral. Manual detorsion is a bridge, not the cure. Do not invent an analgesic plan in place of the operation. |

Call for help. | Malignant Hyperthermia Association of the United States protocol, and standard anesthesia emergency care. Dantrolene is the antidote. Do not quote a milligram. This is not a bit of a fever. A postoperative fever the night after surgery is a different list: atelectasis, a drug, a transfusion, and later the wound, the urine, the lung, and a clot. |
| Trauma, hypotension, cool skin, and a source you can see or compress: a limb, an open pelvis, or the chest or abdomen. | Stop the bleed. Balanced transfusion, not a cooler of red cells alone and not a crystalloid flood. Operating room or angioembolization for the source. A binder for an open-book pelvis. A tourniquet for a limb you cannot compress. | ATLS and trauma-resuscitation practice. Plasma and platelets go with the red cells. Replace calcium and heat as the protocol directs. An antifibrinolytic, if your trauma protocol uses one, is given early, not the next morning. Permissive low pressure is not the strategy in traumatic brain injury. Do not invent a ratio, a milliliter quota, or a Parkland volume. |
| Fever, jaundice, and right-upper-quadrant pain. Hypotension or confusion means the severe form. | Resuscitate, draw cultures, start antibiotics that cover gram-negative rods and anaerobes, and drain the duct. Drainage is usually endoscopic. | Tokyo guidelines. Antibiotics without drainage do not finish the job. Cholangitis is a duct emergency. Cholecystitis is the gallbladder and is not the same night. Confirm the antibiotic. Do not invent a dose. A mildly abnormal ultrasound without fever or jaundice is not this disease. |
| Sudden severe abdominal pain, a rigid abdomen, and free air under the diaphragm or on CT. The patient is sick. | Resuscitate, antibiotics, and the operating room. Not a bowel prep. Not oral contrast as the next step. | Acute-care surgery standard. Diffuse peritonitis and free air in an unstable or rigid patient is an operation. A contained perforation in a stable patient, such as some diverticular perforations, is a different conversation the stem has to earn. Do not invent an antibiotic menu. An ulcer, a diverticulum, and a cancer can all be the hole. The operation finds out. |
| Elective surgery is planned, and a coronary stent was placed recently. The preop form says to hold aspirin and the P2Y12 inhibitor. | Do not stop both antiplatelet drugs because a form says to hold blood thinners. Involve cardiology and the surgeon. After balloon angioplasty without a stent, delay elective surgery at least 14 days. Continue aspirin when possible. Confirm the current table. Do not invent a dose. |

Hold the SGLT2 inhibitor for 3 to 4 days before elective surgery. | 2024 AHA/ACC perioperative guideline. A new beta blocker, if there is a real indication, is started far enough ahead to judge tolerance, optimally more than 7 days, not on the morning of surgery. Day-of initiation increased death in the trials that tested it. Ertugliflozin is the SGLT2 inhibitor with the longer hold. Confirm the current list. Do not invent a dose. |
| Blunt trauma, hypotension, and a bedside ultrasound that shows free fluid. Or hypotension and a pelvis that is open. | Operating room for free fluid and shock. A binder for the open pelvis. Not a CT. A negative ultrasound does not clear the retroperitoneum. | ATLS. The focused assessment finds intraperitoneal blood in the unstable patient and sends that patient to laparotomy. Stable patients are imaged with CT, which also sees the retroperitoneum the bedside study misses. Do not repeat a negative ultrasound as false comfort in a patient who remains unstable. Do not invent a transfusion threshold. |
| A patient after major abdominal surgery or hip or knee arthroplasty. The only clot plan is to walk today. Bleeding risk has not been named as a reason to withhold a drug. | Pharmacologic prophylaxis unless bleeding risk forbids it. Walking is an addition, not the whole plan. | CHEST and ASH venous thromboembolism guidance, and the hospital's protocol. A Caprini-style judgment lives in that protocol. A stable postoperative pulmonary embolus is anticoagulation. A hypotensive embolus is a reperfusion emergency. Do not invent a dose. Do not start prophylaxis by giving a treatment dose to a patient who is not clotting. |

Rupture and instability are surgery. A discriminatory hormone level is a tool for the stable patient with an uncertain ultrasound, not a reason to delay the operating room. Do not invent that threshold. Rh status and immune globulin are part of the pathway. Confirm the current bulletin. |

Motor loss means the limb is threatened. | Immediate vascular surgery. Anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin while they arrive, unless bleeding forbids it. Thrombosis in place has a claudication history. A paralyzed, rigid, mottled limb may be irreversibly ischemic, and revascularizing dead muscle can kill with reperfusion. That is an amputation discussion with the surgeon in the room. Do not invent a heparin bolus. After flow is restored, watch for compartment syndrome even if the pulse returns. |

Aortic dissection is tearing pain and unequal pulses and is a different operation. Screening is one ultrasound in a well man aged 65 to 75 who has smoked. |

| Testicular torsion versus the neighbor | Epididymitis is more gradual, often with urinary symptoms or fever, in a sexually active or older patient, and it gets antibiotics and scrotal support rather than an immediate exploration. Torsion of the appendix testis is a tender blue-dot spot and a less sick patient. Neither cancels a classic twist. |
| Malignant hyperthermia versus the neighbor | Neuroleptic malignant syndrome evolves over days on an antipsychotic and is not an intraoperative carbon dioxide problem. Serotonin toxicity has a drug history and clonus. A transfusion reaction is a fever and a pressure change without rigidity from a volatile agent. Ordinary postoperative atelectasis is the next night, not this minute. |
| Hemorrhagic shock versus the neighbor | Distributive shock is warm, with a low diastolic pressure, after a source of infection or a cord injury, and the bleeding exam is absent. Cardiogenic shock is cold and wet with full neck veins. Tension pneumothorax shares the hypotension and has a silent chest. |
| Acute cholangitis versus the neighbor | Biliary colic has pain without fever or jaundice. Cholecystitis is gallbladder inflammation and is a surgical plan on a different clock. Choledocholithiasis without infection is a stone in the duct and is not yet cholangitis. Pancreatitis shares the region and has a lipase. |
| Perforated hollow viscus versus the neighbor | Pancreatitis can be severe and has a lipase and no free air as the headline. Mesenteric ischemia is pain out of proportion with a soft abdomen early. Obstruction distends without free air unless the bowel has already died and perforated. |

| Unstable blunt abdominal bleeding versus the neighbor | A stable patient with abdominal tenderness goes to CT, not directly to a laparotomy, unless the exam is frankly peritoneal. A ruptured ectopic pregnancy is free fluid and shock in a patient with a positive pregnancy test and no trauma. Tension pneumothorax is a chest diagnosis. |
| Missing prophylaxis after major surgery versus the neighbor | A patient who is actively bleeding does not get a prophylactic anticoagulant, and mechanical devices may be what you can do. A known clot gets treatment, not a prophylactic dose. Fat embolism after a long-bone fracture is a petechial rash and hypoxemia and is not treated as a venous clot. |
| Unstable ectopic pregnancy versus the neighbor | A stable patient with a positive test, pain, and an uncertain ultrasound needs a quantitative level and close follow-up, and may be a methotrexate candidate if criteria are met. Appendicitis shares the quadrant and has a negative pregnancy test. A miscarriage with an open cervix and stable pressure is not this operation. |
| Acute limb ischemia versus the neighbor | Compartment syndrome keeps a pulse until late and hurts on passive stretch. Chronic limb-threatening ischemia is rest pain and a wound on a background of claudication, still urgent, and not a sudden embolus. A deep vein thrombosis swells a leg that still has pulses. |

### An unfinished primary survey

The primary survey finds what kills in minutes, in order. A complete story is the secondary survey. Hemorrhage is a circulation problem you can see and stop.

**Vignette.** A driver is pulled from a car, hypotensive, with an obviously deformed pelvis and a bleeding thigh. The cervical collar is not on. A pan-scan is requested before anyone holds the airway, binds the pelvis, or puts pressure on the thigh.
**Reasoning.** The sequence is the treatment. Airway and spine protection, then breathing, then bleeding, which here is a binder and pressure, then a look at disability and a warm exposure. The scan is for a patient whose blood pressure allows it. Red cells alone are not a transfusion protocol. The diagnosis is An unfinished primary survey.
**NBME trap.** A pan-scan, or a long history, before airway and hemorrhage control.
**Hook.** ATLS order: airway and spine, breathing, bleeding, disability, exposure.

### Tension pneumothorax

Air under pressure collapses the lung and kinks venous return. The mediastinum shifts. Waiting for a film waits through the arrest.

**Vignette.** After placement of a subclavian line, a patient becomes hypotensive. Breath sounds are gone on that side, the neck veins are up, and the trachea is shifting away. A portable chest film has been ordered and people are waiting for it.
**Reasoning.** The silent side, the pressure, and the shift are enough. You decompress that side now. The film can happen after the chest tube, or not at all if he is about to arrest. The other side is the side you do not needle. The diagnosis is Tension pneumothorax.
**NBME trap.** A chest radiograph before decompression in a crashing patient.
**Hook.** Silent side, shock, shift: decompress. Do not wait for the film.

### Acute mesenteric ischemia

The gut is ischemic before the peritoneum is irritated, so the pain is fierce and the exam is quiet. Peritonitis means the wall may already be dead.

**Vignette.** A woman with atrial fibrillation not on anticoagulation has sudden unbearable abdominal pain. The abdomen is soft and not tender. Lactate is only slightly up. A laxative and a discharge are proposed because the exam is benign.
**Reasoning.** Pain out of proportion, a soft abdomen, and atrial fibrillation are an embolus to the mesenteric artery until angiography says otherwise. A normal-feeling abdomen is the classic trap. Lactate is a late consolation, not a clearance. She needs imaging of the vessels and a surgeon. The diagnosis is Acute mesenteric ischemia.
**NBME trap.** Discharge, a laxative, or reassurance because the abdomen is soft.
**Hook.** Pain out of proportion, quiet abdomen: mesenteric ischemia until proven otherwise.

### Revascularization only to clear elective surgery

A stent done only to get through a hernia adds antiplatelet risk and delay, and it does not make a low-risk operation safer. The coronaries are treated on their own indications.

**Vignette.** A 68-year-old man needs an elective inguinal hernia repair. He climbs two flights without chest pain. A stress test was obtained anyway and shows a moderate stenosis that is not left main. A stent is proposed so he can be cleared for the hernia.
**Reasoning.** The operation is low-risk and elective, his capacity is adequate, and the lesion would not earn a stent on its own today. Clearing him is not an indication. If he had rest pain and ST depression, elective surgery would wait and the coronaries would be treated as an acute coronary syndrome. Left main disease is the other exception the guideline treats separately. The diagnosis is Revascularization only to clear elective surgery.
**NBME trap.** A coronary stent whose only purpose is to clear a low-risk elective case.
**Hook.** Do not revascularize a heart only to get someone through an elective hernia.

### Unstable ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm

The aneurysm has ruptured into the retroperitoneum or the peritoneum. The hematoma may be temporarily contained. The trip to CT is how that tamponade is lost in a hallway.

**Vignette.** A 72-year-old man who smoked has sudden back pain and a blood pressure of 80. A pulsatile mass is felt. He is pale and restless. CT is requested to confirm the leak before the surgeon is called.
**Reasoning.** The triad in a crashing patient is the diagnosis. He goes to the operating room while blood is prepared. A confirmatory scan is for the patient whose pressure allows a scan. Calling it a kidney stone because the pain is in the back is the other miss. The diagnosis is Unstable ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm.
**NBME trap.** A CT scan in a patient too unstable to lie flat.
**Hook.** Pulsatile mass, back pain, hypotension: the operating room, not the scanner.

### Appendicitis

The lumen is obstructed, the wall is infected, and the process marches toward perforation. The differential in a person who can be pregnant includes ectopic pregnancy.

**Vignette.** A 28-year-old woman has a day of pain that moved to the right lower quadrant, with fever and focal tenderness. No pregnancy test has been sent. A CT is ordered, and someone mentions skipping surgery if she gets antibiotics.
**Reasoning.** The story fits appendicitis, and a pregnancy test comes before the radiation because an ectopic pregnancy shares the quadrant. Ultrasound is preferred if she is pregnant. Antibiotics are not the plan for a peritoneal abdomen. A selected, uncomplicated, nonpregnant adult is the only place that debate even starts. The diagnosis is Appendicitis.
**NBME trap.** A CT in a child or a pregnant patient as the first image, or antibiotics as a substitute for surgery when the abdomen is peritoneal.
**Hook.** Pregnancy test, then the image that fits the patient, then surgery for the usual case.

### Complicated small-bowel obstruction

The bowel is blocked. Adhesions are the common cause after surgery. A hernia that is the transition point is a mechanical emergency. Ischemia is the consequence you cannot watch.

**Vignette.** A woman with a midline scar is vomiting and distended. The groin has a firm, tender, irreducible bulge. Lactate is rising and the abdomen is guarding. The plan is a nasogastric tube and a recheck in the morning.
**Reasoning.** The scar suggests adhesions, and the irreducible tender groin says this will not settle on a tube. Rising lactate and guarding are ischemia. She goes to the operating room. A partial obstruction with a soft abdomen and a falling nasogastric output is the patient you can watch. The diagnosis is Complicated small-bowel obstruction.
**NBME trap.** Morning rounds on a strangulated hernia or a peritonitic obstruction.
**Hook.** Partial adhesions can wait. A hernia, peritonitis, or dead bowel cannot.

### Testicular torsion

The cord has twisted and the testis is ischemic. Nausea is part of the story. The cremasteric reflex is usually gone.

**Vignette.** A 15-year-old boy has an hour of sudden left testicular pain and vomiting. The testis sits high and transverse. Stroking the thigh does not elevate it. Doppler is the next available slot in two hours. He is given an analgesic and a waiting-room chair.
**Reasoning.** The clock and the exam are enough. Two hours in a chair is testis lost. He goes to the operating room, and the other side is fixed too. Doppler is for the unclear exam, and even then a classic exam outranks a reassuring machine. The diagnosis is Testicular torsion.
**NBME trap.** Waiting on ultrasound when the exam is already classic.
**Hook.** High, transverse, no cremasteric reflex: the operating room.

### Malignant hyperthermia

Uncontrolled skeletal-muscle calcium release. Carbon dioxide production is the early sign. The temperature is not required to start treatment.

**Vignette.** Ten minutes after induction with a volatile agent, the end-tidal carbon dioxide climbs despite increased ventilation. The jaw is rigid. The temperature is only slightly up. Someone suggests acetaminophen and a note to watch it.
**Reasoning.** The trigger, the carbon dioxide, and the rigidity are the crisis. You stop the vapor, give oxygen, and give dantrolene while you cool and treat potassium. Waiting for a high fever waits too long. Acetaminophen does not close the calcium channel. The diagnosis is Malignant hyperthermia.
**NBME trap.** Acetaminophen and observation for rigidity and a rising end-tidal carbon dioxide.
**Hook.** Trigger, rising carbon dioxide, rigidity: stop the agent, give dantrolene.

### Hemorrhagic shock

Oxygen delivery failed because the blood left. Crystalloid does not carry oxygen and dilutes what remains. The source has to stop.

**Vignette.** A patient with a gunshot wound to the abdomen is pale, hypotensive, and agitated. A large crystalloid infusion is in, no blood has been given, and CT is next because the abdomen is the question. The pelvis is unstable and unbound.
**Reasoning.** This is hemorrhage. Blood in a balanced protocol, a binder if the pelvis is open, and the operating room. More crystalloid and a scanner delay the source control. Brain injury, if present, changes the pressure you accept. It does not add a CT before the bleeding stops. The diagnosis is Hemorrhagic shock.
**NBME trap.** A crystalloid flood and a CT scan while the source is still open.
**Hook.** Stop the bleed. Blood, not a cooler of saline.

### Acute cholangitis

A stone, a stricture, or a blocked stent plus bacteria in the biliary tree. Pressure in the duct seeds the blood. The source control is drainage, not a longer course of the same drug.

**Vignette.** A man with a prior gallstone admission has fever, jaundice, and right-upper-quadrant pain, and his blood pressure is falling. Antibiotics are started. The plan is to reassess the duct in a day or two if the fever persists. No one has called for drainage.
**Reasoning.** The triad plus shock is the severe form. Antibiotics and fluid are the start. The duct has to be drained, usually by ERCP, not observed. Removing the gallbladder does not decompress an obstructed common duct tonight. The diagnosis is Acute cholangitis.
**NBME trap.** Antibiotics alone, or a cholecystectomy, as the treatment of a pus-filled duct.
**Hook.** Fever, jaundice, right-upper-quadrant pain: drain the duct.

### Perforated hollow viscus

Gut contents are in the peritoneum. Free air is the clue. A rigid abdomen is the exam. Delay is sepsis.

**Vignette.** A man has sudden epigastric pain that became whole-abdomen pain. He is rigid and febrile. An upright film shows air under the diaphragm. The plan is a bowel prep for a colonoscopy in the morning and a surgical clinic referral.
**Reasoning.** Free air and a rigid abdomen are a hole in a hollow organ. He is resuscitated and operated. Colonoscopy and a prep are how the contamination gets worse. A stable patient with a walled-off diverticular perforation is not this film and not this exam. The diagnosis is Perforated hollow viscus.
**NBME trap.** A bowel prep, or a clinic referral, for free air and peritonitis.
**Hook.** Free air and a rigid abdomen: the operating room.

### A recent coronary stent

The stent has not yet been covered by endothelium. Stopping both drugs in that window is how the stent thromboses. Bleeding is real and is balanced, not solved by a checkbox.

**Vignette.** Three weeks after a drug-eluting stent for a heart attack, a patient is scheduled for an elective knee replacement. The form says to hold aspirin and clopidogrel starting now. No cardiologist has been asked.
**Reasoning.** Three weeks is inside the window where interrupting an antiplatelet for elective surgery is potentially harmful, and the stent was for an acute coronary syndrome, which wants the longer delay. You do not hold both drugs for a form. The knee waits, or the antiplatelet plan is made by cardiology and the surgeon together if the operation truly cannot. The diagnosis is A recent coronary stent.
**NBME trap.** Holding both antiplatelet drugs for an elective case a few weeks after a stent.
**Hook.** A recent stent is not a box that says hold blood thinners.

### A beta blocker started the morning of surgery

A new beta blocker started at full dose in a fasting, vasodilated patient causes stroke and death more than it prevents infarction. A chronic beta blocker that is stopped invites rebound. An SGLT2 inhibitor invites perioperative ketoacidosis even when the glucose is not high.

**Vignette.** A man who has never taken a beta blocker is scheduled for a colectomy today. His risk score is elevated. A high dose is ordered with the preop sips. He also took his SGLT2 inhibitor this morning. His statin was held because it is not an anesthetic.
**Reasoning.** The new beta blocker does not start today. If he had been on one for months, he would take it. The statin continues. The SGLT2 inhibitor should have been held for several days, and this morning's dose is a reason to tell anesthesia, not a reason to add a second new cardiac drug. The diagnosis is A beta blocker started the morning of surgery.
**NBME trap.** A new beta blocker with the morning sip, or holding a chronic beta blocker and a statin out of superstition.
**Hook.** Continue the beta blocker he already takes. Do not start one at the door.

### Unstable blunt abdominal bleeding

Intraperitoneal blood in a hypotensive patient is a source you can stop in the operating room. Retroperitoneal and pelvic bleeding can kill with a negative abdominal ultrasound.

**Vignette.** A pedestrian is hypotensive after being struck. A bedside ultrasound shows fluid in the hepatorenal recess. CT is next on the board because the team wants a grade of the liver injury before calling the operating room.
**Reasoning.** Free fluid and shock are the indication for laparotomy. The grade of the laceration does not change the next room. If the ultrasound were negative and he stayed unstable, you would still not relax: the pelvis and the retroperitoneum are not cleared by that probe. The diagnosis is Unstable blunt abdominal bleeding.
**NBME trap.** CT grading of a liver laceration in a hypotensive patient with free fluid.
**Hook.** Unstable and fluid on the bedside ultrasound: laparotomy.

### Missing prophylaxis after major surgery

Surgery is a provoked venous thromboembolism waiting to happen. Early ambulation reduces the risk and does not replace a drug after major orthopedic or abdominal operations.

**Vignette.** The morning after a hip replacement, the order set has no anticoagulant. The note says he will walk, so prophylaxis is unnecessary. The calf is not swollen and he is comfortable.
**Reasoning.** Major orthopedic surgery is a standard indication for pharmacologic prophylaxis. Walking is encouraged and is not the substitute. You add the drug the protocol names unless he is bleeding. A swollen calf later is a diagnostic problem. Comfort on day one does not mean the risk was imaginary. The diagnosis is Missing prophylaxis after major surgery.
**NBME trap.** Ambulation alone after hip replacement, or a thrombolytic for a stable postoperative embolus.
**Hook.** Major surgery makes clots. Walking does not replace the drug.

### Unstable ectopic pregnancy

A pregnancy outside the uterus has ruptured or is bleeding. The abdomen is the source. An empty uterus on a bedside study in a hypotensive pregnant patient is enough.

**Vignette.** A woman of reproductive age has sudden lower abdominal pain and a blood pressure of 78. A urine pregnancy test is positive. Bedside ultrasound shows an empty uterus and free fluid. A quantitative hormone level and a CT are ordered before surgery is called.
**Reasoning.** She is unstable, pregnant, and empty in the uterus, with fluid. That is a ruptured ectopic pregnancy until the operation says otherwise. The hormone number will not rise fast enough to help, and CT delays the room. Methotrexate is for a different, stable patient. The diagnosis is Unstable ectopic pregnancy.
**NBME trap.** CT, a hormone level, or methotrexate in a hypotensive pregnant patient.
**Hook.** Pregnant, hypotensive, empty uterus: surgery.

### Acute limb ischemia

The limb is not perfused. Sensory loss and weakness are muscle and nerve dying. A pulse that is present is a different disease. Pain out of proportion with a pulse can be a compartment.

**Vignette.** A woman with atrial fibrillation has a suddenly cold, painful right foot. The foot is pale, she cannot feel the toes, and she can still wiggle them a little. The dorsalis pedis pulse is absent. Heparin has not been given. The admission plan is a Doppler in the morning.
**Reasoning.** Sudden ischemia in atrial fibrillation is an embolus. She still moves the toes, so the limb is threatened and salvageable, and the clock is now. Heparin and the vascular team, not a morning Doppler. If the calf were rigid and the ankle paralyzed, the conversation would include amputation rather than a triumphant revascularization of dead muscle. The diagnosis is Acute limb ischemia.
**NBME trap.** A morning vascular study for a pale pulseless foot, or reopening a rigid dead limb without a surgeon's plan for dead muscle.
**Hook.** Pale, cold, pulseless: heparin and vascular surgery now.

## Common traps

- A CT of the abdomen in a patient too unstable to lie flat.
- Holding a statin or a beta blocker out of superstition. Most of these continue.
- Antibiotics as the treatment for an undrained abscess or a dead gallbladder.
- The five W’s as a substitute for looking at the wound and the lungs.
