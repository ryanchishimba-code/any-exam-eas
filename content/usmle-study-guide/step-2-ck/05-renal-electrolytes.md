# Renal and electrolyte disorders

> **Study aid only.** The decision, not a dialysis prescription. KDIGO is the kidney source. The 2025 blood-pressure guideline matters when CKD and hypertension meet.

## What this chapter covers

How to name an acute kidney injury, what to do with potassium, sodium, and acid, and the CKD interventions that change outcome. Renal and reproductive content together are about 7–13% of Step 2 CK. Reproductive disease is in the women’s health chapter.

## The framework

Acute kidney injury is prerenal, intrinsic, or postrenal until you have looked. A bladder scan or a catheter answers obstruction faster than a long differential. Urine sediment answers glomerulonephritis (red-cell casts) and acute tubular injury (muddy casts). The fraction of excreted sodium helps only when diuretics are not already in the story.

| Problem | The move |
| --- | --- |
| Hyperkalemia with ECG changes | Stabilize the membrane with calcium, shift potassium inward, then remove it. Calcium does not lower the potassium. It buys time. |
| Severe hyponatremia with seizures | Hypertonic saline, in a monitored setting. Chronic asymptomatic hyponatremia is corrected slowly because the brain has adapted. |
| Hypernatremia | Calculate the free-water deficit and replace it at a pace the brain can follow. |
| CKD with albuminuria | An ACE inhibitor or an ARB, not both. SGLT2 inhibitors have a kidney indication in diabetic kidney disease and in many patients without diabetes. Confirm the eGFR floor in the current label and in KDIGO before you start one. |
| Hypertension and CKD | 2025 AHA/ACC guideline: ACE inhibitor or ARB when eGFR is under 60 and albuminuria is at least 30 mg/g. |
| Dialysis indications | Acidosis, electrolyte emergency you cannot move, intoxicant, overload, uremic complication (pericarditis, encephalopathy, refractory bleeding). The letters are a memory hook. The patient is the indication. |

Contrast, NSAIDs, and ACE inhibitors are the three outpatient drugs that show up in a creatinine bump. NSAIDs constrict the afferent arteriole. ACE inhibitors dilate the efferent. Together, in a low-flow kidney, filtration falls. A small creatinine rise after starting an ACE inhibitor can be hemodynamic and acceptable. A large rise means stop and look for renal artery disease or volume loss. KDIGO and the drug label own the percent. Do not invent one.

Nephrotic syndrome is edema, heavy protein, and a thrombotic tendency. Nephritic syndrome is blood, casts, and hypertension. The Step 2 question is the complication and the next test (biopsy when it will change treatment), not the Step 1 immunofluorescence pattern, unless the stem is clearly asking for the mechanism.

Stones: a noncontrast CT is the usual adult test when the diagnosis is uncertain. Infection behind an obstructing stone is a drainage emergency, the same logic as cholangitis. Alpha blockers may be offered for some distal stones. Confirm size cutoffs rather than memorizing a millimeter from an old lecture.

## High-yield atlas

Renal items on Step 2 CK are a sequence. Name prerenal, intrinsic, or postrenal before you chase a rare sediment. Calcium does not remove potassium. A normal creatinine does not make an infected blocked kidney safe. The framework above is the map. This atlas is the set.

| Presentation and findings | Next best step | First-line management and the source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volume loss or low flow, BUN out of proportion to creatinine, bland urine, and a low urine sodium if no diuretic was given. | Restore perfusion if he is dry. If the lungs are wet, the physiology is still low flow and a fluid bolus is wrong. Stop the NSAID. | KDIGO acute kidney injury guideline. Fractional excretion of sodium under 1 percent supports this state only off diuretics. On a diuretic, fractional excretion of urea under 35 percent is the substitute. A modest creatinine rise after an ACE inhibitor can be hemodynamic. A large rise means stop and look for volume loss or renal-artery stenosis. KDIGO owns that percent. Do not invent one. |
| A hypotensive or toxic insult, then oliguria that persists after euvolemia. Muddy brown casts. Fractional excretion of sodium over 2 percent. | Stop nephrotoxins. Dose drugs for the current GFR. Dialyze only for an indication, not for the creatinine. | KDIGO. There is no renal-dose dopamine. Fluid after he is euvolemic causes pulmonary edema. The recovery phase can be polyuric. Watch the potassium. |
| Anuria or fluctuating output, suprapubic discomfort, prostate symptoms, or a solitary kidney. Creatinine rises only if outflow from both kidneys, or the only kidney, is blocked. | Bladder scan or catheter before a serologic workup. Ultrasound for hydronephrosis. Relieve the block. Replace only the hemodynamically important part of a post-obstructive diuresis. | KDIGO. A catheter is both the test and the treatment of bladder outlet obstruction. Bilateral hydronephrosis or a solitary kidney needs decompression. |

Do not decide with urinary eosinophils. Steroids are a nephrology decision if function does not return after the drug is stopped. | KDIGO discussions of acute interstitial nephritis. Urinary eosinophils are not accurate enough to keep a suspect drug going. Pyelonephritis also has white-cell casts, plus bacteria, nitrites, fever, and flank pain. |
| Peaked T waves, a flat P, a wide QRS, or a sine wave, with a high potassium. | Calcium to stabilize the membrane now. Shift potassium inward. Then remove it. Calcium does not lower the potassium. | KDIGO and emergency cardiac care. Removal is a loop diuretic if the kidneys work, a gastrointestinal binder, or dialysis. Give glucose with the insulin and recheck the glucose. Stop the offending drug. Do not stop after the calcium. |
| A seizure or deep coma and a sodium often under 120 mEq/L. | A bolus of 3 percent saline in a monitored bed until the seizure stops. Then slow down. Do not fluid-restrict a seizing patient. | European hyponatremia clinical practice guideline. After the acute danger, keep the 24-hour rise conservative, generally not more than about 8 mEq/L, especially with alcohol use, malnutrition, hypokalemia, or liver disease. If you overshoot, desmopressin and free water can bring the sodium back. A vaptan is not for a seizure. |
| Euvolemia, low serum osmolality, urine that is not dilute, urine sodium that is not low, and no recent diuretic. Thyroid and adrenal function must be normal. | Fluid restriction for mild symptoms. Check cortisol and TSH before you lock the label. Hypertonic saline only for severe symptoms. Do not use isotonic saline as the routine fix. | European hyponatremia guideline. Isotonic saline can worsen the sodium when the urine is very concentrated, because the sodium is excreted and the water is kept. A vaptan is not for an acute neurologic emergency. Cerebral salt wasting after subarachnoid hemorrhage is hypovolemic, and restriction harms that patient. |
| Sodium above 145, no access to water or a concentrating defect, and a volume exam. Urine osmolality says whether the kidney is doing its job. | Isotonic fluid first if he is in shock. Then free water, slowly. Do not drop a chronic deficit faster than about 10 to 12 mEq/L in a day. | Replace the free-water deficit and ongoing losses. Central diabetes insipidus has dilute urine and responds to desmopressin. Nephrogenic disease, often from lithium, does not. A concentrated urine means the problem is water he did not get. |
| eGFR under 60, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio at least 30 mg/g, and blood pressure that is up. Often diabetes. | An ACE inhibitor or an ARB, not both. An SGLT2 inhibitor if the eGFR floor allows and he is eating and well. Do not start one during acute illness. | KDIGO CKD guideline and the 2025 AHA/ACC blood pressure guideline. That blood pressure guideline recommends an ACE inhibitor or an ARB when eGFR is under 60 and albuminuria is at least 30 mg/g. KDIGO suggests a systolic pressure under 120 mm Hg, measured in a standardized way, for many adults with CKD. Confirm the SGLT2 inhibitor floor in the label and in KDIGO. In diabetic kidney disease, a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid antagonist is an add-on when potassium allows. Do not stack it on spironolactone. |
| Acidosis you cannot move, potassium you cannot move, a dialyzable poison, pulmonary edema that will not yield, or uremia with pericarditis, encephalopathy, or bleeding. | Dialyze for the indication. Do not dialyze a creatinine. Do not wait on a potassium if he has a rub. | KDIGO. Acidosis, electrolyte, intoxicant, overload, and uremia are the memory hook. The patient is the indication. A creatinine of 6 in a walking patient with a normal ECG and no rub is not an emergency procedure. |
| Fever or sepsis, flank pain, and hydronephrosis. Creatinine may be normal if the other kidney works. | Antibiotics and decompression, by stent or percutaneous nephrostomy. Not antibiotics alone. Not shock-wave lithotripsy during sepsis. | AUA and IDSA. An infected closed space is drained, the same logic as an infected bile duct. A normal creatinine does not cancel drainage. Do not wait on the culture to decompress. |
| Colic, hematuria, a ureteral stone on noncontrast CT, no fever, pain that can be controlled, and two working kidneys. | Pain control, often an NSAID if kidney function allows. Offer medical expulsive therapy for a selected distal stone. Urology if the stone is unlikely to pass, pain is uncontrolled, there is a solitary kidney, or creatinine is rising. | AUA. Noncontrast CT is the usual adult test when the diagnosis is uncertain. Ultrasound first in pregnancy. An alpha blocker may be offered for a distal ureteral stone up to about 10 mm. Confirm the cutoff. Antibiotics are for infection, not for every stone. |
| Edema, urine protein at or above 3.5 g per day, low albumin, and high cholesterol. This patient is an adult. | Quantify the protein. Biopsy an adult before disease-specific immunosuppression. Salt restriction, a diuretic, and an ACE inhibitor or an ARB. Suspect renal-vein thrombosis if the flank hurts or the creatinine jumps, especially in membranous disease. | KDIGO glomerular disease guideline. A child with typical sudden nephrotic syndrome is often treated as minimal change without a biopsy. An adult is not. Anticoagulation thresholds depend on the albumin, the histology, and bleeding risk. Confirm KDIGO. Do not anticoagulate every swollen leg. Intravenous albumin is not the treatment of the syndrome. |
| Creatinine rising over days to weeks, hematuria, red-cell casts, sometimes hemoptysis. | Urgent serologies and nephrology the same day. ANCA, anti-GBM antibody, ANA, and complements. Biopsy if it is safe. Do not send him home on an antibiotic for urinary blood. | KDIGO glomerular disease guideline. Anti-GBM disease, especially with lung hemorrhage, is plasma exchange plus immunosuppression. ANCA vasculitis needs prompt immunosuppression. Plasma exchange is not automatic for every ANCA patient. Normal complements point toward ANCA, anti-GBM, or IgA. Low C3 points toward post-streptococcal disease, lupus, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, or endocarditis. |
| Long-standing diabetes, retinopathy, albuminuria, a slow creatinine rise, and a bland sediment. | An ACE inhibitor or an ARB, an SGLT2 inhibitor, blood pressure, and glucose. Do not biopsy this picture. Biopsy when the story does not fit. | KDIGO and the ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026. Biopsy for red-cell casts, a rapid decline, a short duration, absent retinopathy in type 1, or systemic signs. A nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid antagonist can be added for persistent albuminuria when potassium allows. |
| Low bicarbonate, a normal anion gap, and a potassium that separates the causes. Diarrhea is the common one. | Use the history and the urine anion gap to separate gut loss from renal tubular acidosis. Replace potassium and bicarbonate. Treat ongoing diarrhea. | A negative urine anion gap, sodium plus potassium minus chloride, means the kidney is excreting ammonium and the loss is gastrointestinal. A positive gap and a urine pH above 5.3 during acidosis is distal renal tubular acidosis: hypokalemia and stones. Type 4 is hyperkalemic and mild, with diabetes or an aldosterone-blocking drug. Proximal disease can show a Fanconi pattern, and the urine can acidify once serum bicarbonate is low enough. |
| Anion gap acidosis and an osmolar gap, without marked hyperglycemia. Visual symptoms suggest methanol. Oxalate crystals, hypocalcemia, and kidney injury suggest ethylene glycol. | Give fomepizole now if the story is strong. Do not wait for a send-out level. Dialyze severe acidosis, visual injury, kidney failure, or a high level. | American Academy of Clinical Toxicology and the EXTRIP workgroup. Fomepizole blocks alcohol dehydrogenase. Ethanol is the alternative only if fomepizole is unavailable. Use bicarbonate if he is severely acidemic. Isopropyl alcohol is the neighbor: ketones and an osmolar gap without an anion gap acidosis, and it is supportive care. |
| A crush, a long lie, or a statin plus a fibrate. Dark urine, a creatine kinase in the thousands, dipstick blood with no red cells, and hyperkalemia. | Isotonic fluid. Treat ECG changes from potassium. Examine for a tight compartment. Do not use a loop diuretic as the plan to flush the kidney. | Volume resuscitation is the kidney protection consistent with KDIGO acute kidney injury care. A tense compartment needs a surgeon, not more fluid alone. There is no creatine kinase number that replaces the exam. Do not give calcium for early asymptomatic hypocalcemia. Alkalinization is not required. Confirm before you add bicarbonate. |

| --- | --- |
| Prerenal acute kidney injury versus the neighbor | The same numbers with rales and a known cardiomyopathy are cardiorenal. Another liter drowns him. Muddy brown casts and a sodium excretion over 2 percent are tubular injury and will not reverse in an hour. |
| Acute tubular injury versus the neighbor | Prerenal urine is bland, the urine sodium is low, and volume fixes it. White-cell casts after a new drug are interstitial nephritis. Red-cell casts are glomerulonephritis. |
| Postrenal acute kidney injury versus the neighbor | A unilateral ureteral stone with a working other kidney often leaves the creatinine normal. That does not make an infected blocked kidney safe. It does mean a normal creatinine does not exclude one-sided obstruction. |
| Acute interstitial nephritis versus the neighbor | Muddy brown casts are tubular necrosis. Red-cell casts are glomerulonephritis. Infected urine and flank pain are pyelonephritis. |
| Hyperkalemia with ECG changes versus the neighbor | A potassium of 5.4 with a normal ECG and a working kidney needs the drug stopped and removal, not a reflex calcium push. A dialysis patient with a sine wave needs dialysis. |
| Severe symptomatic hyponatremia versus the neighbor | A sodium of 124 in a walking, talking patient is corrected slowly. The seizure protocol is the injury in that patient. |
| Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis versus the neighbor | Dry mucous membranes and a low urine sodium after diarrhea need isotonic saline. Edema from heart failure is treated as the heart, plus restriction. Cerebral salt wasting is volume down after brain injury. |
| Hypernatremia versus the neighbor | Dilute urine and polyuria are diabetes insipidus. Shock with a high sodium gets isotonic volume first, not dextrose in water as the resuscitation liter. |
| CKD with albuminuria versus the neighbor | A normal albumin ratio, an eGFR over 60, and a pressure of 134/84 is a stage 1 blood pressure question, not this kidney regimen. Acute illness is a reason to hold the SGLT2 inhibitor. |
| Uremic indication for dialysis versus the neighbor | A creatinine of 3 and a BUN of 80 in a well patient with a normal ECG is not an emergency procedure. A wide QRS from hyperkalemia is an indication even if the creatinine is only 2. |
| Infected obstructing stone versus the neighbor | The same stone without fever, with pain controlled, is expulsive therapy and follow-up. Pyelonephritis without obstruction is antibiotics and no tube. |
| Uncomplicated ureteral stone versus the neighbor | Fever plus hydronephrosis is decompression. Pregnancy gets ultrasound first. A solitary kidney with a rising creatinine goes to urology. |
| Nephrotic syndrome versus the neighbor | Nephritic disease is blood, red-cell casts, and hypertension. A child with sudden edema and heavy protein may be treated without a biopsy. |
| Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis versus the neighbor | Post-streptococcal disease follows pharyngitis by one to three weeks, drops C3, and is usually supportive. IgA nephropathy bleeds during the infection, not weeks later, and complements are usually normal. Neither is a two-week crash with hemoptysis. |
| Diabetic kidney disease versus the neighbor | Two years of type 2 diabetes, no retinopathy, red-cell casts, and a creatinine that doubled in a month is a biopsy, not this label. |
| Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis versus the neighbor | No diarrhea, bicarbonate 14, potassium 2.8, and urine pH 6.8 is distal renal tubular acidosis. Diabetes with potassium 5.8 and a mild normal-gap acidosis is type 4. Do not give bicarbonate for type 4 and ignore the potassium. |
| Toxic alcohol acidosis versus the neighbor | Isopropyl alcohol is ketosis without this acidosis. Diabetic ketoacidosis has ketones, and the glucose is usually high unless an SGLT2 inhibitor is involved. Neither produces oxalate crystals. |
| Rhabdomyolysis versus the neighbor | True hematuria has red cells on the slide. A compartment syndrome is a tense painful compartment and needs a surgeon even if fluid is already running. |

### Prerenal acute kidney injury

The kidney is underfilled and holding sodium. The tubules are intact.

**Vignette.** Three days of vomiting. He is dry. Blood pressure is 96/60. BUN is 48 mg/dL and creatinine is 1.9 mg/dL. Urine is bland. Urine sodium is 8 mEq/L. No diuretic. Lungs are clear.
**Reasoning.** A dry exam, a high BUN-to-creatinine ratio, bland urine, and a low urine sodium are underfilling. He needs volume. Dialysis before a fluid trial treats a number, not the cause. The diagnosis is Prerenal acute kidney injury.
**NBME trap.** A kidney ultrasound as the first test in a dry patient, a fluid bolus into a wet chest, or a sodium excretion interpreted after a loop diuretic.
**Hook.** Dry and bland: fill him. Wet lungs: do not.

### Acute tubular injury

Tubular cells have died. The kidney cannot concentrate or hold sodium. Time is the repair.

**Vignette.** Yesterday his pressure was 70 from sepsis. Today he is euvolemic and still oliguric, with muddy brown casts. Creatinine is 4.2 mg/dL. Fractional excretion of sodium is 3 percent. Potassium is 4.8. Lungs are clear. There is no rub.
**Reasoning.** The insult was ischemic and the sediment is tubular. More fluid will not restart the tubule, and he has no dialysis indication today. The diagnosis is Acute tubular injury.
**NBME trap.** More saline in a euvolemic oliguric patient, renal-dose dopamine, or dialysis because the creatinine is 4.
**Hook.** Muddy casts after the insult: stop the toxin. Do not flood him.

### Postrenal acute kidney injury

Back pressure stops filtration. Relief is often followed by a salt-wasting diuresis.

**Vignette.** A 75-year-old with prostate symptoms has not voided for 14 hours and has lower abdominal discomfort. Creatinine rose from 1.0 to 3.1 mg/dL. The bladder scan shows 900 mL.
**Reasoning.** A full bladder in a man who cannot void is the obstruction. The catheter comes before any antibody panel. After drainage, match only the losses that affect perfusion. The diagnosis is Postrenal acute kidney injury.
**NBME trap.** Urinary eosinophils and a nephrology consult before anyone scans the bladder, or liter-for-liter replacement of the diuresis for days.
**Hook.** Scan the bladder before you biopsy the kidney.

### Acute interstitial nephritis

The drug is driving allergic tubular inflammation. Continuing it is the ongoing injury.

**Vignette.** Ten days after a penicillin, creatinine has doubled. There is a faint rash. Urine shows white cells and white-cell casts and no bacteria. He is afebrile now.
**Reasoning.** Timing, rash, and sterile white-cell casts are drug-related interstitial inflammation. Stop the penicillin. A urinary eosinophil would not change that. Antibiotics for a culture-negative urine are the wrong direction. The diagnosis is Acute interstitial nephritis.
**NBME trap.** A urinary eosinophil used to continue the drug, or treating sterile pyuria as a urinary infection.
**Hook.** Sterile white-cell casts after a new drug: stop the drug.

### Hyperkalemia with ECG changes

The membrane is unstable. Calcium buys time. Body potassium is unchanged until you remove it.

**Vignette.** Potassium is 6.9 mEq/L. The QRS is wide and the T waves are peaked. He takes lisinopril and spironolactone. Blood pressure is 130/70.
**Reasoning.** The tracing is the emergency. Calcium now, then insulin with glucose, then a plan that removes potassium, and both drugs stop. A binder alone while the QRS is wide misses the membrane. Calcium alone lets the potassium return when the shift wears off. The diagnosis is Hyperkalemia with ECG changes.
**NBME trap.** A binder as the only order, or skipping calcium because it does not lower the number.
**Hook.** Wide QRS: calcium, shift, remove. Calcium is not the removal.

### Severe symptomatic hyponatremia

The immediate problem is brain edema. The later problem is osmotic demyelination if you keep correcting as if the seizure were still happening.

**Vignette.** Sodium is 114 mEq/L and he is seizing. He was well yesterday. The suggestion on the ward is fluid restriction and a morning recheck.
**Reasoning.** A seizure is hypertonic saline now. Fluid restriction is for mild asymptomatic disease. Driving the sodium to 135 tonight is how demyelination follows a seizure that has already stopped. The diagnosis is Severe symptomatic hyponatremia.
**NBME trap.** Normal saline as the seizure treatment, fluid restriction during the seizure, or full correction in one day.
**Hook.** Seizing: 3 percent. Then do not overshoot.

### Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis

Antidiuretic hormone is on when it should be off. The urine stays concentrated. Body sodium is not the deficit. Water is the excess.

**Vignette.** Sodium is 126 mEq/L. He looks euvolemic. Serum osmolality is low. Urine osmolality is 480. Urine sodium is 50 mEq/L. He is not on a diuretic. An SSRI was started last month. TSH and a morning cortisol are not back. He is walking and talking.
**Reasoning.** The urine is inappropriately concentrated and he is euvolemic, but adrenal failure can look the same. For this mild picture the treatment is fluid restriction after the cortisol and TSH, not 3 percent saline and not a liter of normal saline. The diagnosis is Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis.
**NBME trap.** A liter of normal saline, 3 percent saline in a talking patient, or the label before the cortisol is back.
**Hook.** Euvolemic and concentrated urine: restrict, after adrenal failure is out.

### Hypernatremia

Free-water deficit. A chronic deficit has idiogenic osmoles. Fast correction is cerebral edema.

**Vignette.** An 84-year-old from a nursing home is tachycardic and dry. Sodium is 158 mEq/L. Urine osmolality is 800. He is not hypotensive. No one has been helping him drink.
**Reasoning.** Concentrated urine means the kidney can save water, so this is water he did not receive. He needs free water at a controlled pace. Desmopressin is the wrong drug. A drop to 140 today is too fast. The diagnosis is Hypernatremia.
**NBME trap.** Desmopressin for concentrated urine, dextrose as the first liter in shock, or correcting a week-long deficit in one shift.
**Hook.** Concentrated urine: give water. Dilute urine: diabetes insipidus.

### CKD with albuminuria

Albuminuria is the injury you can slow. Two renin-angiotensin blockers add hyperkalemia and acute injury.

**Vignette.** eGFR is 42. Albumin-to-creatinine ratio is 280 mg/g. He has type 2 diabetes. Blood pressure is 148/88 on amlodipine. Potassium is 4.4. He is eating and is not in the hospital.
**Reasoning.** This is the patient both guidelines describe. One of ACE inhibitor or ARB, plus an SGLT2 inhibitor. A modest creatinine rise after the ACE inhibitor can be hemodynamic. A large rise is a stop and a look. The diagnosis is CKD with albuminuria.
**NBME trap.** An ACE inhibitor plus an ARB, stopping the ACE inhibitor for a small creatinine rise, or starting an SGLT2 inhibitor in a patient who is hypotensive and not eating.
**Hook.** Albuminuria: one blocker, and think SGLT2. Never both blockers.

### Uremic indication for dialysis

Medical therapy cannot hold the internal environment, or a toxin will not wait.

**Vignette.** He missed dialysis and has a new pericardial friction rub. Potassium is 5.2 mEq/L. Creatinine is 8 mg/dL. The lungs are clear. Blood pressure is 150/90.
**Reasoning.** Uremic pericarditis is the indication. The potassium is only mildly high. Waiting for a potassium of 7, or reassurance because the lungs are clear, misses the rub. The diagnosis is Uremic indication for dialysis.
**NBME trap.** Emergency dialysis for a creatinine, or a normal potassium used to ignore a rub.
**Hook.** The rub, the lungs, and the potassium are the indication. The creatinine is not.

### Infected obstructing stone

Bacteria behind an obstruction. Antibiotics do not reliably enter a closed, high-pressure space.

**Vignette.** Flank pain, temperature 39, blood pressure 88/50. CT shows a 7 mm ureteral stone and hydronephrosis. Creatinine is 0.9 mg/dL. The other kidney looks normal.
**Reasoning.** He is septic behind a blockage. The normal creatinine only means the other kidney is filtering. He needs a drain and antibiotics, not observation and not lithotripsy. The diagnosis is Infected obstructing stone.
**NBME trap.** Reassurance because the creatinine is normal, antibiotics while you watch a septic obstructed kidney, or lithotripsy in an unstable patient.
**Hook.** Fever plus a blocked kidney: drain it, even if the creatinine is normal.

### Uncomplicated ureteral stone

A stone is moving. Small distal stones often pass. Infection changes the category.

**Vignette.** A 29-year-old has colic. Noncontrast CT shows a 6 mm stone at the ureterovesical junction. He is afebrile. Pain is controlled with an NSAID. Creatinine is normal. Urinalysis shows blood and no white cells.
**Reasoning.** Distal, small enough that passage is plausible, no infection, pain controlled. Offer expulsive therapy and a follow-up plan. A stent tonight treats a stable patient as if he were septic. The diagnosis is Uncomplicated ureteral stone.
**NBME trap.** Antibiotics without infection, CT in pregnancy, or an emergency stent for controlled pain and a small distal stone.
**Hook.** No fever and pain controlled: a small distal stone can try to pass.

### Nephrotic syndrome

The filter leaks protein. Loss of anticoagulant proteins promotes clot, most of all in membranous disease.

**Vignette.** A 46-year-old has anasarca. Urine protein is 6 g/day. Albumin is 2.1 g/dL. Cholesterol is high. He has no diabetes, no retinopathy, and no hematuria. Creatinine is 1.0 mg/dL.
**Reasoning.** Heavy protein in an adult is a biopsy before you pick an immune drug. Supportive care starts while that is arranged. Empiric prednisone is the pediatric shortcut, not his. The diagnosis is Nephrotic syndrome.
**NBME trap.** Empiric prednisone in this adult, intravenous albumin as the plan, or anticoagulation of every nephrotic patient.
**Hook.** An adult with heavy protein: biopsy before the immune drug.

### Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis

Crescents are destroying filters on a timescale of days.

**Vignette.** Creatinine was 1.0 mg/dL two weeks ago and is 4.5 today. Urine shows red-cell casts. He has coughed blood. Complements are normal. He is not septic.
**Reasoning.** The pace, the casts, and the lung bleeding are a pulmonary-renal emergency. Normal complements point toward anti-GBM or ANCA. Treatment is coordinated with biopsy now, not at a clinic in a month. Plasma exchange is the anti-GBM move, not a reflex for every crescent. The diagnosis is Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis.
**NBME trap.** Antibiotics for dipstick blood, plasma exchange for every glomerulonephritis, or a routine biopsy a month from now.
**Hook.** Days, casts, and lung bleeding: treat as a crescent.

### Diabetic kidney disease

The clinical pattern is specific enough that tissue will not change treatment. An active sediment means a second disease.

**Vignette.** Type 1 diabetes for 18 years, proliferative retinopathy, an albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 900 mg/g, and a creatinine that drifted from 1.1 to 1.4 over two years. No red-cell casts. Blood pressure is 146/86 on amlodipine only.
**Reasoning.** Duration, the retina, albuminuria, and a slow course are diabetic kidney disease. Start one of ACE inhibitor or ARB and an SGLT2 inhibitor. A biopsy would not change that. The diagnosis is Diabetic kidney disease.
**NBME trap.** Biopsy of this classic patient, an ACE inhibitor plus an ARB, or ignoring albuminuria because the creatinine is only 1.4.
**Hook.** Retina, albumin, slow course: treat. Casts or a crash: biopsy.

### Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis

Bicarbonate was lost or not regenerated. Chloride rose, so the gap stays normal.

**Vignette.** A week of watery diarrhea. Bicarbonate is 14 mEq/L. The anion gap is normal. Potassium is 3.0. Urine pH is 5.0. He is still having watery stools.
**Reasoning.** The kidney is acidifying, which is the right response to diarrhea. This is not distal renal tubular acidosis. Replace potassium and bicarbonate and treat the diarrhea. A tubular workup during ongoing losses does not change today. The diagnosis is Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis.
**NBME trap.** A renal tubular acidosis label during active diarrhea, or bicarbonate alone for type 4 while the potassium stays high.
**Hook.** Diarrhea and a low urine pH: the kidney is responding.

### Toxic alcohol acidosis

Parent alcohol raises the osmolar gap. Acid metabolites raise the anion gap. As the parent is metabolized, the osmolar gap falls and the anion gap rises.

**Vignette.** He is found down. pH is 7.10. Anion gap is 28. Glucose is 110 mg/dL. Ketones are negative. The osmolar gap is 30. Calcium is low. Urine shows envelope crystals. A methanol level is pending at a reference lab.
**Reasoning.** Gap acidosis, an osmolar gap, low calcium, and oxalate crystals are ethylene glycol. Fomepizole now. Insulin treats a ketoacidosis he does not have. Waiting for the level lets toxic metabolites form. The diagnosis is Toxic alcohol acidosis.
**NBME trap.** Waiting for the send-out level, an insulin drip for a normal glucose and no ketones, or fomepizole for isopropyl alcohol.
**Hook.** Anion gap plus osmolar gap: fomepizole now.

### Rhabdomyolysis

Myoglobin injures tubules and potassium leaves muscle. The dipstick cannot tell myoglobin from blood. The microscope can.

**Vignette.** Found on the floor. Creatine kinase is 40,000 U/L. Potassium is 6.4 mEq/L with peaked T waves. Dipstick blood is strong and microscopy shows no red cells. The calf is soft.
**Reasoning.** Pigment without red cells and this kinase are muscle, not glomerular bleeding. Peaked T waves get calcium, a shift, and removal, and he needs isotonic fluid. The calf is soft, so there is nothing to decompress. A loop diuretic does not protect the tubule. The diagnosis is Rhabdomyolysis.
**NBME trap.** A loop diuretic to flush pigment, calcium for asymptomatic early hypocalcemia, or withholding fluid because the creatinine has not risen yet.
**Hook.** Dipstick blood, no red cells, high kinase: fluid and the potassium.

## Checklist

- [ ] Obstruction is a bedside question, not a lab question.
- [ ] Calcium for ECG changes does not remove potassium.
- [ ] Slow correction of chronic hyponatremia.
- [ ] One of ACE inhibitor or ARB, plus think about an SGLT2 inhibitor.
- [ ] Infected obstructing stone gets drained.

## Common traps

- Insulin and beta agonist without a plan to remove potassium. The shift reverses.
- Normal saline as a reflex for every sodium problem. It is the wrong fluid for hypernatremia’s free-water deficit and it can worsen some acidoses.
- Combining ACE inhibitor and ARB “for the protein.” More hyperkalemia and acute injury, without a benefit you want.
- Biopsying every diabetic with protein and retinopathy. The diagnosis is often already clinical. Biopsy when the story does not fit.
