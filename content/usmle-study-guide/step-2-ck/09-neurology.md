# Neurology

> **Study aid only.** The time-limited emergencies and the outpatient diagnoses. Thresholds for thrombolysis change with new AHA/ASA science. Confirm the window before you treat.

## What this chapter covers

Stroke, seizure, headache that is a bleed or a tumor, and the weakness patterns that tell you the place. Nervous system and special senses are about 5–10% of Step 2 CK. Localization was Step 1. This chapter is the action.

## The framework

A focal deficit that is sudden is a vascular event until imaging says otherwise. The sequence is glucose, a non-contrast CT to look for blood, and a clock that started at last known well, not at when the person was found. Thrombolysis is for selected patients inside a window the AHA/ASA defines, and a longer window exists only when imaging selects them. Thrombectomy is for a large-vessel occlusion in a selected patient, including some who are well past the thrombolysis window. Do not invent the hours. Know that both clocks exist and that “too late for the drug” is not “too late for the procedure.”

Blood pressure in acute ischemic stroke is left alone unless it is extremely high or you are about to give a thrombolytic, which has its own ceiling. Dropping the pressure in a chronic hypertensive patient who is having a stroke can extend the infarct. Hemorrhagic stroke is the opposite problem: pressure control is part of the treatment. The 2025 high blood pressure guideline has a specific note on spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage. Confirm it before you quote a target.

| Problem | Next step |
| --- | --- |
| First seizure, back to baseline | It may not need a lifelong drug. Look for a provoking cause. Image and an EEG as the neurology guidance directs. Counsel on driving using your jurisdiction’s law, not a guess. |
| Status epilepticus | Benzodiazepine now, then a longer-acting antiseizure drug. Do not keep repeating the benzodiazepine as the only plan. Protect the airway. Check glucose. |
| Thunderclap headache | A bleed until a CT and, if the CT is negative and suspicion stays high, a lumbar puncture or a vessel study says otherwise. |
| Temporal arteritis, age over 50, new headache, jaw claudication, vision symptoms | Start steroid. Do not wait for the biopsy if vision is threatened. The ESR supports the story. It does not clear the person. |
| Guillain-Barré | Ascending weakness, lost reflexes, often after an infection. Monitor the vital capacity. IVIG or plasma exchange. Not steroids as the treatment. |
| Myasthenic crisis | Fatigable weakness, airway risk. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor may worsen secretions in a crisis. The disease treatment is plasma exchange or IVIG, plus the airway. |
| Bacterial meningitis | See the infectious disease chapter. Time to antibiotic beats time to a pretty LP. |

Headache red flags are fever, cancer, immunosuppression, neurologic deficit, sudden onset, older age at onset, and papilledema. A chronic migraine without flags does not need a CT to be kind. It needs a prevention plan when attacks are frequent: a preventive drug, and attention to medication-overuse from the as-needed drugs.

Parkinson disease treatment starts when function suffers. Levodopa is the most effective motor drug. The choice among drugs in a young patient is a neurology conversation about dyskinesia over years. Step 2 wants you to recognize the syndrome and the drug class that can cause it (antipsychotics that block dopamine).

## High-yield atlas

![Last known well starts two clocks](visuals/stroke-clock.svg)

A focal deficit that arrived in a moment is a blood vessel until a scan says otherwise. The rest of this chapter is the same habit applied to seizures, headaches, and weak limbs: name the place, name the clock, and do the thing that changes the next hour. The feed cards are single associations. This atlas is the decision those associations sit inside.

### Ischemic stroke, the first hour

Last known well is the time the person was last seen at their baseline, not the time they were found. A wake-up stroke starts the clock at bedtime unless imaging later selects them for a treatment. Glucose comes before you blame the brain, because hypoglycemia copies a stroke and is fixed with sugar. The first image is a non-contrast CT of the head. Its job is to find blood. It is a poor test for an early infarct, and a normal CT does not mean this is not a stroke.

| What you have | Next step | Management and the source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deficit ongoing, glucose normal, CT shows no blood, inside the drug window, no contraindication | Thrombolysis if the AHA/ASA selection is met | [AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke guideline](https://www.ahajournals.org/). Confirm the current window. Do not print a dose from memory. |
| A large-vessel occlusion on vascular imaging, even if the drug window has closed | Thrombectomy evaluation | Imaging-selected patients can be treated many hours after last known well. “Too late for the drug” is not “too late for the procedure.” |
| Blood on the non-contrast CT | Stop. This is not a thrombolysis case | Hemorrhage has its own pressure and surgical questions. Contrast imaging does not come before you know about blood. |

**Vignette.** A 68-year-old man was last seen well at 7 a.m. His wife found him at 9 a.m. unable to speak, with a weak right arm. Fingerstick glucose is 96 mg/dL. He takes no anticoagulant.
**Reasoning.** Last known well is 7 a.m. The deficit is ongoing and cortical (language plus arm). Glucose is not the cause. The next image is a non-contrast CT, looking for blood, and the drug decision uses the AHA/ASA window measured from 7 a.m. The diagnosis is an acute ischemic stroke until the CT shows blood. The trap is starting the clock at 9 a.m. because that is when he was found, or ordering a CT angiogram before anyone has excluded hemorrhage.
**NBME trap.** A normal early CT is used as proof that he can go home. Early infarcts are often invisible. The story is the diagnosis.
**Hook.** Last known well starts both clocks. Blood on the CT stops only the drug.

### Blood pressure while the brain is ischemic

A chronic hypertensive circulation is used to a high pressure. Dropping that pressure into the “normal” range during an acute ischemic stroke can extend the infarct. If he is not getting a thrombolytic and he has no other emergency (aortic dissection, a hypertensive encephalopathy, a heart attack that needs it), the AHA/ASA approach is to leave the pressure alone unless it is extremely high, classically above 220 systolic or 120 diastolic. Confirm the current threshold before you treat a real patient.

A thrombolytic has its own ceiling. The pressure has to be brought under the pretreatment limit before the drug, and kept under a slightly lower ceiling afterward. The figures taught from the AHA/ASA guideline are under 185/110 before the drug and under 180/105 for the next day. Those ceilings are why you treat the pressure in a thrombolysis candidate and why you do not “normalize” everyone else.

**Vignette.** Blood pressure is 198/104. The CT has no blood. The team is not giving a thrombolytic because he is outside the window. Someone asks for a drip to bring him to 130.
**Reasoning.** He is not a thrombolysis candidate and 198 is under the “extremely high” range. Lowering him toward 130 can grow the infarct. The diagnosis is an acute ischemic stroke whose pressure should be watched, not normalized. The trap is treating the number because it looks untidy on the monitor.
**NBME trap.** They offer nicardipine for every stroke pressure above 160. The indication is the treatment plan (a lytic, or a pressure that is extreme), not the fact of a stroke.
**Hook.** Lytic candidates get their pressure lowered. Everyone else is left alone unless the pressure is extreme or another organ is failing.

### Intracerebral hemorrhage

Blood on the CT changes the job. The thrombolytic is forbidden. Pressure control becomes part of the treatment, which is the opposite of the ischemic rule. The 2022 AHA/ASA guideline on spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage treats a presenting systolic pressure between 150 and 220 by lowering it toward 140, and it warns that going below 130 can be harmful. Confirm that target. A cerebellar hemorrhage that is compressing the brainstem is a surgical conversation. A deep bleed in a person on warfarin is a reversal conversation. Vitamin K alone is too slow for a life-threatening warfarin bleed. A factor product is the fast reversal, with vitamin K so the reversal lasts. A direct oral anticoagulant is not reversed by vitamin K.

Coagulopathy, a spot sign, and expansion on a repeat scan are why these patients are not sent to a quiet ward and forgotten. Seizure prophylaxis for every bleed is not the same as treating a witnessed seizure. Do not start a long antiseizure drug because blood was seen, unless the guideline in front of you says to.

**Vignette.** Sudden headache and a left hemiparesis. The CT shows a right basal ganglia bleed. He takes warfarin. The INR is 3.4. Systolic pressure is 188.
**Reasoning.** This is a spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage on warfarin. Reverse the warfarin with a factor product and vitamin K, and lower the pressure along the AHA/ASA hemorrhage target rather than the ischemic “leave it alone” rule. The trap is alteplase because the story sounded like a stroke, or vitamin K alone because that is the warfarin drug he knows.
**NBME trap.** They apply the ischemic blood-pressure rule (do not treat) to a CT that already shows blood.
**Hook.** Blood on the scan flips the blood-pressure rule and starts a reversal clock if a drug caused it.

### Subarachnoid hemorrhage

A thunderclap headache, the worst of the person’s life and maximal at onset, is a subarachnoid hemorrhage until a CT and, when the CT is negative and the suspicion remains, a lumbar puncture or a vessel study says otherwise. Sensitivity of CT is highest in the first hours and falls after that. A negative CT the next day is not a clean bill. Xanthochromia in the cerebrospinal fluid is the late clue. Do not call a traumatic tap the whole answer when the suspicion was a thunderclap.

Once the bleed is real, two jobs run together. Secure the aneurysm (clip or coil, a neurosurgical and endovascular decision) so it does not bleed again. Prevent delayed ischemia with nimodipine. Nimodipine is not a blood-pressure toy and it is not a treatment you skip because the person looks comfortable on day one. The deficit that appears days later can be vasospasm. Hydration and a vessel study beat a new diagnosis of “a different stroke” made from the doorway.

**Vignette.** She was at a meeting, grabbed her head, and said this was the worst pain of her life, all in one second. An hour later the non-contrast CT is read as normal. She is still in severe pain. The scan was early.
**Reasoning.** An early negative CT does not finish a thunderclap. The next step is a lumbar puncture looking for blood or xanthochromia, or a vascular study, depending on the current pathway. The diagnosis is a subarachnoid hemorrhage until those tests clear it. The trap is a migraine cocktail because the CT was normal.
**NBME trap.** They do the lumbar puncture before the CT in a person who may have a mass or a large bleed. Image first when the story could be a mass. In a classic thunderclap the CT is still first, because it is fast and it finds the blood that would make a puncture dangerous to interpret.
**Hook.** Thunderclap plus a negative CT is a puncture or a vessel study, not a dark room.

### TIA and the next stroke

A deficit that has resolved is not a person who dodged the disease. A transient ischemic attack is the unstable angina of the brain. The same-day work is vascular imaging, a look at the heart rhythm, antiplatelet therapy, a high-intensity statin when atherosclerosis is the setting, and blood pressure care. That work is not a clinic appointment next month and it is not done from the parking lot.

Atrial fibrillation changes the drug. A cardioembolic source is anticoagulation when bleeding allows, not another antiplatelet. Symptomatic high-grade carotid stenosis on the side that explains the deficit is a revascularization conversation. An asymptomatic bruit found on a routine exam is a different, quieter question. Do not send a symptomatic carotid lesion to “medical management only” because you remember the asymptomatic trials.

**Vignette.** Ten minutes of right-arm weakness and word-finding trouble. He is normal when you meet him. Blood pressure is 154/90. No headache.
**Reasoning.** The deficit resolved and it was focal. This is a TIA. He stays for the vascular and rhythm work today. Aspirin is started unless a bleed or a cardioembolic source changes the drug. The trap is reassurance because the exam is now normal.
**NBME trap.** They offer a carotid endarterectomy for an asymptomatic bruit on the wrong side. The operation is for the lesion that explains the symptoms, at a stenosis the surgeon and the guideline agree is high enough.
**Hook.** A normal exam after a focal spell is the start of the workup, not the end.

### Status epilepticus

A seizure that does not stop, or that recurs without recovery, is status epilepticus. The first drug is a benzodiazepine, given by a route you actually have: intravenous lorazepam when you have a line, intramuscular midazolam when you do not. Check glucose while that is happening. Then give a real antiseizure drug, a full load, not a third and fourth dose of the benzodiazepine as the entire plan. The American Epilepsy Society sequence is benzodiazepine, then a nonsedating antiseizure drug (levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or valproate are the usual loads). Protect the airway. Look for the cause after the shaking is controlled: a missed drug, a low sodium, a bleed, a withdrawal, an infection.

A single benzodiazepine that works, followed by discharge with no load and no cause, is how the next seizure arrives in the ambulance bay.

**Vignette.** He has been stiff and shaking for six minutes. There is no intravenous line. Glucose is 110 mg/dL. Someone suggests waiting to see if it stops, because most seizures are short.
**Reasoning.** Six minutes is already status for practical purposes. Give intramuscular midazolam now, then a full load of a nonsedating antiseizure drug, and look for a cause. The diagnosis is status epilepticus. The trap is a second and third benzodiazepine and no load, or a lecture about “most seizures stop” while this one has not.
**NBME trap.** They intubate and paralyze without an antiseizure drug, so the brain keeps seizing behind a quiet body. Paralysis without EEG and without a seizure drug hides the disease.
**Hook.** Benzodiazepine to stop it. A real load so it stays stopped.

### A first seizure that has already stopped

A single seizure in an adult who is back to baseline is not automatically a lifelong drug. Look for a provoking cause: alcohol withdrawal, a low sodium, a missed sleep in the setting of a drug that lowers the threshold, a mass, an infection. Imaging and an EEG are the neurology workup. Counseling includes driving, and the interval is the law of the jurisdiction, not a number you liked in a lecture. Do not invent a month count.

Status epilepticus is never this paragraph. A person who is still postictal and hypoxic is not “back to baseline.”

**Vignette.** A 24-year-old had one witnessed convulsion at a party. He is awake, oriented, and embarrassed. He had been up all night and drank heavily. The exam is normal.
**Reasoning.** This is a provoked first seizure until you have looked. He does not automatically start a lifelong drug in the hallway. He needs a real exam for a cause, counseling, and follow-up. The trap is either dismissing him with no workup because alcohol was nearby, or loading him with a drug for life because a seizure happened once.
**NBME trap.** They start phenytoin in the emergency department for a single provoked seizure and call that the standard. Provocation and a return to baseline change the urgency of a drug.
**Hook.** One seizure, awake, a cause you can see: do not hand out a lifetime prescription in the hallway.

### Headache you cannot send home

Red flags are fever, cancer, immunosuppression, a neurologic deficit, sudden onset, older age at a first headache, and papilledema. Each one changes the next test.

Temporal arteritis is a new headache in a person over 50, often with jaw pain on chewing, scalp tenderness, or a visual symptom. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein support the story. They do not clear the person, and they do not delay the steroid when vision is threatened. Start the steroid. Arrange the biopsy. The biopsy can still be useful for days after the steroid starts. Blindness is the complication you did not wait on.

Meningitis is fever, a stiff neck, and a change in thinking. Antibiotics come before a lumbar puncture that is going to be delayed by imaging. That sequence lives in the infection chapter and it applies here the moment the headache has a fever and a sick person attached.

A chronic migraine with no flag does not need a CT to be kind. It needs a prevention plan when attacks are frequent, and it needs you to notice medication-overuse headache from the as-needed drugs taken most days. A triptan is for an attack, and it is the wrong drug in coronary disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or a hemiplegic or brainstem aura. Do not hand a triptan to a person whose “migraine” is a thunderclap.

**Vignette.** She is 72. Two weeks of a new headache, pain in the jaw when she chews, and this morning a gray curtain over one eye for a few minutes. The sedimentation rate is mildly elevated.
**Reasoning.** Age, a new headache, jaw claudication, and a transient visual loss are temporal arteritis even if the sedimentation rate is only mildly up. Start a steroid now and arrange a biopsy. The diagnosis is giant cell arteritis. The trap is a week of imaging because the lab was not dramatic enough.
**NBME trap.** They withhold the steroid until the biopsy is on the calendar. Vision does not keep that appointment.
**Hook.** Jaw pain on chewing in an older new headache is a steroid, not a dental referral.

### Guillain-Barré

Ascending weakness, lost reflexes, often a few weeks after a diarrheal or respiratory illness. The cerebrospinal fluid can show a high protein and a normal cell count, and that albumino-cytologic dissociation can be absent early. Do not wait for it to intubate a person whose vital capacity is falling. The classic respiratory numbers (vital capacity falling toward 20 mL/kg, weakening inspiratory force) are a prompt to move them to a monitored bed. Confirm the current neuromuscular criteria. Treatment is intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange. Steroids are not the treatment. They do not help this disease and the question will offer them because they help so many other immune diseases.

A spinal cord lesion has a sensory level and often bladder findings, and the reflexes below the lesion may be brisk once spinal shock passes. Guillain-Barré reflexes are gone. Botulism descends and starts with the pupils and the eyes. Myasthenia fatigues and fluctuates and usually has a history.

**Vignette.** Two weeks after a camping trip with diarrhea, he cannot stand. Legs are weak, then arms. Reflexes are absent. He is short of breath. Sensation is vaguely reduced in the feet. No sensory level.
**Reasoning.** The timing, the ascent, and the missing reflexes are an acute demyelinating polyneuropathy. Check the vital capacity and start immunoglobulin or exchange. The diagnosis is Guillain-Barré syndrome. The trap is methylprednisolone because it is an immune disease, or a lumbar puncture result required before anyone protects the airway.
**NBME trap.** They call it myasthenia because he is weak. Myasthenia fatigues, often spares the reflexes, and does not ascend a week after diarrhea.
**Hook.** No reflexes, ascending, after an infection: immunoglobulin or exchange. Not a steroid.

### Myasthenic crisis

Fatigable weakness, often with eyes and swallowing involved, that becomes an airway problem. A crisis can be triggered by infection, surgery, or a drug (the aminoglycosides and magnesium are classic offenders). In a crisis the acetylcholinesterase inhibitor can increase secretions and is not the rescue. The disease treatment is plasma exchange or intravenous immunoglobulin, plus the airway. Intubate on the trend of the vital capacity, not on the first normal blood gas. A person with myasthenia can look comfortable and then fail.

Cholinergic crisis from too much inhibitor is the lookalike: diarrhea, salivation, small pupils, weakness. The old edrophonium test is not an emergency department sport. If you cannot tell, protect the airway and stop guessing with more drug.

**Vignette.** She has a known diagnosis and takes pyridostigmine. A urinary infection started yesterday. Today she cannot hold her head up and her voice is nasal. The first blood gas shows a normal carbon dioxide.
**Reasoning.** Fatigable weakness plus a trigger is a myasthenic crisis. A normal carbon dioxide is not reassuring. Trend the vital capacity, stop adding cholinesterase inhibitor as the rescue, and arrange exchange or immunoglobulin. The diagnosis is myasthenic crisis. The trap is more pyridostigmine because the bottle says it treats her disease.
**NBME trap.** They offer edrophonium in a tiring patient. That test can worsen weakness. The airway comes first.
**Hook.** Nasal voice and a falling head in a person on pyridostigmine: the airway, then exchange or immunoglobulin.

### Cord compression and cauda equina

Cancer plus a new back pain is spinal cord compression until an MRI says otherwise. Walking does not clear it. Steroid, same-day MRI, and a radiation or surgical plan. Physical therapy is the trap.

Cauda equina is a set of sacral findings: saddle anesthesia, urinary retention (a post-void residual tells you more than the patient’s guess), and often bilateral leg symptoms. MRI and surgical decompression. Hours matter for continence. A straight-leg-raise and a week of ibuprofen are for a back that has none of those findings.

A traumatic spinal cord injury is a different room: immobilization, blood pressure support so the cord is perfused, and a spine surgeon. Do not sit them up to “test the legs.”

**Vignette.** Metastatic lung cancer. Three days of thoracic back pain. He can walk. Legs are slightly weak. He had one episode of urinary incontinence he blamed on the cough.
**Reasoning.** Cancer, a new back, a new weakness, and a bladder symptom. MRI today, a steroid as a bridge, and radiation oncology or spine surgery. The diagnosis is malignant spinal cord compression. The trap is a clinic visit next week because he is still walking.
**NBME trap.** They order a plain film and a physical-therapy referral. Plain films miss cord compression. The test is MRI.
**Hook.** Cancer and a new back is an MRI today, even if the person can walk.

### Multiple sclerosis, the relapse in front of you

A young adult with a neurologic deficit that developed over hours to days, especially optic neuritis (painful vision loss) or a partial cord syndrome, is a demyelinating relapse until imaging says otherwise. The acute treatment of a relapse that affects function is a high-dose steroid, not an antibiotic and not a low-dose oral prednisone started because a Medrol pack was easy. The Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial is why low-dose oral prednisone alone is the wrong regimen for optic neuritis. Disease-modifying therapy is the outpatient plan that reduces the next relapse. It is not what you start in the emergency department instead of treating this relapse.

A stroke is maximal at onset. A relapse builds. That tempo is the split when the stem is trying to make you pick alteplase for a 28-year-old with a week of numbness.

**Vignette.** She is 29. Over three days the vision in one eye has dimmed and the eye aches when she looks sideways. The pupil constricts poorly to direct light and briskly to light in the other eye. The CT done at a clinic yesterday was normal.
**Reasoning.** Painful subacute vision loss and a relative afferent pupillary defect are optic neuritis. She needs an MRI and a high-dose steroid conversation, not a thrombolytic and not a Medrol pack as the whole plan. The diagnosis is optic neuritis, often the first event of multiple sclerosis. The trap is a normal CT used as proof the brain is fine. CT is the wrong test for this disease.
**NBME trap.** They give alteplase because “vision loss is a stroke.” The tempo was three days, and the eye hurts.
**Hook.** Pain with eye movement and a dim optic nerve: MRI and a real steroid, not a CT and a thrombolytic.

### Normal pressure hydrocephalus and intracranial hypertension

Normal pressure hydrocephalus is a gait that fails first, then urgency or incontinence, then a quieter cognition. The scan shows large ventricles out of proportion to the atrophy. A large-volume tap that improves the gait predicts who may benefit from a shunt. Do not call every wide ventricle Alzheimer’s disease. Alzheimer’s gait fails late. This gait fails first.

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension is usually a young woman with obesity, headache, transient visual obscurations, and papilledema. Image before the lumbar puncture so you do not puncture a mass. The opening pressure is high and the composition is normal. Acetazolamide and weight loss protect the vision. Serial visual fields are the monitoring. A headache treatment that ignores the papilledema misses the point, which is blindness.

**Vignette.** He is 74. The family says he shuffles and has wet himself twice. He is not particularly forgetful yet. The CT shows large ventricles.
**Reasoning.** Gait and bladder before dementia, with large ventricles, is normal pressure hydrocephalus. A tap test is the next functional step, and a shunt is the treatment if he responds. The diagnosis is normal pressure hydrocephalus. The trap is donepezil because the ventricles were large and someone said dementia.
**NBME trap.** They do a lumbar puncture as the treatment and send him home with no plan. The tap is a test. The shunt is the treatment.
**Hook.** Feet first, then bladder, then mind, with big ventricles: think shunt, not a cholinesterase inhibitor.

### Bell palsy and Wernicke

Bell palsy includes the forehead. A stroke of the cortex spares the forehead because the upper face has input from both hemispheres. If the forehead is weak, the lesion is peripheral, and the treatment is a steroid started early, eye care so the cornea does not dry, and a thought for Lyme disease where Lyme is common. It is not alteplase. Antiviral therapy is sometimes added. The steroid is the part with the clearer benefit. Do not image every classic Bell palsy before the steroid. Do image if the story is atypical, bilateral, or slowly progressive, because a mass in the angle can imitate it.

Wernicke encephalopathy is confusion, eye-movement weakness, and ataxia in a person who has not been eating: alcohol use, hyperemesis, a long fast after weight-loss surgery. Thiamine is the drug. Give it when you give glucose to a malnourished person. Do not withhold glucose from a low fingerstick while you look for the vial. Give both. The trap is a banana bag tomorrow morning.

**Vignette.** Half of his face is weak, including the forehead, since this morning. He can close the eye only partly. The arm and leg are strong. Speech is normal apart from the slur of a weak mouth.
**Reasoning.** Forehead involved, body spared, language spared. This is a peripheral facial nerve. Steroid and eye care. The diagnosis is Bell palsy. The trap is a stroke alert and a thrombolytic because the face is asymmetric.
**NBME trap.** They spare the forehead in the stem and still call it Bell palsy. Forehead sparing is central. Read the forehead line twice.
**Hook.** Forehead down: steroid and eye care. Forehead spared: think brain.

### Parkinson disease and the drug that copies it

Rest tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia. Levodopa is the most effective motor drug and it is started when the symptoms limit life, not because a tremor exists in a person who is fully functional and the neurologist has a reason to wait. The question you can answer tonight is the drug that caused the picture. Antipsychotics that block dopamine, and metoclopramide, produce a parkinsonian syndrome. Stop the offender. Do not add levodopa to a metoclopramide prescription and call it idiopathic Parkinson disease.

Essential tremor is with action, often the hands and the head, and a drink of alcohol may quiet it. Parkinson tremor is at rest. That split is the whole item.

**Vignette.** He is 70 and has slowed down over a year. The tremor is there when his hands are in his lap and quiets when he reaches for a cup. He has been on metoclopramide for a year for nausea.
**Reasoning.** The tremor is a rest tremor, so the phenomenology is parkinsonian. The drug history is the cause you can remove. Stop the metoclopramide and reassess before you label this idiopathic Parkinson disease. The diagnosis is drug-induced parkinsonism until the drug has been gone long enough to see. The trap is levodopa added to the offending drug.
**NBME trap.** They call an action tremor Parkinson disease and start levodopa. Action tremor, especially with a head tremor, is essential tremor.
**Hook.** Rest tremor plus a dopamine blocker: stop the blocker before you start the levodopa.

### Concussion and the athlete who is not back

A concussion is a functional brain injury. Loss of consciousness is not required. The decision to image uses a validated rule in the right population, not kindness and not a parent’s anxiety alone. A worsening headache, repeated vomiting, a seizure, a focal sign, or a coagulopathy is a different decision and gets imaged. Return to play is graded and starts only after the symptoms have cleared. A second impact while the person is still symptomatic is the disaster the stem is pointing at. Do not clear a symptomatic player because the championship is Saturday.

**Vignette.** A 17-year-old was elbowed in a game, was briefly dazed, and has a headache. He did not pass out. The neurologic exam is normal aside from the headache. His coach wants him back in for the second half.
**Reasoning.** This is a concussion. He does not return today. Image only if a red flag appears. The diagnosis is a concussion. The trap is a CT for reassurance and a same-day return because the CT was normal.
**NBME trap.** They require loss of consciousness before they will call it a concussion. Most concussions have none.
**Hook.** Still symptomatic means still out. A normal CT does not put him back in the game.

### Posterior circulation and the vertigo trap

Peripheral vertigo is intense, positional in a way you can reproduce, and the nystagmus follows rules. Central vertigo brings a second sign: a skew deviation, direction-changing nystagmus, severe ataxia, diplopia, dysarthria, or a limb deficit. The CT of the posterior fossa is a bad friend in the first hours. A normal CT does not send home a person whose bedside exam is central. They stay, and the image that can actually see the brainstem is the one you arrange.

A lone dizzy person who can walk and whose exam is peripheral can leave with a maneuver and precautions. The stem will hide one central sign in the last sentence. Read it.

**Vignette.** Sudden spinning, vomiting, and she cannot walk without falling to one side. There is a subtle droop of one side of the face and the nystagmus changes direction with gaze. The non-contrast CT is normal.
**Reasoning.** Direction-changing nystagmus plus a facial droop and an inability to walk is a brainstem stroke until proven otherwise. A normal CT does not clear the posterior fossa. The diagnosis is a posterior-circulation ischemic stroke. The trap is meclizine and a diagnosis of vestibular neuritis because she is dizzy and the CT was clean.
**NBME trap.** They offer a carotid ultrasound for a posterior-circulation syndrome. The vertebral and basilar arteries are not the cervical carotid. Get the vascular image that matches the territory.
**Hook.** Vertigo plus one central sign stays in the building. The CT is allowed to be normal.

### What the Qbank is pairing with this chapter

Practice the stems that withhold the clock. Last known well, a forehead line, a reflex line, and a tempo (seconds versus days) are the four words that change the answer. When you miss one, write down which of those four you skipped. That log is the study plan.

## Another pass on the stems

These are the items the first pass named and did not fully argue. The vignette still withholds the label.

**Vignette.** He is 58. At lunch he dropped his fork and could not find the word for water. Twenty minutes later he is himself. An ECG shows atrial fibrillation he did not know about. A non-contrast CT has no blood.
**Reasoning.** A focal deficit that resolved is a TIA, and new atrial fibrillation makes the source cardioembolic. The drug, once a bleed is excluded and the rest of the workup allows it, is anticoagulation, not a second antiplatelet piled onto aspirin. He does not go home to “follow up on the irregular heartbeat.” The diagnosis is a cardioembolic TIA. The trap is aspirin alone because the deficit resolved, or a thrombolytic because the word stroke appeared in the triage note after the deficit was already gone.
**NBME trap.** They start heparin in the emergency department for every TIA. Anticoagulation is for the atrial fibrillation indication, started when it is safe, not a reflex drip for a resolved deficit with a sinus ECG.
**Hook.** Fibrillation plus a focal spell: the anticoagulant is the point of the ECG.

**Vignette.** A 19-year-old with known myasthenia is given intravenous magnesium for asthma and becomes too weak to swallow. Reflexes are present. No diarrhea. Pupils are not tiny.
**Reasoning.** Magnesium is a classic precipitant of myasthenic weakness. This is a crisis triggered by a drug, not a cholinergic picture (no diarrhea, no pinpoint pupils). Stop the magnesium, protect the airway, and treat the crisis with exchange or immunoglobulin. The diagnosis is a myasthenic crisis provoked by magnesium.
**NBME trap.** They give more pyridostigmine and another gram of magnesium because the asthma pathway and the myasthenia pathway were written by different people.
**Hook.** Magnesium and an aminoglycoside are myasthenia’s enemy list.

**Vignette.** She is 31, weighs more than she did two years ago, and sees the room go gray for a second when she stands. The discs are swollen on both sides. A CT with contrast shows no mass.
**Reasoning.** Papilledema and transient visual obscurations with a normal scan are idiopathic intracranial hypertension until the lumbar puncture measures the pressure. The puncture comes after the scan. Acetazolamide and weight loss, and a visual-field appointment, are the treatment. The diagnosis is idiopathic intracranial hypertension. The trap is a migraine preventive that never looks at the back of the eye.
**NBME trap.** They do the lumbar puncture first, before imaging, in a person who could have had a mass. Image, then measure the pressure.
**Hook.** Papilledema is a pressure problem until the scan and the opening pressure sort it out.

**Vignette.** Three days of shoulder and neck pain after a viral illness, then a wrist drop. He is not short of breath. Reflexes in the legs are normal. The weakness is in one arm.
**Reasoning.** One limb, pain, after a virus, legs spared, is a neuralgic amyotrophy or a single-nerve lesion, not Guillain-Barré. Guillain-Barré is bilateral and areflexic and marches. The diagnosis is a focal inflammatory neuropathy until imaging of the shoulder plexus region is done if the story is unclear. The trap is immunoglobulin for a wrist drop.
**NBME trap.** They see “after a virus” and “weak” and order plasma exchange. Count the limbs and the reflexes.
**Hook.** Guillain-Barré is bilateral and loses the reflexes. One arm with pain is a different disease.

**Vignette.** He has taken sumatriptan four days a week for six months. The headache is now most days, dull, and he escalates the dose. The exam is normal. He is 34.
**Reasoning.** Frequent as-needed triptan use has created a medication-overuse headache on top of migraine. The plan is to stop the overused drug, often with a bridge, and to start a preventive. Another refill of the triptan is the disease. The diagnosis is medication-overuse headache. The trap is a CT and a higher triptan dose.
**NBME trap.** They add an opioid for the “new daily headache.” Opioids are a common cause of the same loop.
**Hook.** A rescue drug used most days is the headache.

**Vignette.** After a fall from a ladder he cannot move his legs. The blood pressure is 88/60. He is bradycardic. The arms are strong.
**Reasoning.** Acute spinal cord injury can produce neurogenic shock: low pressure and a slow heart, because sympathetic tone is gone. This is not hypovolemic shock (he should be tachycardic if he were simply bleeding, unless something else is wrong) and it is not a reason to sit him up. Immobilize, support the pressure so the cord is perfused, and get him to a spine surgeon. The diagnosis is acute spinal cord injury with neurogenic shock.
**NBME trap.** They give a fluid bolus only and call the slow heart “vasovagal” while the legs stay paralyzed.
**Hook.** Paralyzed legs and a slow, low pressure: the cord, not a fainting spell.

**Vignette.** She is 44 and the room spins when she rolls over in bed. The spinning lasts under a minute. There is no headache, no double vision, and she can walk. Nystagmus is horizontal and fatigues.
**Reasoning.** Brief, positional, with nystagmus that follows peripheral rules and a normal gait, is benign paroxysmal positional vertigo. A canalith maneuver is the treatment. She does not need a thrombolytic and she does not need meclizine for a month. The diagnosis is benign paroxysmal positional vertigo.
**NBME trap.** They admit every vertigo for an MRI. The central signs are what buy the MRI. Their absence, in a classic positional story, is the maneuver.
**Hook.** Under a minute, only in bed, walks fine: move the crystal. Do not move her into the stroke unit.

**Vignette.** A man with alcohol use disorder is brought in hypoglycemic and confused. The nurse has glucose in hand and asks if she must wait for thiamine.
**Reasoning.** Give the glucose. Give thiamine with it. Withholding sugar from a low fingerstick while a vial is hunted is how hypoglycemic brain injury happens. The diagnosis you are preventing is Wernicke encephalopathy on top of hypoglycemia, and you prevent it by giving both. The trap is a strict “thiamine first” rule used to delay the glucose.
**NBME trap.** They pick thiamine and no glucose because a mnemonic said “before.” The mnemonic means “do not give glucose alone and forget thiamine,” not “let the glucose sit.”
**Hook.** Low sugar is treated now. Thiamine rides along. Neither waits on the other.

**Vignette.** He is 66. Over two minutes the left side of the face, the left arm, and the left leg became weak. He speaks in full sentences and understands you. The forehead wrinkles on both sides. He ignores the left half of the dinner tray.
**Reasoning.** Face, arm, and leg together, forehead spared, plus neglect of the weak side, is a right-hemisphere cortical stroke. It is not Bell palsy. Bell palsy takes the forehead and spares the arm and the leg. Last known well, glucose, and a non-contrast CT are the next steps. The diagnosis is an acute hemispheric ischemic stroke.
**NBME trap.** A weak face is called Bell palsy without anyone checking the forehead or the limbs. Forehead spared, plus a limb, is the brain.
**Hook.** Forehead spared and the arm is in it: cortex. Forehead down and the arm is strong: the facial nerve.

**Vignette.** A woman on enoxaparin for a recent clot develops the worst headache of her life. The CT shows subarachnoid blood. Her injection was this morning.
**Reasoning.** The bleed is real and she is anticoagulated. Reversal follows the drug. Enoxaparin is not reversed by vitamin K. Protamine is the specific conversation, and neurosurgery is the other. The diagnosis is a subarachnoid hemorrhage on anticoagulation. The trap is vitamin K because every anticoagulant reversal in her memory is warfarin.
**NBME trap.** They give alteplase because the headache was sudden and they did not read the CT line.
**Hook.** Name the anticoagulant before you pick the reversal. Vitamin K is for warfarin.

## Checklist

- [ ] Last known well, glucose, non-contrast CT.
- [ ] Thrombectomy can still be possible after the drug window.
- [ ] Status gets a benzodiazepine and then a real antiseizure drug.
- [ ] Temporal arteritis steroid does not wait on the biopsy when vision is at risk.
- [ ] Guillain-Barré is not a steroid disease.

## Common traps

- A CT angiogram before the non-contrast CT in a stroke. You need to know about blood first.
- Lowering blood pressure into the normal range during an acute ischemic stroke.
- Steroids for Guillain-Barré because “inflammation.”
- Calling every dizzy person a posterior stroke, or calling none of them one. Use the bedside posterior-circulation clues and do not rely on a CT, which misses early posterior infarcts.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Stroke items are a clock and a contraindication. Seizure items are “is this status.” Headache items are the red flag or its absence. Feed cards with a fixed “4.5 hours” should also say that imaging-selected patients and thrombectomy have different clocks. A card that stops at 4.5 hours will miss the procedure question.
