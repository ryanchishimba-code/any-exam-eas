# Infectious disease

> **Study aid only.** Site, severity, and the likely organism. The regimen is the current IDSA or CDC page, not a mnemonic from a previous year.

## What this chapter covers

How to choose an empiric spectrum without pretending one drug list fits every hospital, plus the syndromes that are emergencies. Blood and immune content is about 5–10% of Step 2 CK. Multisystem infection sits in the 4–8% multisystem range as well.

## The framework

Three questions before any antibiotic name. Where is the infection? How sick is the person? What did they already receive, and what is the local resistance? Then start the narrowest regimen that still covers the killers, take cultures first when a few minutes will not harm, and shrink the regimen when the organism is known.

| Syndrome | Do not miss |
| --- | --- |
| Sepsis and shock | Source, cultures, prompt antibiotics, fluid for hypoperfusion, vasopressor if fluid is not enough. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign discusses a 30 mL/kg starting volume that must be individualized. Lactate is a perfusion marker, not a diagnosis. |
| Meningitis, adult | Do not delay antibiotics for the lumbar puncture if the puncture will be delayed by imaging. Cover the usual bacteria and add the age-and-risk organisms. Dexamethasone is given with or just before the first antibiotic in many adult bacterial cases. Confirm the current IDSA timing. |
| Community pneumonia | Severity decides ward, ICU, or home. See the pulmonary chapter. |
| Cellulitis versus abscess | Abscess is a drainage procedure. Antibiotics do not replace the knife. |
| Sexually transmitted infection | Treat the syndrome and the partners as the CDC guidelines say. Test for HIV and syphilis when you find one of these. |
| HIV, new | Start antiretroviral therapy. The current regimen is a DHHS choice and it changes. Opportunistic prophylaxis is a CD4-threshold table you should open, not memorize from an old card. |
| Tuberculosis | Respiratory isolation when you suspect it. Never treat active disease with one drug. |
| Tick-borne disease | Doxycycline is the drug for suspected Rocky Mountain spotted fever in all ages. Do not withhold it from a child because of an outdated tooth myth. CDC is explicit. |
| C. difficile | Stop the inciting antibiotic when you can. Severity decides oral vancomycin versus a milder regimen. Confirm the current IDSA/SHEA table. Do not reach for metronidazole as a reflex from an old edition. |

Prevention is part of infectious disease. Vaccines in adults, including the ones around pregnancy and the ones after splenectomy, are CDC schedules. Post-exposure HIV prophylaxis is a time-sensitive CDC/DHHS protocol. This chapter will not invent the hours. Open the page.

Fever in a returning traveler and fever in a neutropenic patient are both “do not send home with reassurance.” Neutropenic fever gets an examination for a source and prompt antipseudomonal coverage. The exact drug is your hospital’s protocol, built on IDSA.

## Checklist

- [ ] Source, severity, then drug.
- [ ] Cultures before antibiotics when minutes do not matter. Antibiotics before a delayed lumbar puncture.
- [ ] Drain pus.
- [ ] One drug is never a complete regimen for active tuberculosis.
- [ ] Doxycycline for suspected Rocky Mountain spotted fever, including children.

## Common traps

- A broad carbapenem for a simple cellulitis because it feels thorough. Spectrum is a side effect.
- Waiting for the CT to treat meningitis.
- Metronidazole as the default for C. difficile because that is what an older book said.
- A feed card with a CD4 cutoff from five years ago. Open the current opportunistic-infection guideline.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Infection items are won by the syndrome table above. When you miss one, write the organism class you failed to cover and why that host needed it. Feed cards that name a single first-line drug should be dated. If the card has no year, do not trust the drug name without the IDSA or CDC page.
