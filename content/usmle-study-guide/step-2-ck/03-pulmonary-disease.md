# Pulmonary disease

> **Study aid only.** Next step and the guideline that owns it. Ventilator manipulation for a crashing patient is a supervised skill.

## What this chapter covers

The outpatient and emergency decisions in asthma, COPD, pulmonary embolism, and pneumonia. Respiratory content is about 5–10% of Step 2 CK. The mechanism of gas exchange was Step 1. This chapter is what you do.

## The framework

Shortness of breath is the same first question as chest pain. Is the airway open, is oxygenation failing, is ventilation failing? Oxygen for hypoxemia. Noninvasive ventilation when the COPD exacerbation is acidotic and tiring, if no contraindication. Intubation when those fail or the airway is not protected. The rest of the chapter assumes you have already done that.

| Problem | Decision | Source to trust |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asthma, any persistent symptoms | Do not treat with a short-acting beta agonist alone. Use an inhaled corticosteroid–containing regimen. | GINA strategy report, current year |
| Asthma exacerbation | Oxygen as needed, repeated bronchodilator, systemic corticosteroid. Magnesium and escalation when severe. | GINA and the emergency chapter |
| COPD maintenance | Group by symptoms and exacerbations, not by FEV1 alone. Long-acting bronchodilators are the base. Inhaled steroids are for higher exacerbation risk, not for everyone. | GOLD, current report |
| COPD exacerbation | Bronchodilators, steroid, antibiotic when sputum is more purulent or ventilation is needed, noninvasive ventilation for respiratory acidosis. | GOLD |
| Suspected PE, unstable | Treat and image. Do not send an unstable person to the CT scanner alone. | AHA / ESC PE guidance |
| Suspected PE, stable | Gestalt first. PERC only if gestalt is already low. Otherwise D-dimer if PE is unlikely, CT pulmonary angiography if likely. | ACEP and specialty PE guidance |
| Community pneumonia | Severity (PSI or CURB-65) plus judgment decides the site of care. Cover the typical and atypical organisms appropriate to the ward versus the ICU. | IDSA/ATS community-acquired pneumonia guideline |
| Light’s criteria | Pleural protein and LDH ratios separate exudate from transudate. An exudate needs a cause: infection, cancer, embolus, inflammation. | Standard pulmonary teaching; confirm a borderline case with the full criteria |

Smoking cessation is the disease-modifying treatment in COPD. Inhalers are symptom and exacerbation tools. Long-term oxygen is for chronic hypoxemia meeting criteria, not for breathlessness with adequate saturation. Confirm the saturation threshold in the current CMS/ATS oxygen guidance before you quote a number. A commonly taught threshold is a resting saturation at or below 88%. Flag it and check it.

Lung cancer screening is not a pulmonary-clinic aside. USPSTF recommends annual low-dose CT for adults 50 to 80 with at least a 20 pack-year history who smoke now or quit within 15 years. That lives again in the prevention chapter. Do not start screening someone outside that frame because they cough.

## High-yield atlas

Pulmonary items on Step 2 CK are a stability decision, then the syndrome, then the one action that changes today. Say whether the patient can leave the monitored bed. A normal carbon dioxide in a tiring patient is not reassuring. The framework above is the map. This atlas is the set.

| Presentation and findings | Next best step | First-line management and the source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daytime symptoms most days, night waking, and a reliever as the only inhaler. | Start an inhaled corticosteroid-containing regimen. Do not refill the reliever as the whole plan. | GINA. Short-acting beta agonist monotherapy is not maintenance. The preferred track uses an inhaled corticosteroid with formoterol. Send a written action plan with the inhaler. |
| Speaks in words, a silent chest, falling saturation, and a normal or rising carbon dioxide. | Oxygen, repeated bronchodilator, early systemic corticosteroid, ipratropium, and magnesium if severe and not turning. Prepare to intubate. Do not send for a chest film first. | GINA. The oxygen saturation target is about 93 to 95 percent. A normal carbon dioxide in a tiring patient is impending failure, not reassurance. |
| A long smoking history, dyspnea on flat ground, and either two exacerbations last year or one hospitalization. | A long-acting dual bronchodilator. Add an inhaled corticosteroid when exacerbations are frequent and the eosinophil count supports it. Treat tobacco dependence. | GOLD. Group by symptoms and exacerbations, not by FEV1 alone. The eosinophil count GOLD has used to favor an inhaled corticosteroid is about 300 cells per microliter. Confirm the current report. Smoking cessation, with pharmacotherapy, is the disease-modifying step. USPSTF supports that offer. |
| Known smoking-related obstruction, purulent sputum, sleepiness, pH at or below 7.35, and a raised carbon dioxide. | Controlled oxygen, nebulized bronchodilators, a systemic corticosteroid, an antibiotic because the sputum is purulent or ventilation is needed, and noninvasive ventilation if no contraindication. | GOLD. The usual oxygen target is a saturation of 88 to 92 percent. Noninvasive ventilation is for respiratory acidosis when the airway is protected. Intubate if he is vomiting, unconscious, or failing the noninvasive trial. |
| A stable outpatient whose resting saturation is at or below 88 percent, measured when he is not in a flare. | Confirm the saturation or the blood gas on optimal treatment, while stable. Then prescribe long-term oxygen. | GOLD and the CMS long-term oxygen criteria. The commonly taught threshold is a resting saturation at or below 88 percent, or a PaO2 at or below 55 mm Hg. Pulmonary hypertension or erythrocytosis can qualify a slightly higher value. Confirm the current criteria. Oxygen is not a treatment for breathlessness when the saturation is adequate. |
| Cough, a new infiltrate, and fever or hypothermia. Older adults may not mount a fever. | A severity score plus judgment decides the site of care. In severe disease, draw blood cultures if doing so does not delay the antibiotic. | IDSA/ATS. PSI is preferred for the site of care. CURB-65 is acceptable: confusion, BUN greater than 19 mg/dL, respiratory rate at least 30, systolic pressure under 90 or diastolic at or under 60, age at least 65. One major criterion, mechanical ventilation or vasopressor shock, or three minor criteria, is severe disease. Do not add routine anaerobic coverage for suspected aspiration. Confirm the current regimen. Do not invent a drug list. |
| Sudden collapse or pleuritic pain, hypotension, clear lungs, and right-heart strain. | Resuscitate. Bedside ultrasound if he is too unstable to travel. Thrombolysis if there is no contraindication. Do not send him to the scanner alone. | ESC and AHA pulmonary embolism guidance. Systemic thrombolysis is for hypotension without a high bleeding risk. A procedure is for thrombolysis that cannot be given or has failed. Anticoagulation alone is the stable pathway. |
| Pleuritic pain, a clear chest film, a normal blood pressure, and a risk such as recent surgery, cancer, or a swollen leg. | Gestalt first. Use PERC only if gestalt is already low. Otherwise a D-dimer if unlikely, and CT pulmonary angiography if likely. | ACEP and ESC. PERC requires every item negative: age under 50, heart rate under 100, saturation at least 95 percent, no hemoptysis, no estrogen, no prior clot, no unilateral leg swelling, no recent surgery or trauma. Anticoagulate a confirmed clot. A direct oral anticoagulant is first-line for many patients without cancer, pregnancy, or severe kidney disease. Confirm the current CHEST choice. Pregnancy is not a PERC population. |
| A pneumonia that is not improving, plus pus, organisms on stain, or a pleural fluid pH under 7.2. | Sample under ultrasound if you have not. Then drain. Do not wait for the culture to grow. | British Thoracic Society pleural guideline and IDSA/ATS. Antibiotics alone do not treat an empyema. Light criteria still mark an exudate: protein ratio over 0.5, LDH ratio over 0.6, or pleural LDH over two-thirds the serum upper limit. A responding heart-failure effusion is a transudate and is not a chest tube. |
| Absent breath sounds on one side, trachea pushed the other way, distended neck veins, and hypotension. | Decompress the chest now. A tube follows. Do not wait for a radiograph. | ATLS and the British Thoracic Society. Unstable air under pressure is a clinical diagnosis. Imaging is for the stable leak. |
| A tall young adult, sudden pleuritic pain, a pleural line, normal blood pressure, and minimal breathlessness. | Oxygen and observation are acceptable when symptoms are minimal. Drain if he is breathless. Do not treat one centimeter as universal. | British Thoracic Society pleural disease guideline. A minimally breathless primary leak can be managed conservatively. Confirm the size cutoff in the current BTS or ACCP document. A secondary leak, especially with severe obstructive lung disease, is not observed the same way. |
| Sepsis, aspiration, or another risk, bilateral infiltrates within a week, hypoxemia on positive pressure, and no good argument that the left heart is the cause. | Lower the tidal volume to lung-protective targets. Treat the cause. Once shock has resolved, keep fluids conservative. | ATS/ESICM and the ARDSNet trial. Tidal volume about 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, not actual weight. Plateau pressure at or under 30 cm H2O. Prone positioning for severe hypoxemia. Steroid use follows the current ATS/ESICM file. Do not invent a high-dose pulse. |
| Loud snoring, witnessed pauses, daytime sleepiness, a thick neck, and hypertension that is hard to treat. | A sleep study. Use the laboratory if there is significant cardiopulmonary disease, neuromuscular disease, or suspected hypoventilation. A home test is acceptable when probability is high and those are absent. | American Academy of Sleep Medicine. Positive airway pressure is first-line for moderate to severe disease. Weight loss helps. Nocturnal oxygen is not a substitute. Tell anesthesia before elective surgery. |
| Age over 60, progressive dyspnea, Velcro crackles, clubbing, and basal subpleural honeycombing, with no exposure that explains it. | A high-resolution CT read for a usual interstitial pattern. Biopsy only if the pattern is not definite and he can tolerate it. Refer for an antifibrotic and a transplant discussion. | ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis guideline. Nintedanib or pirfenidone. Do not use prednisone plus azathioprine plus N-acetylcysteine. That combination caused harm. Add oxygen if he meets hypoxemia criteria. |
| Cough for days to three weeks, clear lungs, normal vital signs, and a well-appearing adult. | No antibiotic and no chest film when the exam and the vital signs are reassuring. Tell him how long the cough can last. | CDC and ACP. Uncomplicated acute bronchitis is viral. Purulent sputum is not an antibiotic indication. The exception is suspected pertussis: paroxysms, post-tussive vomiting, or a known exposure, which is tested and treated. |
| Enough blood from the airway to threaten ventilation, often with bronchiectasis, cancer, or a cavity. | Protect the airway. Keep the bleeding side down if you know which side. Urgent bronchoscopy or bronchial-artery embolization. Do not send an unstable patient to CT alone. | The airway-first pathway used in chest and interventional practice. A teaspoon of blood-streaked sputum in a stable patient is a chest film and a cancer or infection evaluation, not an intubation. Definitions of massive bleeding differ. Airway compromise decides, not a milliliter argument. |
| A small solid opacity on a CT done for another reason, in an adult without known cancer. | Compare with old images. Then use the Fleischner table. Do not biopsy a tiny low-risk opacity. | Fleischner Society 2017. A solid opacity under 6 mm in a low-risk patient does not need routine follow-up. A larger opacity is followed. A suspicious large one is PET or tissue, not a one-year wait. Fleischner does not apply to screening scans, age under 35, known cancer, or immunocompromise. Screening is USPSTF and Lung-RADS: ages 50 to 80, at least 20 pack-years, current smoker or quit within 15 years, annual low-dose CT. |
| Bilateral hilar adenopathy with erythema nodosum, migratory joint pain, and fever, or a less classic infiltrative film. | The acute skin, joint, and hilar pattern in a well patient usually does not need a biopsy. Examine the eyes. Do not start prednisone for asymptomatic nodes and normal lung function. | ATS sarcoidosis practice guideline. Stage I disease without symptoms can be observed. Corticosteroids are for threatening lung, eye, heart, neurologic, or calcium disease. Hypercalcemia comes from granulomatous calcitriol. Uveitis can be silent, so the eye exam is not optional. |

| --- | --- |
| Undertreated persistent asthma versus the neighbor | Vocal cord dysfunction is throat tightness, often with normal spirometry between episodes and without night waking from wheeze. It is not treated with an inhaled corticosteroid as the disease-modifying drug. |
| Life-threatening asthma versus the neighbor | A patient who speaks in sentences, improves after the first bronchodilator treatments, and has a rising peak flow can be observed and discharged on a corticosteroid with follow-up. |
| Exacerbation-prone COPD versus the neighbor | The same obstruction with little breathlessness and no exacerbation in the past year needs a bronchodilator and tobacco treatment, not an inhaled corticosteroid by default. |
| Hypercapnic COPD exacerbation versus the neighbor | More dyspnea with a normal pH and the ability to speak is bronchodilators, a corticosteroid, and an antibiotic if sputum is purulent, usually without a ventilator. |
| COPD with an oxygen indication versus the neighbor | Breathlessness with a resting saturation of 95 percent is an inhaler and conditioning problem, not a home oxygen prescription. |
| Community-acquired pneumonia versus the neighbor | A reliable 40-year-old who is eating, saturating well, and has a low severity score can take oral therapy at home. |
| Massive pulmonary embolism versus the neighbor | Right-heart strain with a normal blood pressure is intermediate risk. Anticoagulate and watch. Routine thrombolysis is not the plan. |
| Stable suspected pulmonary embolism versus the neighbor | A young adult with a brief twinge, no risk, normal vital signs, and a truly low gestalt can stop if PERC is entirely negative. |
| Complicated parapneumonic effusion versus the neighbor | A small free-flowing effusion with a pH over 7.2, a negative stain, and a patient who is improving can be watched. Bilateral effusions that shrink as heart failure is treated do not need a tube. |
| Tension pneumothorax versus the neighbor | A small leak in a comfortable patient with a normal blood pressure is imaged and often observed. The same film with shock is not observed. |
| Primary spontaneous pneumothorax versus the neighbor | Tracheal shift and a pressure of 80 are decompressed before the film is admired. Breathlessness, or a background of severe lung disease, moves you off observation. |
| Acute respiratory distress syndrome versus the neighbor | Cardiogenic edema has a full left heart and responds to unloading. It does not get a low-tidal-volume recipe as the treatment of the heart. |
| Obstructive sleep apnea versus the neighbor | Narcolepsy is sleep attacks and cataplexy without obstructive pauses. Obesity hypoventilation has daytime hypercapnia and needs laboratory testing. |
| Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis versus the neighbor | Hypersensitivity pneumonitis has an exposure, often mid or upper lung fibrosis, and removal of the antigen comes first. Sarcoidosis is hilar adenopathy and a different steroid decision. |
| Acute bronchitis versus the neighbor | Fever, a focal crackle, and hypoxia are a pneumonic infiltrate until the film says otherwise. A cough longer than eight weeks is no longer this acute illness. |
| Life-threatening airway bleeding versus the neighbor | Blood-streaked sputum in a stable smoker is a cancer pathway: a film, then a CT. It is not an endotracheal tube. |
| Incidental pulmonary nodule versus the neighbor | A new 2 cm spiculated opacity in a 40-pack-year smoker is a diagnostic pathway, not a one-year wait. A screening low-dose CT is read with Lung-RADS, not Fleischner. |
| Pulmonary sarcoidosis versus the neighbor | Night sweats, a cavity, and a positive sputum are tuberculosis: isolation and combination therapy, never one drug. Syncope or heart block is cardiac involvement and is not observed. |

### Undertreated persistent asthma

The airway is inflamed. A bronchodilator alone does not treat the inflammation.

**Vignette.** A 26-year-old uses an albuterol inhaler every day and wakes coughing twice a week. No controller is listed.
**Reasoning.** Daily reliever use is ongoing airway inflammation. Another canister leaves that inflammation untreated. The diagnosis is Undertreated persistent asthma.
**NBME trap.** A theophylline level, or another reliever inhaler. Neither changes the plan.
**Hook.** No reliever-only regimen.

### Life-threatening asthma

Airflow is so limited that ventilation is failing. The patient should be blowing carbon dioxide down. A normal value means they no longer can.

**Vignette.** A 19-year-old can say only one word at a time. The chest is quiet. Saturation is 90 percent on air. Arterial carbon dioxide is 40 mm Hg.
**Reasoning.** A quiet chest and a carbon dioxide that is not low mean the airway is closing and the drive is tiring. Treatment starts before imaging. The diagnosis is Life-threatening asthma.
**NBME trap.** A chest radiograph, or a blood gas, used as a reason to delay steroids because the carbon dioxide looks normal.
**Hook.** A normal carbon dioxide here is a warning.

### Exacerbation-prone COPD

Fixed obstruction plus exacerbations. The eosinophil count changes the inhaler. A sputum culture at a stable visit does not.

**Vignette.** A 64-year-old former smoker is breathless walking across a room. He was admitted once last winter. He uses only an albuterol inhaler. The eosinophil count is 320 cells per microliter.
**Reasoning.** Symptoms plus a hospitalization are the frequent-exacerbation pattern. Dual long-acting bronchodilation is the base, and this eosinophil count is the usual signal to add an inhaled corticosteroid. The diagnosis is Exacerbation-prone COPD.
**NBME trap.** An inhaled corticosteroid for every low FEV1, or a sputum culture at a stable clinic visit.
**Hook.** Symptoms and exacerbations pick the inhaler.

### Hypercapnic COPD exacerbation

He is not clearing carbon dioxide. Uncontrolled oxygen can raise it further.

**Vignette.** A 70-year-old with a 50-pack-year history is sleepy. Sputum is thick and green. pH is 7.28. Arterial carbon dioxide is 70 mm Hg. Saturation is 86 percent. He protects his airway and is not vomiting.
**Reasoning.** Acidosis in a tiring patient with a protected airway is noninvasive ventilation, not a high-flow face mask on a ward. The diagnosis is Hypercapnic COPD exacerbation.
**NBME trap.** High-concentration oxygen because the saturation looked low, or intubation of a cooperative acidemic patient before a noninvasive trial.
**Hook.** Acidemic, tiring, airway protected: noninvasive ventilation.

### COPD with an oxygen indication

Chronic hypoxemia is the mortality signal the oxygen trials treated. Dyspnea alone is not that signal.

**Vignette.** Weeks after any flare, a 72-year-old already on long-acting bronchodilators has a resting saturation of 86 percent on room air at two visits.
**Reasoning.** The indication is resting hypoxemia while stable. Starting oxygen in a flare and never rechecking, or refusing it when the stable saturation is this low, are the two errors. The diagnosis is COPD with an oxygen indication.
**NBME trap.** A walk test ordered before anyone records the resting saturation that already qualifies, or oxygen prescribed for dyspnea with a normal saturation.
**Hook.** Resting hypoxemia when stable. Not breathlessness alone.

### Community-acquired pneumonia

The decision is mortality risk and whether he can drink, not a favorite antibiotic name.

**Vignette.** A 74-year-old is confused. Respiratory rate is 32. Blood pressure is 88/50. BUN is 28 mg/dL. There is a new lobar infiltrate. He is not yet intubated.
**Reasoning.** Age, confusion, tachypnea, a high BUN, and shock-range pressure are a high score and a hospital patient. The pressure plus the other features argue for a higher level of care. A low score in a patient who cannot drink still does not go home. The diagnosis is Community-acquired pneumonia.
**NBME trap.** A procalcitonin or a sputum culture used to send a hypoxic, confused patient home. Or anaerobic coverage added only because he might have aspirated.
**Hook.** The score plus the person. Not a reflex drug name.

### Massive pulmonary embolism

Clot is obstructing the pulmonary circuit and the right ventricle is failing.

**Vignette.** One week after a hip replacement, a 58-year-old collapses. Blood pressure is 78/40. Neck veins are up. The chest is clear. The monitor shows a new right-heart strain pattern.
**Reasoning.** Hypotension, a clear chest, and a clot setting are a reperfusion decision. A probability score and a D-dimer do not belong here. A scan that delays treatment in a patient this unstable is the miss. The diagnosis is Massive pulmonary embolism.
**NBME trap.** PERC, a D-dimer, or heparin alone as the complete plan while the pressure is 78.
**Hook.** Low pressure means reperfusion, not a pretest score.

### Stable suspected pulmonary embolism

Pretest probability decides which test can change the plan. A D-dimer in a high-probability patient does not.

**Vignette.** Three weeks after pelvic surgery, a 68-year-old has pleuritic pain and one swollen calf. Blood pressure is 128/76. Saturation is 96 percent. The chest film is clear.
**Reasoning.** Recent surgery and a swollen leg make the probability high, and age alone fails PERC. She goes to CT pulmonary angiography. A postoperative D-dimer will be positive and will not decide anything. The diagnosis is Stable suspected pulmonary embolism.
**NBME trap.** PERC applied because the blood pressure is normal, or a D-dimer in a high-probability postoperative patient.
**Hook.** PERC is only for a low gestalt.

### Complicated parapneumonic effusion

Infected pleural fluid is a closed space, the same logic as an undrained abscess.

**Vignette.** After three days of antibiotics for a lobar infiltrate, he is still febrile. Fluid from a layering effusion has a pH of 7.05, and the gram stain shows organisms.
**Reasoning.** The pH and the organisms mean the space needs a tube today. Another day of the same antibiotic, without drainage, does not change the outcome. The diagnosis is Complicated parapneumonic effusion.
**NBME trap.** Waiting for the culture, or a CT, before draining a pH of 7.05. Or tubing a transudate from heart failure.
**Hook.** Pus, organisms, or pH under 7.2: drain.

### Tension pneumothorax

Pleural pressure collapses the lung and obstructs venous return.

**Vignette.** After a central-line attempt, blood pressure falls to 70. Neck veins rise. The right chest is silent and the trachea is left of midline.
**Reasoning.** Unilateral silence, a shifted trachea, and shock after a chest procedure are air under pressure. A film would document a problem you already have to treat. The diagnosis is Tension pneumothorax.
**NBME trap.** A chest radiograph or a CT before decompression of a crashing patient.
**Hook.** Silence, shift, and shock: decompress before you film.

### Primary spontaneous pneumothorax

A bleb leaked into an otherwise healthy chest. Tension physiology is a different disease.

**Vignette.** A tall, thin 22-year-old has sudden left chest pain. He speaks in full sentences. Blood pressure is 120/70. Saturation is 97 percent. The film shows a small pleural line and no tracheal shift.
**Reasoning.** He is stable and minimally breathless, so this is observation. It is not a rush to a tube and it is not a tension pathway. The diagnosis is Primary spontaneous pneumothorax.
**NBME trap.** A CT to be sure in a stable patient whose film and exam already allow observation, or an emergency tube for a comfortable patient.
**Hook.** Comfortable and stable can be watched. Breathless or shifted cannot.

### Acute respiratory distress syndrome

Noncardiogenic edema and stiff lungs. A large tidal volume injures what is left.

**Vignette.** A patient intubated for septic shock has white lungs on day two. PaO2/FiO2 is 110 on a positive end-expiratory pressure of 10. Ultrasound does not show a failing left heart. Tidal volume is set at 12 mL per kilogram of actual body weight.
**Reasoning.** Timing, bilateral infiltrates, and hypoxemia without left-heart failure meet the Berlin pattern. That tidal volume is far above 6 mL/kg predicted body weight. The change that matters today is the ventilator, plus treatment of the sepsis. The diagnosis is Acute respiratory distress syndrome.
**NBME trap.** A pulmonary-artery catheter or a BNP used as a reason to delay the tidal-volume change. Or a diuretic as the only order while the tidal volume stays large.
**Hook.** Six milliliters per kilogram of predicted weight.

### Obstructive sleep apnea

The upper airway collapses in sleep. The blood pressure and the crashes are downstream.

**Vignette.** A 52-year-old falls asleep in a chair. His partner has watched him stop breathing. His neck is large. Blood pressure stays high on three drugs. He has no heart failure and no neuromuscular disease.
**Reasoning.** Probability is high and nothing forbids a home study. Once the study confirms obstruction, the treatment is positive airway pressure, not a stimulant and not oxygen alone. The diagnosis is Obstructive sleep apnea.
**NBME trap.** A stimulant, or overnight oxygen, before a sleep study. Or a home test in severe heart failure with suspected hypoventilation.
**Hook.** Pauses and sleepiness: a study, then positive airway pressure.

### Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Fibrotic usual interstitial pneumonia without a cause. Immunosuppression is the wrong disease-modifying plan.

**Vignette.** A 68-year-old has a year of worsening dyspnea and a dry cough. He has clubbing and bibasilar Velcro crackles. CT shows subpleural honeycombing, worse at the bases. He keeps no birds and has no mold or metal exposure.
**Reasoning.** Age, clubbing, basal honeycombing, and no antigen are this fibrotic pattern. The drug class is an antifibrotic. A chronic prednisone trial is the old error. The diagnosis is Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.
**NBME trap.** A surgical biopsy when the CT is already definite, or chronic prednisone as disease-modifying therapy.
**Hook.** Basal honeycombing: an antifibrotic, not prednisone.

### Acute bronchitis

Airway inflammation after a virus. The film is clear because the alveoli are not filled.

**Vignette.** A healthy 30-year-old has coughed for five days. Sputum is yellow. Temperature is 37.4. Saturation is 99 percent. The lungs are clear.
**Reasoning.** A well adult with normal oxygenation and a clear exam does not have a bacterial pneumonia. Yellow sputum is inflammation. Azithromycin does not change the course. The diagnosis is Acute bronchitis.
**NBME trap.** A chest radiograph, a sputum culture, or azithromycin. None of them changes the correct plan in this well adult.
**Hook.** Clear lungs and normal vitals: no antibiotic.

### Life-threatening airway bleeding

Bronchial arteries, not the low-pressure pulmonary arteries, are the usual source of brisk bleeding.

**Vignette.** A 60-year-old with known bronchiectasis is coughing up cupfuls of bright blood and can barely speak. Saturation is 88 percent. One side is clearly worse on exam.
**Reasoning.** The airway is the emergency. The bleeding side stays down so the good lung stays open, and the people who can bronchoscope or embolize are called now. A CT before the airway is secure lets the good lung drown. The diagnosis is Life-threatening airway bleeding.
**NBME trap.** Laying the bleeding side up, or a CT angiogram as the first stop while he cannot protect the airway.
**Hook.** Cups of blood: bad lung down, then embolize.

### Incidental pulmonary nodule

Most small incidental opacities are benign. The interval avoids both a missed cancer and a needless resection.

**Vignette.** A 48-year-old who never smoked has a CT for suspected kidney stones. The report describes a 4 mm solid rounded opacity. Films from two years ago look the same. He has no cancer history.
**Reasoning.** Low risk, under 6 mm, and stable. No PET, no biopsy, and no routine follow-up. A PET of a 4 mm opacity cannot change management. The diagnosis is Incidental pulmonary nodule.
**NBME trap.** PET or biopsy of a 4 mm low-risk opacity, or Fleischner intervals applied to a screening examination.
**Hook.** Under 6 mm and low risk: do not needle it.

### Pulmonary sarcoidosis

Noncaseating granulomas. The acute pattern is clinical. The heart and the eye are the organs you do not ignore.

**Vignette.** A 34-year-old has tender red pretibial nodules, ankle pain, and fever. The chest film shows bilateral hilar adenopathy. Spirometry is normal. Calcium is normal.
**Reasoning.** The shin nodules, the joints, and the hilar nodes are the acute pattern. She needs an eye evaluation. She does not need mediastinoscopy or prednisone for asymptomatic nodes and normal spirometry. The diagnosis is Pulmonary sarcoidosis.
**NBME trap.** A mediastinal biopsy, or empiric prednisone, when nothing threatening is present. Or skipping the eye exam because she has no eye complaint.
**Hook.** The acute pattern can be watched. The eye still gets a look.

## Checklist

- [ ] No SABA-only asthma treatment.
- [ ] COPD inhalers follow symptoms and exacerbations.
- [ ] Unstable PE is a resuscitation, not a PERC item.
- [ ] PERC is only for low gestalt.
- [ ] Site of care for pneumonia is a severity tool plus the person in front of you.

## Common traps

- Steroids for every wheeze forever. COPD and asthma do not have the same inhaled-steroid indication.
- A negative D-dimer “ruling out” PE when the pretest probability was high. D-dimer is for the unlikely group.
- Waiting for a fever to call pneumonia. Older adults may not mount one.
- Quoting an antibiotic by habit from medical school. The IDSA/ATS regimen depends on severity and on local resistance. Check the current table.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Pulmonary misses are usually “sent them for a test while unstable” or “used the wrong severity tool.” Feed cards on GOLD groups go stale when GOLD revises the letters. Learn the principle (symptoms plus exacerbations) so a renamed group does not break you.
