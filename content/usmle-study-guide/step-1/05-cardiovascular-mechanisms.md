# Cardiovascular mechanisms

> **Study aid only.** Pump physiology and the lesion that explains the murmur or the pressure tracing. Drug choice for a living patient is in the Step 2 and Step 3 books.

## What this chapter covers

The cardiac cycle, shock as a pattern, heart-failure physiology, and the murmurs that move with maneuvers. Cardiovascular is about 7–11% of Step 1. The questions are physiology wearing a clinical costume.

## The framework

![Shock: pump, tank, or pipes](visuals/shock-patterns.svg)

Stroke volume depends on preload, afterload, and contractility. Cardiac output is stroke volume times rate. Blood pressure is cardiac output times systemic vascular resistance. Every shock and every drug in this chapter is a change in one of those terms.

| Pattern | Cardiac output | Vascular resistance | A clue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hypovolemic | Down | Up | Flat neck veins, cold skin |
| Cardiogenic | Down | Up | Distended neck veins, pulmonary edema |
| Obstructive | Down | Up | Tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive embolus |
| Distributive | Up, until the heart fails | Down | Warm skin. Sepsis, anaphylaxis |

Tamponade equalizes diastolic pressures and drops the y descent. The heart cannot fill. Pulsus paradoxus is an exaggerated inspiratory drop in systolic pressure. Constrictive pericarditis is the chronic cousin, with a sharp y descent. Do not merge them.

## The cycle and the murmurs

S1 is mitral and tricuspid closure. S2 is aortic and pulmonic closure. Inspiration widens the split of S2 because the right heart has more preload. A paradoxical split (P2 then A2, narrowing on inspiration) means the aortic valve is delayed: bundle branch block or severe aortic stenosis.

Maneuvers change preload or afterload. Most murmurs get louder with more blood. Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy and mitral valve prolapse get softer with more preload (squat, passive leg raise) and louder when preload falls (Valsalva, stand). That opposite behavior is the whole question.

| Lesion | When you hear it | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aortic stenosis | Systolic, crescendo-decrescendo | Carotids. Pulsus parvus et tardus when severe. |
| Mitral regurgitation | Holosystolic | Axilla. A click and a late murmur is prolapse, not a fixed hole. |
| Aortic regurgitation | Early diastolic | Wide pulse pressure. The ventricle sees both forward stroke volume and the leak. |
| Mitral stenosis | Diastolic rumble, opening snap | Left atrial pressure up. A closer snap means a tighter valve. |
| VSD | Holosystolic | Smaller defects are louder. Eisenmenger is the late reversal when pulmonary pressure wins. |
| PDA | Continuous | Machinery. Closes functionally when oxygen rises after birth. |

## Failure as backup and forward flow

Left heart failure backs into the lungs: dyspnea, orthopnea, edema of the lung. Right heart failure backs into the body: edema, liver, jugular veins. Forward failure is fatigue, cool skin, and low urine. Chronic left failure becomes right failure because the lung vessels are the right heart’s afterload.

Ischemia is supply versus demand. Atherosclerosis is the usual supply problem. Anemia, hypoxia, and tachycardia are demand problems. Troponin means myocyte membrane damage. It does not, by itself, say why.

## High-yield atlas

Step 1 cardiology is a tracing, a murmur, or a complication calendar. Name the normal event, then the one thing that changed. The feed cards are single associations. This atlas is the map those associations sit on.

### The cycle, read off the tracing

One heartbeat is filling, contraction, ejection, and relaxation. The wiggers-style tracing is that sentence drawn. S1 is the mitral and tricuspid valves closing because ventricular pressure just passed atrial pressure. S2 is the aortic and pulmonic valves closing because ventricular pressure just fell below arterial pressure. The split of S2 is the right heart taking a little longer. Inspiration increases right-heart preload, so the split widens. A split that gets narrower on inspiration means the aortic valve is the slow one: bundle-branch block or severe aortic stenosis. That is called paradoxical because it is the opposite of the normal respiratory movement.

| Moment on the tracing | What just happened | What a stem uses it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a wave of the jugular venous pressure | Atrial contraction into the ventricle | A giant a wave means the atrium is contracting against a closed or stiff right ventricle (stenosis, hypertrophy, a junctional beat) |
| c wave | The tricuspid valve bulging as the ventricle contracts | Rarely the whole question. Do not invent a disease from a c wave alone |
| x descent | Atrial relaxation and the valve being pulled down | Lost x descent is a tamponade clue when the y descent is also blunted |
| v wave | Atrial filling while the valve is closed | A giant v wave is regurgitation back into the atrium |
| y descent | The valve opens and the atrium empties | Tamponade blunts it. Constriction makes it sharp and deep |
| S3 | Rapid filling into a dilated, compliant ventricle | Volume overload or a failing dilated ventricle. Can be normal in a young healthy person or in pregnancy |
| S4 | Atrium pushing blood into a stiff ventricle | Pressure overload, ischemia, hypertrophy. It needs a sinus beat. Atrial fibrillation cannot make an S4 |

Isovolumetric contraction is the short time when both sets of valves are closed and pressure rises with no change in volume. Isovolumetric relaxation is the same idea after ejection. A question that says “volume is constant while pressure rises” is isovolumetric contraction, not ejection.

**Vignette.** A 24-year-old woman in the third trimester has a soft early-diastolic sound at the apex. She has no dyspnea at rest. The neck veins are not distended.
**Reasoning.** An early filling sound in a young, high-output, volume-expanded person is often a normal S3. The trap is calling it heart failure because the stem says “diastolic sound,” or calling it mitral stenosis because it is at the apex. Stenosis would bring an opening snap and a rumble, and usually a longer story of dyspnea. The diagnosis is a physiologic third heart sound until the stem adds congestion.

**NBME trap.** They describe the respiratory movement of a split and ask which valve is delayed. Students pick the valve on the side of the louder sound. The question is about timing, not loudness.

**Hook.** Inspiration widens a normal split. If inspiration narrows it, the aortic valve is late.

### Murmurs as experiments on preload and afterload

Most murmurs get louder when more blood crosses the lesion. Two lesions do the opposite: hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy and mitral valve prolapse. More preload (squat, passive leg raise, handgrip for some lesions) fills the ventricle, the outflow tract or the prolapsing leaflet is less slack, and those two murmurs get softer. Less preload (standing, Valsalva strain) does the reverse. Handgrip raises afterload. Aortic regurgitation and mitral regurgitation get louder because more blood leaks backward against a higher pressure. Hypertrophic obstruction gets softer because the larger ventricle is less obstructed.

| Lesion | When | Where it goes | The maneuver that separates it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aortic stenosis | Systolic, crescendo-decrescendo, often a soft S2 when severe | Carotids | Pulsus parvus et tardus. Squatting makes it louder, unlike the obstructive cardiomyopathy murmur |
| Hypertrophic obstruction | Systolic, left sternal border, often with a bifid pulse | Does not radiate to the carotids the way stenosis does | Softer with more preload or more afterload. Louder with Valsalva |
| Mitral regurgitation | Holosystolic | Axilla | Louder with handgrip. A click plus a late murmur is prolapse, not a fixed hole |
| Mitral valve prolapse | Mid-systolic click, then a murmur | Apex | The click moves earlier when preload falls. Students call every click a stenosis snap |
| Ventricular septal defect | Holosystolic, often harsh | Left sternal border | Smaller holes are louder. A huge hole with pulmonary hypertension can get quiet |
| Aortic regurgitation | Early diastolic, blowing | Left sternal border, sitting forward | Wide pulse pressure. Head bobbing and bounding pulses are the leak, not a separate disease |
| Mitral stenosis | Opening snap, then a diastolic rumble | Apex, in left lateral decubitus | A closer snap means a higher left-atrial pressure and a tighter valve |
| Patent ductus arteriosus | Continuous “machinery” | Left infraclavicular | It does not stop at S2. A venous hum also continues, but it changes with the neck and with lying down |
| Atrial septal defect | Fixed split S2, often a soft pulmonary flow murmur | Upper left sternal border | The split does not move with respiration. That is the lesion, not the soft murmur |
| Innocent flow | Soft systolic, no thrill, normal S2 | Changes with position | A normal examination otherwise. Do not call it stenosis because the child is anxious |

**Vignette.** A 19-year-old collapses while sprinting. A systolic murmur at the left sternal border gets louder when he stands and softer when he squats. The carotid upstroke is brisk, not delayed.
**Reasoning.** Exertional collapse plus a murmur that softens when the ventricle fills is dynamic obstruction, not fixed aortic stenosis. Fixed stenosis would have a delayed carotid upstroke and would get louder, not softer, with the squat. The diagnosis is hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. The trap is “aortic stenosis” because both are systolic and both can cause syncope.

**NBME trap.** They say “the murmur gets longer” or “the click moves” instead of “gets louder.” For prolapse, less preload makes the click earlier and the murmur longer. Loudness alone is the wrong variable.

**Hook.** Only two famous murmurs hate a full ventricle: hypertrophic obstruction and prolapse.

### Pressure overload versus volume overload

The ventricle remodels to the load you give it. Pressure (aortic stenosis, hypertension) adds sarcomeres in parallel: a thick wall and a normal cavity. Volume (aortic or mitral regurgitation, a long-standing shunt) adds sarcomeres in series: a larger cavity. Laplace’s relation is why a bigger cavity needs more wall tension to make the same pressure. Dilation is expensive. Hypertrophy is the attempt to pay that bill.

| Load | Shape | The clinical consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chronic pressure | Concentric hypertrophy | Diastolic stiffness, an S4, a higher filling pressure for the same volume |
| Chronic volume | Eccentric hypertrophy | A dilated chamber, an S3, eventual systolic failure |
| Acute regurgitation | No time to remodel | Pulmonary edema at a normal cavity size. The left atrium has not stretched, so pressure spikes |

**Vignette.** A 70-year-old man has had a blowing diastolic murmur for years and a blood pressure of 160/50. The chest film shows a large heart. He is comfortable at rest.
**Reasoning.** The wide pulse pressure is the aortic valve leaking: diastolic pressure falls, stroke volume rises, systolic pressure rises. Years of volume allow the ventricle to enlarge and stay compensated. The diagnosis is chronic severe aortic regurgitation. The trap is shock, because the diastolic pressure is 50. Shock would look ill. This man is comfortable, and the low diastolic pressure is the lesion.

**Pair.** Acute aortic regurgitation (endocarditis, dissection) is a small ventricle and flash edema. Chronic aortic regurgitation is a big ventricle and a wide pulse pressure. Same valve, different clock.

### Ischemia, and the calendar after myocyte death

Ischemia is demand that supply cannot meet. A fixed atherosclerotic narrowing causes exertional pain that stops with rest. Plaque rupture with thrombus causes rest pain. ST elevation means a coronary artery is usually fully closed and the full wall thickness is in trouble. ST depression or T-wave change with a rising troponin is injury that has not closed the artery completely. Troponin means membrane damage. It does not say why. Demand ischemia from a fast rhythm or from anemia can raise it too.

Complications follow a calendar because dead muscle and inflammation have a clock. This is physiology, not a protocol.

| When, in the usual teaching | What fails | The finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minutes to the first day | Electrical irritability | Ventricular arrhythmia. This is why the first hours are watched |
| First day to a few days | Neutrophils and early necrosis | A rub can be the infarct itself, not a late autoimmune event |
| Several days | Soft necrotic wall | Papillary muscle rupture (a new murmur and flash edema), septal rupture (a new harsh murmur and a step-up in oxygen in the right ventricle), free-wall rupture (sudden arrest and tamponade) |
| Weeks | A scar, or an autoimmune reaction | Aneurysm of a thin scar. Dressler-type pericarditis is late, with fever and a rub, not in the first hour |

A physician should confirm the day-ranges against a current pathology source before using them as exact cutoffs. The order is the testable part: arrhythmia early, mechanical rupture when the wall is soft, autoimmune pericarditis late.

**Vignette.** Four days after a severe anterior infarct, a 66-year-old suddenly cannot breathe. A new holosystolic murmur is at the apex. The ECG is unchanged from the morning.
**Reasoning.** The infarct is not extending in a way the tracing shows. The new murmur and the sudden edema several days later are a mechanical break. At the apex, that is papillary muscle rupture into the left atrium. A septal rupture is also holosystolic but is often harsher at the sternal border and causes an oxygen step-up in the right ventricle. The trap is “reinfarction” because the pain of pulmonary edema feels cardiac. The tracing did not change.

**NBME trap.** They put the complication at the wrong time. A rub on day 2 is the infarct. A rub at six weeks with fever is the late immune picture. Students use one word, pericarditis, for both.

**Hook.** Soft wall, then rupture. Scar, then aneurysm. Immune, then late rub.

### Shunts, cyanosis, and the lesions that need a patent ductus

A left-to-right shunt is acyanotic. Blood that already visited the lungs visits them again. The chambers that see the extra flow enlarge. A right-to-left shunt is cyanotic. Venous blood skips the lung. Eisenmenger is a left-to-right shunt that has raised pulmonary pressure until the shunt reverses. The murmur of a ventricular septal defect can get quieter as that happens. Quieter is not better.

| Lesion | Early shunt | What enlarges | The one finding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ventricular septal defect | Left to right | Left heart, then lungs | Holosystolic. Small and loud can be harmless. Large and quiet can be severe |
| Atrial septal defect | Left to right | Right heart | Fixed splitting. Often found in an adult |
| Patent ductus arteriosus | Left to right (aorta to pulmonary artery) | Left heart | Continuous murmur. Prematurity is the setting |
| Tetralogy of Fallot | Right to left through the ventricular septal defect | Right ventricle hypertrophies against stenosis | Cyanotic spells. Squatting raises systemic resistance and sends more blood to the lungs |
| Transposition of the great arteries | Two parallel circuits | — | A neonate who is cyanotic and needs a mixing lesion (a septal opening or a ductus) to live |
| Coarctation | Not a shunt | Left ventricle, and collaterals | Higher blood pressure in the arms than the legs. Radio-femoral delay. Turner syndrome is the association, not the mechanism |
| Aortic stenosis in a newborn, or a hypoplastic left heart | Forward flow depends on the ductus | — | Shock when the ductus closes. Cyanosis is not the only ductal emergency |

**Vignette.** A 3-month-old becomes deeply blue and limp during a crying spell. A harsh systolic murmur is present. Bringing the knees to the chest eases the color.
**Reasoning.** Crying drops systemic resistance and increases the right-to-left fraction across a ventricular septal defect when the pulmonary outflow is tight. Knees-to-chest raises systemic resistance and improves pulmonary flow. That is a tetralogy spell, not a seizure and not apnea of prematurity. The trap is morphine-versus-oxygen trivia. The mechanism is the resistance ratio.

**Pair.** Truncus arteriosus is one artery leaving the heart over a septal defect. Transposition is two arteries swapped. Both can present with cyanosis. Truncus has a single semilunar valve and often more pulmonary flow. Transposition has two separate circuits and depends on mixing.

### Muscle disease and the pericardial sac

| Disease | Cavity | Wall | The function that fails first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dilated cardiomyopathy | Big | Thin | Systole. S3, low ejection, a regurgitant murmur from a stretched ring |
| Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy | Small | Thick, often the septum | Diastole, and sometimes dynamic obstruction. S4 |
| Restrictive cardiomyopathy | Normal | Stiff | Diastole. Looks like constriction until the myocardium itself is infiltrated (amyloid is the classic) |
| Acute pericarditis | — | Inflamed sac | Pain better leaning forward, a rub, diffuse ST elevation without reciprocal depression the way a regional infarct has |
| Tamponade | — | Fluid under pressure | Equalized diastolic pressures, hypotension, distended neck veins, muffled sounds, pulsus paradoxus. The y descent is lost |
| Constrictive pericarditis | — | A rigid shell | A sharp y descent, a pericardial knock, edema. The heart cannot fill in late diastole |

**Vignette.** After a viral illness, a 28-year-old has chest pain that is worse lying flat and better sitting forward. The ECG shows ST elevation in many leads. Troponin is normal.
**Reasoning.** Positional pain and a diffuse tracing without a territorial pattern are the sac, not one coronary artery. The diagnosis is acute pericarditis. The trap is thrombolysis or a catheterization lab, because “ST elevation” was treated as a word and not as a distribution.

**Pair.** Tamponade equalizes pressures and blunts the y descent. Constriction has a sharp y descent and a knock. Both limit filling. The venous wave tells them apart.

### Vessels, lipids, and the plaque

Atherosclerosis is endothelial injury, lipid entry, inflammation, a fibrous cap, and then either a stable narrowing or a ruptured cap with thrombus. A stable cap causes demand angina. A ruptured cap causes an acute coronary syndrome. Aneurysm is dilation of all three layers. Dissection is a tear in the intima with blood in the media. They are not the same word.

| Particle | Where it comes from | What a question wants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chylomicron | Gut, after a meal | Delivers dietary fat. A creamy supernatant after a fatty meal |
| VLDL | Liver | Carries endogenous triglyceride |
| LDL | From VLDL | Delivers cholesterol to tissues and to plaques. The usual target of a statin |
| HDL | Peripheral and liver exchange | Returns cholesterol toward the liver. Low HDL is a risk marker, not a reason to invent a drug in this chapter |

Statins block HMG-CoA reductase. The liver senses less cholesterol and puts more LDL receptors on the surface, so blood LDL falls. That receptor story is the mechanism. Muscle injury is the adverse effect to recognize, not a dose. Fibrates act on triglyceride-rich particles. Bile-acid binders keep bile acids in the gut so the liver spends cholesterol to make more. Ezetimibe blocks cholesterol absorption at the brush border. PCSK9 inhibitors keep LDL receptors from being destroyed. You do not need a regimen. You need which step each class touches.

**Vignette.** A 62-year-old has a sudden tearing pain between the scapulae. The right arm pulse is weaker than the left. The ECG has no ST elevation. The chest film is wide.
**Reasoning.** Unequal pulses and a tearing pain to the back are a flap in the aortic wall, not a coronary occlusion. The diagnosis is aortic dissection. The trap is treating it as infarction because the pain is severe, or as an aneurysm because the mediastinum is wide. An aneurysm can be silent. A dissection is a propagating tear.

### The ECG as territory

| Leads with the injury | Wall | Usual artery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V1 through V4 | Anterior septum and anterior wall | Left anterior descending |
| II, III, aVF | Inferior wall | Right coronary in most people. A dominant circumflex in some |
| I, aVL, V5, V6 | Lateral wall | Circumflex |
| V4R and right-sided leads | Right ventricle, with an inferior infarct | Proximal right coronary |
| Posterior leads, or tall R waves with depression in V1–V3 | Posterior wall | Usually the same circulation as the inferior or lateral event |
| Diffuse elevation, PR depression | The sac, not one wall | Not a single coronary artery |

An inferior infarct can involve the right ventricle. Those patients are preload-dependent. Nitrates, which pool blood in veins, can drop the pressure hard. That is a mechanism, and it is why the right-sided leads exist.

**Vignette.** ST elevation is in II, III, and aVF. Blood pressure falls after a nitrate. The lungs are clear.
**Reasoning.** The inferior wall and a nitrate collapse with clear lungs point to a right ventricle that needed its filling pressure. The diagnosis is right-ventricular infarction with an inferior event. The trap is “papillary rupture” because the pressure fell. Rupture would bring a murmur and pulmonary edema, and it would not be caused by one nitrate in the first hour.

### Drugs as arrows on the same four terms

| Class or drug | The arrow | The Step 1 consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beta blocker | Slower rate, less contractility, less renin | Less oxygen demand. Can hide tachycardia in hypoglycemia. Do not start the story in a decompensated shock and call it “heart-failure therapy” |
| Dihydropyridine calcium blocker | Arterial dilation | Reflex tachycardia. Ankle edema is precapillary dilation, not heart failure, until proven otherwise |
| Non-dihydropyridine calcium blocker | Slower rate and less contractility | A second AV-nodal blocker on top of a beta blocker can stop the node |
| Nitrate | Venodilation, less preload | Headache, hypotension, and a dangerous fall in pressure if a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor is on board |
| Digoxin | More contractility, more vagal tone at the node | Toxicity is arrhythmias and visual change. Hypokalemia makes toxicity more likely because the binding site is freer |
| Adenosine | Briefly blocks the AV node | A diagnostic and therapeutic pause for a re-entrant supraventricular tachycardia. It will not fix atrial fibrillation’s ventricular rate for long, and it will not fix ventricular tachycardia |
| Atropine | Blocks vagal slowing | Raises the sinus rate. It does not fix a block that is below the node |
| Class I antiarrhythmics | Sodium-channel block, slower phase 0 | Use-dependence: a faster rate means more block. Quinidine, procainamide, lidocaine, and flecainide are not interchangeable. The ECG effect (a wider QRS) is the teaching point |
| Class III | Potassium-channel block, longer repolarization | A longer QT. Torsades is the risk, especially with a low potassium or magnesium |
| Amiodorone | Many channels, and a very long half-life | Lung, thyroid, liver, and skin are the organs it stores in. The mechanism of toxicity is accumulation, not an allergy story |

**NBME trap.** They ask which drug increases the PR interval, which widens the QRS, and which lengthens the QT. Those are three different channels. A student who memorized “antiarrhythmic” as one word cannot answer.

**Hook.** Sodium channel, QRS. Potassium channel, QT. Calcium and vagal tone at the node, PR.

### More worked vignettes

**Vignette.** A 78-year-old with long-standing hypertension has a displaced apex beat and an S4. The carotid upstroke is normal. There is no diastolic murmur.
**Reasoning.** A stiff, pressure-overloaded ventricle makes an S4 and a sustained apex. A normal carotid upstroke argues against severe aortic stenosis. There is no leak to explain volume. The diagnosis is hypertensive heart disease with concentric hypertrophy. The trap is aortic stenosis because of the age and the S4.

**Vignette.** A newborn whose mother had an unmonitored pregnancy becomes gray and shocked on day 3. The legs are colder than the arms. A murmur is not impressive.
**Reasoning.** Day-of-life timing is the ductus closing. Shock at ductal closure means systemic flow depended on the ductus: a left-sided obstructive lesion such as coarctation or a hypoplastic left heart, not a primary lung disease. The trap is sepsis, which is also real in a neonate and must be considered, but the arm-leg difference is the vascular clue. The diagnosis to name first is ductal-dependent systemic flow.

**Vignette.** A 40-year-old has a fever, a new regurgitant murmur, and a small red lesion on the palm. Blood cultures are not back.
**Reasoning.** Fever plus a new valve leak is infection of the valve until cultures say otherwise. The palm lesion is a vascular phenomenon, not the diagnosis by itself. Acute disease on a normal valve is often a destructive organism. Subacute disease on an abnormal valve is often a less aggressive oral organism. The trap is waiting for a dental history before believing the valve. The diagnosis is infective endocarditis. Drug choice is a later Step and an IDSA/AHA document, not this page.

**Vignette.** A young adult has a fixed split second heart sound and no cyanosis. The right ventricle is enlarged. Exercise tolerance has slowly fallen.
**Reasoning.** A split that does not move is an atrial septal defect. Extra pulmonary flow enlarges the right heart. The absence of cyanosis means the shunt is still left to right. The trap is pulmonic stenosis, which also enlarges the right ventricle but widens the split in a respiratory way and has a louder ejection murmur. The diagnosis is an atrial septal defect.

**Vignette.** After a weekend of heavy alcohol, a middle-aged adult develops pulmonary edema and a dilated left ventricle. The coronary arteries, if imaged, are not the story the stem emphasizes.
**Reasoning.** A dilated, weak ventricle after a binge is a toxic cardiomyopathy until ischemia is excluded. The mechanism is direct myocardial depression, not a plaque. The trap is “infarction” because of the edema. The diagnosis is dilated cardiomyopathy, often alcohol-associated in this stem.

### Distinguishing pairs worth freezing

| A | B | The split |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aortic stenosis | Hypertrophic obstruction | Delayed carotid versus brisk carotid. Squat louder versus squat softer |
| Mitral stenosis | Left atrial myxoma | A snap and a rumble that may change with position in a myxoma. A tumor plop is positional. Stenosis is not a positional tumor |
| Pericarditis | ST-elevation infarct | Many leads and positional pain versus one territory and a crushing pain |
| Tamponade | Constriction | Blunted y versus sharp y |
| Left-to-right ventricular septal defect | Tetralogy | Pink and often loud versus blue spells and a right-to-left fraction |
| Stable angina | Acute coronary syndrome | Predictable exertion versus rest pain or a changing pattern |
| Aneurysm | Dissection | A dilated sac versus a tearing flap and unequal pulses |
| S3 | S4 | Early filling of a slack ventricle versus atrial kick into a stiff one |

## Stem craft

Maneuvers and complications the stem describes without naming the lesion.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Handgrip makes a holosystolic murmur at the apex louder. The carotid upstroke is normal.
**Reasoning.** More afterload sends more blood backward through a mitral leak. Aortic stenosis would radiate to the carotids and have a different upstroke. Hypertrophic obstruction would get softer with handgrip, not louder.

**Vignette 2.** A neonate is cyanotic and only stays alive because a septal opening is letting the circuits mix. The great arteries leave the wrong ventricles.
**Reasoning.** Two parallel circuits. That is transposition. Truncus is one artery over a septal defect. The trap is using cyanosis as a synonym for tetralogy, which has a right-to-left shunt through a ventricular septal defect and a pulmonary stenosis, not swapped arteries.

**Vignette 3.** A continuous murmur under the left clavicle in a premature infant. It does not stop at the second heart sound.
**Reasoning.** The ductus is still open from aorta to pulmonary artery. A venous hum also continues but changes with the neck. The trap is calling every continuous murmur an atrial septal defect. An atrial septal defect fixes the split and is not continuous.

**Vignette 4.** Giant v waves and a holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border that gets louder on inspiration.
**Reasoning.** Inspiration loads the right heart, so this leak is tricuspid, not mitral. Mitral regurgitation does not get louder on inspiration in that way. The giant v wave is the atrium receiving the regurgitant volume.

**Vignette 5.** A pericardial knock, edema, and a sharp y descent. The heart is not large.
**Reasoning.** A rigid shell stops late filling. Constriction. Tamponade blunts the y descent and equalizes pressures with a quieter, fuller pericardium. The venous wave is the split.

## Filing the stem

**Vignette.** A holosystolic murmur at the apex gets louder with handgrip. The carotid pulse is brisk and normal in timing.
**Reasoning.** Handgrip raises afterload, so more blood leaks backward through the mitral valve. Aortic stenosis radiates to the carotids and delays the upstroke. Hypertrophic obstruction gets softer, not louder, when afterload rises. The diagnosis is mitral regurgitation.

**Vignette.** A child with tetralogy squats during a spell and the color improves.
**Reasoning.** Squatting raises systemic vascular resistance, so less blood crosses the ventricular septal defect from right to left and more goes to the lungs. The trap is oxygen alone as the whole story. The resistance ratio is the mechanism.

**Vignette.** A fixed split second heart sound has been present for years. The patient is not cyanotic. The right ventricle is enlarged.
**Reasoning.** The split does not move because the atrial septal defect equalizes respiratory variation. Flow is still left to right. Eisenmenger would reverse the shunt and the patient would be cyanotic. The trap is pulmonic stenosis, which widens the split with inspiration instead of fixing it.

**Vignette.** ST elevation is confined to V1 through V4. II, III, and aVF are not elevated.
**Reasoning.** Those precordial leads look at the anterior wall. The usual vessel is the left anterior descending artery. Inferior elevation would be II, III, and aVF, usually the right coronary artery. The trap is naming the artery from the patient’s age instead of from the leads.

**Vignette.** A wide pulse pressure, a head that bobs, and a diastolic murmur. He has been this way for years and can still walk.
**Reasoning.** Chronic aortic regurgitation. The ventricle enlarged and compensated. Acute regurgitation is a small ventricle, a short history, and pulmonary edema. The low diastolic number is the leak, not sepsis, unless he is ill.

**Vignette.** A continuous murmur under the left clavicle in a premature infant does not stop at the second heart sound. Pressing the neck does not silence it.
**Reasoning.** Blood is moving from the aorta into the pulmonary artery all through the cycle. That is a patent ductus arteriosus. A venous hum is also continuous, but it changes when the neck is pressed or the child lies down. An atrial septal defect fixes the split of the second heart sound and is not a continuous murmur. The trap is calling every murmur in a premature infant a ventricular septal defect, which is holosystolic and stops at the second heart sound.

**Vignette.** The jugular venous waveform shows a giant v wave. A holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border gets louder when the patient breathes in.
**Reasoning.** Inspiration increases venous return to the right heart, so a right-sided leak gets louder. The giant v wave is the right atrium accepting that regurgitant volume. The diagnosis is tricuspid regurgitation. Mitral regurgitation is louder with handgrip, not with inspiration, and it radiates to the axilla. The trap is calling every holosystolic murmur mitral.

**Vignette.** A newborn whose ductus has just closed becomes shocked, with weaker pulses in the legs than in the arms, and almost no murmur.
**Reasoning.** Systemic flow was depending on the ductus. When it closed, a coarctation or another left-sided obstruction declared itself. Sepsis is still on the neonatal list, but the arm-leg pulse gap is a vascular lesion. The trap is spending the hour on a full septic workup before anyone feels both the brachial and the femoral pulses. Feel the femorals in every shocked neonate before you call it sepsis alone. A missing femoral pulse with a present brachial pulse is coarctation until an imaging study says otherwise. Radio-femoral delay is the same lesion felt in time rather than in strength. Check both sides. A one-sided delay can be a local arterial injury instead of a coarctation.

## Checklist

- [ ] Name pump, tank, or pipes before the diagnosis.
- [ ] Opposite maneuvers: hypertrophic obstruction and prolapse.
- [ ] Inspiration splits S2 wider unless the aortic valve is the slow one.
- [ ] Tamponade: equalization, lost y descent. Constriction: sharp y descent.
- [ ] A loud VSD can be a small hole.

## Common traps

- Calling every systolic murmur aortic stenosis. Timing, radiation, and the carotid pulse separate stenosis, regurgitation, and hypertrophic obstruction.
- Treating warm shock like cold shock. The pipes are open. Vasoconstriction is not the missing piece at the start.
- Forgetting that aortic regurgitation widens pulse pressure because the diastolic leak lowers diastolic pressure and the big stroke volume raises systolic pressure.
- Memorizing drug doses here. Beta blockade, afterload reduction, and diuresis show up as arrows on a tracing. The regimen is a later Step.
