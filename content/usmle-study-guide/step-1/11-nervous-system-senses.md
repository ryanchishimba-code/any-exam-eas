# Nervous system and special senses

> **Study aid only.** Localization. Imaging choice and thrombolysis windows are Step 2 and Step 3 topics and follow AHA/ASA guidance.

## What this chapter covers

How to place a lesion from the finding, the tracts worth drawing, and the special-sense organs as receptors. Behavioral health and the nervous system together are about 10–14% of Step 1. Behavior and biostatistics are the next chapter. This one is the wire.

## The framework

Localization beats diagnosis lists. Ask three questions. Is it brain, cord, or peripheral nerve? If brain, is it cortex, brainstem, or basal ganglia? If cord, which tract and which level?

| Finding | Where to look |
| --- | --- |
| Face and body weak on the same side, aphasia or neglect | Contralateral cortex, above the brainstem |
| Face weak on one side and body on the other, crossed signs, cranial nerves | Brainstem |
| Resting tremor, rigidity, slow movement | Basal ganglia, often the substantia nigra’s dopamine |
| Intention tremor, ataxia, nystagmus | Cerebellum or its wires |
| A sensory level, bowel or bladder, bilateral motor | Spinal cord until proven otherwise |
| One root: pain in a dermatome, reflex lost | Root, not a single peripheral nerve |
| Stocking-glove sensory loss | Peripheral nerve, length-dependent |

Upper motor neuron is cortex or cord: spastic, Babinski present, no fasciculations. Lower motor neuron is anterior horn or nerve: flaccid, fasciculations, reduced reflexes, atrophy. A mixed picture (both) is the classic amyotrophic lateral sclerosis pattern. The mechanism is two populations of motor neurons dying, not one disease name to recite.

The corticospinal tract crosses in the medulla. A lesion above the decussation hits the opposite body. A lesion in the cord hits the same side of the body. Dorsal columns (vibration, proprioception) cross in the medulla. Spinothalamic (pain, temperature) crosses within a level or two of entry. A hemisection of the cord therefore loses same-side motor and vibration, and opposite-side pain, a level or two below. Draw it once. The syndrome is just that drawing.

Circle of Willis items are “which artery, which deficit.” Anterior cerebral is leg-predominant weakness. Middle cerebral is face and arm, plus language if it is the dominant hemisphere. Posterior cerebral is vision (occipital). Lenticulo-striate perforators are the small deep strokes: pure motor or pure sensory. Aneurysms follow the junctions. A posterior communicating artery aneurysm is the classic ipsilateral third-nerve palsy with a dilated pupil, because the parasympathetic fibers ride the outside of the nerve.

## Special senses, only the mechanism

- Retina: rods for dim light, cones for color and acuity, concentrated in the fovea. A detached retina loses the field that corresponds to the region that lost contact.
- Visual pathway: nasal fibers cross at the chiasm. A chiasm lesion (often a pituitary mass) hits both temporal fields. A tract or occipital lesion hits one field of both eyes.
- Ear: conductive loss is external or middle ear (bone conduction better than air, or Weber to the bad ear). Sensorineural is cochlea or nerve (Weber to the good ear, air still better than bone).
- Vestibular: a peripheral lesion is violent vertigo with nystagmus that suppresses with fixation. A central lesion is less kind and does not follow the peripheral rules. Step 1 wants the distinction. Step 2 wants the stroke rule-out.

## High-yield atlas

Localization is the whole chapter. Ask whether the lesion is cortex, brainstem, cord, or nerve before you ask the disease name. A crossed cranial-nerve sign and a body sign means the brainstem. A sensory level means the cord until proven otherwise. A stocking-glove pattern means the longest nerves.

### Tracts you can rebuild

The corticospinal tract crosses in the lower medulla. A lesion above the decussation hits the opposite body. A lesion in the cord hits the same side of the body. Dorsal columns (vibration, proprioception) cross in the medulla. Spinothalamic pain and temperature cross within a level or two of entry. A hemisection therefore loses same-side motor power and vibration, and opposite-side pain a level or two below. Draw it. The eponym is just that drawing.

| Finding | The place |
| --- | --- |
| Face and body weak on the same side, plus aphasia or neglect | Contralateral cortex, above the brainstem |
| Face weak on one side, body on the other | Brainstem |
| Resting tremor, rigidity, slow movement | Substantia nigra and the direct/indirect pathways. Dopamine is low |
| Intention tremor, ataxia | Cerebellum or its wires |
| A sensory level, bowel or bladder, bilateral motor | Spinal cord |
| One dermatome and a lost reflex | A root, not a single peripheral nerve |
| Stocking-glove sensory loss | Length-dependent neuropathy |
| Upper motor neuron | Spastic, Babinski present, no fasciculations |
| Lower motor neuron | Flaccid, fasciculations, atrophy, reduced reflexes |
| Both, in the same person | The classic motor-neuron disease pattern. Two populations of motor neurons |

**Vignette.** After a stab wound to the back, one leg is weak and has lost vibration. The other leg has lost pain sensation beginning a little below the wound. The arms are spared.
**Reasoning.** Same-side dorsal column and corticospinal tract, opposite spinothalamic tract, at one level. The diagnosis is a cord hemisection. The trap is a peripheral nerve, which would not split the sensory modalities across the two legs this cleanly.

**Hook.** Pain crosses low. Vibration crosses high. Motor crosses in the medulla.

### Arteries and the deficit they own

| Leads or the deficit | Territory | Usual vessel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leg-predominant weakness, and sometimes abulia | Medial cortex | Anterior cerebral |
| Face and arm weakness, aphasia if dominant, neglect if nondominant | Lateral cortex | Middle cerebral |
| Vision of one field | Occipital lobe | Posterior cerebral |
| Pure motor or pure sensory, no cortical signs | Internal capsule or thalamus | A small penetrating artery, often a lenticulostriate branch |
| Cranial nerve and a contralateral body | Brainstem | The vertebral-basilar branches. Name the nerve and you name the level |
| Bitemporal field cut | The chiasm | A pituitary mass underneath, or a craniopharyngioma from above in a child |
| A dilated pupil and a down-and-out eye | Compression of the third nerve | A posterior communicating artery aneurysm until a microvascular palsy is more likely. Pupil fibers ride the outside, so compression hits them first |

**Vignette.** A sudden inability to speak in full sentences, with a weak right face and arm and a spared leg. The person understands poorly.
**Reasoning.** Language plus face and arm is the dominant lateral cortex, middle-cerebral territory. The leg cortex is medial, anterior cerebral, and was spared. The diagnosis is a left middle-cerebral infarct in a typical left-dominant person. The trap is an anterior cerebral infarct because “stroke” and “weakness” were enough for the student. The leg being strong is the split.

**NBME trap.** A third-nerve palsy with a spared pupil and vascular risk factors is often a microvascular ischemic palsy. A blown pupil is compression until proven otherwise. The stem includes or omits the pupil on purpose.

### Ventricles, pressure, and the herniations

CSF is made in the choroid plexus, flows through the ventricles, out the foramina, and is absorbed at the arachnoid granulations. A block inside the ventricular system is noncommunicating hydrocephalus. A block at the granulations is communicating. Normal-pressure hydrocephalus is a communicating picture in an older adult: wet, wobbly, and wacky (incontinence, gait, cognition), with a large ventricle and a disproportionately small sulcal pattern.

Raised pressure causes a headache that is worse lying down, vomiting, and papilledema. Cushing’s reflex is hypertension and bradycardia when the brainstem is compressed. Uncal herniation compresses the ipsilateral third nerve and then the cerebral peduncle. A tonsillar herniation through the foramen magnum stops breathing.

**Vignette.** An older adult shuffles, wets himself, and has become slow in conversation. The ventricles are large and the sulci are not correspondingly wide. A large-volume tap improves the gait for a day.
**Reasoning.** The ventricle is big because CSF is not absorbed well, not because the brain shrank. The diagnosis is normal-pressure hydrocephalus. The trap is Alzheimer disease, which atrophies the brain and widens the sulci with the ventricles. The tap is the clinical split in the classic stem.

### Spinal cord syndromes besides the hemisection

| Syndrome | What was hit | The finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anterior spinal artery | Both spinothalamic tracts and both corticospinal tracts. Dorsal columns often spared | Loss of pain and motor power. Vibration remains. Often a watershed of hypotension or an aortic surgery |
| Central cord, a syrinx | Crossing spinothalamic fibers first | A cape-like loss of pain over the shoulders. Later the motor neurons in the arms |
| Subacute combined degeneration | Dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts | Vibration gone, a Babinski present. B12, not folate |
| Tabes dorsalis | Dorsal roots and columns | Vibration gone, lightning pains, a slapping gait. A syphilis story |
| Cauda equina | The roots below the cord | Saddle anesthesia, urinary retention, lower-motor-neuron legs. A surgical emergency on the later Steps |
| Conus | The tip of the cord | A similar saddle and bladder picture, often more sudden and more bilateral |

**Vignette.** Numb, clumsy hands and a cape of lost pain and temperature over the shoulders. Vibration in the feet is intact. There is a Chiari malformation on the film.
**Reasoning.** A cavity in the center of the cervical cord hits the crossing pain fibers. The diagnosis is a syrinx. The trap is a dorsal-column disease, which would take vibration first.

### Neurotransmitters and the diseases that are a missing transmitter

| Disease | The transmitter or the structure | The drug logic, mechanism only |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parkinson | Dopamine low in the nigrostriatal path. Acetylcholine is relatively high | Replace dopamine or block the relative cholinergic excess. Antipsychotics that block dopamine can cause the same picture |
| Huntington | Caudate atrophy. Too much movement | An autosomal dominant CAG repeat. Anticipation means an earlier, worse next generation |
| Alzheimer | Cortical acetylcholine is low. Plaques and tangles | A cholinesterase inhibitor is a modest symptomatic idea, not a cure. The later books say so out loud |
| Wilson | Copper in the basal ganglia and the liver | A low ceruloplasmin, a Kayser-Fleischer ring, a movement disorder in a young person |
| Myasthenia | Antibodies on the nicotinic receptor | Fatigable weakness. A cholinesterase inhibitor helps briefly. Too much of it becomes a cholinergic crisis |
| Lambert-Eaton | Antibodies on the presynaptic calcium channel | Strength improves for a moment with use. A small-cell lung cancer is the association |
| Botulism | No acetylcholine release | Descending paralysis. Pupils can be involved |

**Vignette.** A young person has a wing-beating tremor, a low mood, and elevated liver enzymes. A ring is seen at the edge of the cornea.
**Reasoning.** Copper is not being incorporated and excreted, so it sits in the liver and the basal ganglia. The diagnosis is Wilson disease. The trap is Parkinson disease because of the tremor. Age, the liver, and the ring are the split.

**Pair.** Myasthenia gets worse with use. Lambert-Eaton gets briefly better. Both are neuromuscular junction. The cancer and the antibody sit on opposite sides of the synapse.

### Seizures, the mechanism, not the regimen

A seizure is too much excitation or too little inhibition. GABA is the main brake (a chloride channel). Glutamate is the gas. A focal seizure starts in one place and can spread. A generalized seizure starts across both hemispheres. Absence is a brief stare in a child, a 3-hertz spike-and-wave, and it is not a daydream you can interrupt. A febrile seizure is a child, a rising fever, and a short event. Status is a seizure that does not stop. The benzodiazepine and the next drug are the Step 2 chapter. The mechanism of the benzodiazepine is a positive modulator of GABA.

**Vignette.** A child stops talking for ten seconds, stares, and then continues the sentence. Hyperventilation reproduces it. The EEG shows a three-per-second spike and wave.
**Reasoning.** A brief generalized seizure without a convulsion. The diagnosis is absence epilepsy. The trap is a complex partial seizure, which is longer, often has an aura, and does not have that EEG.

### Special senses, only the rules

| Finding | The place |
| --- | --- |
| A monocular blackout | The eye or the optic nerve, before the chiasm |
| Bitemporal hemianopsia | The chiasm. Nasal fibers cross |
| A homonymous hemianopsia | The tract or the occipital lobe. Same field of both eyes |
| Macular sparing | Often an occipital lesion whose macula has a dual supply. Do not treat it as a law |
| Conductive hearing loss | Bone conduction better than air, or Weber toward the bad ear. The canal or the middle ear |
| Sensorineural loss | Weber toward the good ear. The cochlea or the nerve |
| Vertigo that is violent, with nystagmus that lessens when the person fixes on a target | Peripheral vestibular |
| Vertigo with a central sign, diplopia, or a skew | Brainstem until proven otherwise. A CT is a poor friend here |

**Vignette.** Vision is missing in the temporal field of both eyes. A child has a headache and a calcified suprasellar mass.
**Reasoning.** Nasal fibers from both eyes were compressed at the crossing. In a child the mass above the sella is often a craniopharyngioma, a Rathke-pouch remnant. The diagnosis is a chiasmal lesion, classically that tumor in this stem. The trap is a pituitary adenoma, which is the adult version of the same field cut.

### Demyelination and the infections that like the nervous system

Multiple sclerosis is lesions separated in time and space, in a young adult, often with an optic neuritis or an internuclear ophthalmoplegia. Oligodendrocytes are the central myelin. Guillain-Barré is peripheral myelin or the axon, ascending, with lost reflexes, often after an infection. Steroids are not the disease treatment for Guillain-Barré. That fact is mechanism-adjacent and is repeated in the Step 2 book because it is also a management trap.

**Vignette.** A young woman has had an episode of painful vision loss in one eye that recovered, and now has double vision when she looks to the side. The adducting eye lags and there is nystagmus in the abducting eye.
**Reasoning.** The medial longitudinal fasciculus was hit. A previous optic nerve event means a second place and a second time. The diagnosis is multiple sclerosis. The trap is myasthenia, which fatigues and does not produce that abducting nystagmus.

### Eight stems, worked

**Vignette.** A sudden worst headache of a life, then a stiff neck. The person was straining.
**Reasoning.** A vessel popped into the subarachnoid space. The diagnosis is subarachnoid hemorrhage until imaging says otherwise. The trap is migraine, which is not sudden-maximal and not meningitic.

**Vignette.** Weakness that starts in the feet a week after a diarrheal illness and climbs. Reflexes are gone. There is no sensory level.
**Reasoning.** Peripheral nerves, not a cord. The diagnosis is Guillain-Barré. The trap is a cord compression, which would have a level and usually a Babinski.

**Vignette.** A resting pill-rolling tremor, a soft voice, and a face that does not blink. An antipsychotic was started last month.
**Reasoning.** Dopamine blockade in the nigrostriatal path. The diagnosis is drug-induced parkinsonism. The trap is idiopathic Parkinson disease, which is possible but the calendar points at the drug.

**Vignette.** Dance-like movements, irritability, and a parent who had the same illness in middle age.
**Reasoning.** An autosomal dominant CAG repeat with anticipation. The caudate is the structure. The diagnosis is Huntington disease. The trap is a habit twitch. The family history is the disease.

**Vignette.** Fatigable ptosis that is worse at the end of the day. A chest CT shows a thymic mass.
**Reasoning.** Antibodies on the muscle receptor, and a thymus that educated them badly. The diagnosis is myasthenia gravis. The trap is a third-nerve palsy, which would include the pupil and would not fluctuate through the day in this way.

**Vignette.** A baby has a tuft of hair over the lower spine and weak legs.
**Reasoning.** The neural tube did not close. The diagnosis is a neural-tube defect, spina bifida in this cutaneous form. Folate is the prevention story, not the treatment of the formed defect.

**Vignette.** Loss of pain and temperature on one side of the body and the opposite side of the face, with a Horner syndrome and ataxia. The onset was sudden.
**Reasoning.** A lateral brainstem infarct. The face’s pain nucleus and the crossed body pain, plus the sympathetic tract and the cerebellar peduncle, sit together laterally. The diagnosis is a lateral medullary syndrome, usually a vertebral or a PICA branch. The trap is a cortex stroke, which would not cross the face and the body.

**Vignette.** Bilateral loss of vibration and a positive Babinski. The blood count shows large red cells.
**Reasoning.** Dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts, plus a megaloblastic marrow. The diagnosis is B12 deficiency. The trap is folate, which fixes the anemia and not the cord.

## Stem craft

Place the lesion before you name the disease.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Same-side paralysis and loss of vibration. Opposite-side loss of pain starting a level below a stab wound.
**Reasoning.** Hemisection. Pain crossed low. Vibration and motor did not. A peripheral nerve cannot split the two legs this cleanly.

**Vignette 2.** Sudden inability to speak, weak right face and arm, strong leg.
**Reasoning.** Dominant middle-cerebral territory. The leg is anterior cerebral and was spared. The trap is an anterior cerebral stroke because the word weakness was enough.

**Vignette 3.** A blown pupil and an eye that is down and out, after the worst headache of a life.
**Reasoning.** Compression of the third nerve, classically a posterior communicating aneurysm, and a subarachnoid bleed. A microvascular palsy spares the pupil. The pupil fibers are on the outside.

**Vignette 4.** Shuffle, incontinence, and slow thinking. Ventricles are large. Sulci are not wide. A tap improves the gait.
**Reasoning.** Normal-pressure hydrocephalus. Alzheimer atrophy widens the sulci too. The tap is the classic split.

**Vignette 5.** Fatigable ptosis and a thymic mass. Strength returns for a minute after a cholinesterase inhibitor.
**Reasoning.** Antibodies on the postsynaptic receptor. Lambert-Eaton is presynaptic, improves with a brief use, and follows a small-cell cancer. Botulism descends and the pupils can die.

## Another pass on the stems

More localization stems.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Vibration is gone in the feet. Pain is intact. A Babinski sign is present. The red cells are large.
**Reasoning.** Dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts. B12. A syrinx would take pain over the shoulders and spare vibration early. Folate would not do this to the cord.

**Vignette 2.** A cape of lost pain and temperature over the shoulders. The hands are weak and atrophic. Vibration in the toes is present.
**Reasoning.** A central cord cavity hit the crossing spinothalamic fibers and the anterior horn. A Chiari malformation often sits nearby. The trap is a peripheral neuropathy, which is stocking-glove and takes vibration too.

**Vignette 3.** Weber test lateralizes to the bad ear. Bone conduction is better than air on that side.
**Reasoning.** Conductive loss. Sensorineural loss lateralizes to the good ear. The trap is remembering the direction backward.

**Vignette 4.** One eye goes blind for minutes, then recovers, in a person with a carotid bruit.
**Reasoning.** The lesion is in front of the chiasm, on that side, often an embolus to the retinal artery from the carotid. A chiasmal lesion hits both temporal fields and does not black out one eye.

## Checklist

- [ ] Crossed cranial-nerve and body findings mean brainstem.
- [ ] Draw cord hemisection before naming the eponym.
- [ ] ACA leg, MCA face and arm, PCA vision.
- [ ] Chiasm is bitemporal. Tract is homonymous.
- [ ] Weber lateralizes toward a conductive loss and away from a sensorineural loss.

## Common traps

- Calling every third-nerve palsy a posterior communicating aneurysm. If the pupil is spared and there are vascular risk factors, a microvascular ischemic palsy is the other classic. Pupil fibers are peripheral, so compression hits them first.
- Swapping dorsal columns and spinothalamic. Pain crosses low. Vibration crosses high.
- Treating Parkinson disease as a cerebellar tremor. Rest versus intention.
- Memorizing dozens of brainstem eponyms. The rule (which cranial nerve, which side of the body) rebuilds them.
