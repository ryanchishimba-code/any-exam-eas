# Nervous system and special senses

> **Study aid only.** Localization. Imaging choice and thrombolysis windows are Step 2 and Step 3 topics and follow AHA/ASA guidance.

## What this chapter covers

How to place a lesion from the finding, the tracts worth drawing, and the special-sense organs as receptors. Behavioral health and the nervous system together are about 10–14% of Step 1. Behavior and biostatistics are the next chapter. This one is the wire.

## The framework

Localization beats diagnosis lists. Ask three questions. Is it brain, cord, or peripheral nerve? If brain, is it cortex, brainstem, or basal ganglia? If cord, which tract and which level?

| Finding | Where to look |
| --- | --- |
| Face and body weak on the same side, aphasia or neglect | Contralateral cortex, above the brainstem |
| Face weak on one side and body on the other, crossed signs, cranial nerves | Brainstem |
| Resting tremor, rigidity, slow movement | Basal ganglia, often the substantia nigra’s dopamine |
| Intention tremor, ataxia, nystagmus | Cerebellum or its wires |
| A sensory level, bowel or bladder, bilateral motor | Spinal cord until proven otherwise |
| One root: pain in a dermatome, reflex lost | Root, not a single peripheral nerve |
| Stocking-glove sensory loss | Peripheral nerve, length-dependent |

Upper motor neuron is cortex or cord: spastic, Babinski present, no fasciculations. Lower motor neuron is anterior horn or nerve: flaccid, fasciculations, reduced reflexes, atrophy. A mixed picture (both) is the classic amyotrophic lateral sclerosis pattern. The mechanism is two populations of motor neurons dying, not one disease name to recite.

The corticospinal tract crosses in the medulla. A lesion above the decussation hits the opposite body. A lesion in the cord hits the same side of the body. Dorsal columns (vibration, proprioception) cross in the medulla. Spinothalamic (pain, temperature) crosses within a level or two of entry. A hemisection of the cord therefore loses same-side motor and vibration, and opposite-side pain, a level or two below. Draw it once. The syndrome is just that drawing.

Circle of Willis items are “which artery, which deficit.” Anterior cerebral is leg-predominant weakness. Middle cerebral is face and arm, plus language if it is the dominant hemisphere. Posterior cerebral is vision (occipital). Lenticulo-striate perforators are the small deep strokes: pure motor or pure sensory. Aneurysms follow the junctions. A posterior communicating artery aneurysm is the classic ipsilateral third-nerve palsy with a dilated pupil, because the parasympathetic fibers ride the outside of the nerve.

## Special senses, only the mechanism

- Retina: rods for dim light, cones for color and acuity, concentrated in the fovea. A detached retina loses the field that corresponds to the region that lost contact.
- Visual pathway: nasal fibers cross at the chiasm. A chiasm lesion (often a pituitary mass) hits both temporal fields. A tract or occipital lesion hits one field of both eyes.
- Ear: conductive loss is external or middle ear (bone conduction better than air, or Weber to the bad ear). Sensorineural is cochlea or nerve (Weber to the good ear, air still better than bone).
- Vestibular: a peripheral lesion is violent vertigo with nystagmus that suppresses with fixation. A central lesion is less kind and does not follow the peripheral rules. Step 1 wants the distinction. Step 2 wants the stroke rule-out.

## Checklist

- [ ] Crossed cranial-nerve and body findings mean brainstem.
- [ ] Draw cord hemisection before naming the eponym.
- [ ] ACA leg, MCA face and arm, PCA vision.
- [ ] Chiasm is bitemporal. Tract is homonymous.
- [ ] Weber lateralizes toward a conductive loss and away from a sensorineural loss.

## Common traps

- Calling every third-nerve palsy a posterior communicating aneurysm. If the pupil is spared and there are vascular risk factors, a microvascular ischemic palsy is the other classic. Pupil fibers are peripheral, so compression hits them first.
- Swapping dorsal columns and spinothalamic. Pain crosses low. Vibration crosses high.
- Treating Parkinson disease as a cerebellar tremor. Rest versus intention.
- Memorizing dozens of brainstem eponyms. The rule (which cranial nerve, which side of the body) rebuilds them.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Do localization items with the options covered. Say the place out loud, then look. Feed cards that are “artery to deficit” are useful only if you can also say why (which cortex, which tract). Ophthalmology and ENT management live in the clinical books.
