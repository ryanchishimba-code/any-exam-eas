# Pathology and inflammation

> **Study aid only.** Patterns, not a slide collection. Facility diagnoses belong to a pathologist.

## What this chapter covers

The injury patterns that show up in every organ chapter. Pathology is the largest Step 1 discipline in the published ranges. You do not learn it as a separate textbook. You learn a small set of patterns and then recognize them in heart, lung, kidney, and liver.

## The framework

Cell injury is a spectrum. Reversible injury is swelling and fatty change. Irreversible injury is membrane damage, mitochondrial failure, and nuclear change (pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis). Necrosis is the mess. Apoptosis is the orderly ATP-dependent removal.

| Pattern | What broke | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coagulative | Ischemia in solid organs except brain | Ghost architecture, then a scar |
| Liquefactive | Enzymes, or brain infarct | Pus or a cystic cavity |
| Caseous | Granulomatous (often mycobacteria) | Cheese-like, no architecture |
| Fat | Lipase on fat (pancreas, breast trauma) | Saponification, calcium soap |
| Fibrinoid | Vessels in malignant hypertension, vasculitis | Fibrin in the wall |
| Gangrenous | Limb or bowel ischemia, often plus infection | Dry (coagulative) or wet (infected) |

Inflammation is vascular plus cellular. Acute is neutrophils and edema. Chronic is lymphocytes, macrophages, and repair. Granulomas are macrophages that could not clear the antigen: mycobacteria, fungi, foreign body, sarcoidosis.

## Repair, not trivia

Labile tissues (skin, gut, marrow) regenerate. Stable tissues (liver, kidney tubule) regenerate if the framework survives. Permanent tissues (cardiac myocyte, neuron, skeletal muscle) scar. A transmural infarct scars. It does not regrow muscle. That single fact explains aneurysm, rupture, and Dressler timing later.

Hypersensitivity is four mechanisms. Learn the mechanism, then the classic disease falls out.

| Type | Mechanism | Examples worth keeping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | IgE, mast cell, immediate | Anaphylaxis, asthma, some drug rash |
| II | Antibody against a cell or receptor | Autoimmune hemolysis, Goodpasture, Graves, myasthenia |
| III | Immune complexes | Serum sickness, many vasculitides, lupus nephritis patterns |
| IV | T cells, delayed | Contact dermatitis, TB skin test, transplant cellular rejection |

## Neoplasia in one page

Benign is local and well differentiated. Malignant invades and can metastasize. Carcinoma is epithelial. Sarcoma is mesenchymal. Teratoma is more than one germ layer. The grade is how wild the cells look. The stage is how far they have gone. Stage drives outcome more often than grade. Do not swap them.

Tumor suppressors need both copies lost (“two hits”). Oncogenes need one bad copy. p53 stops the cycle for repair or apoptosis. Rb holds E2F. A virus that inactivates both (some HPV proteins) removes two brakes.

## Checklist

- [ ] Name the necrosis pattern before the organ.
- [ ] Separate acute, chronic, and granulomatous by the cell, not the adjective.
- [ ] Permanent tissue scars. Say that out loud for heart and CNS.
- [ ] Type II is antibody on a target. Type III is complexes. Do not merge them.
- [ ] Stage is spread. Grade is appearance.

## Common traps

- Calling every brain infarct coagulative. Brain liquefies.
- Calling every granuloma tuberculosis. Sarcoid, fungi, and foreign body do it too. Caseation pushes you toward mycobacteria.
- Treating metaplasia as cancer. It is an adaptation. It can be the soil for dysplasia later.
- Memorizing stain colors without the question the stain answers. Congo red is for amyloid because the question is “which deposit.”

## Practice this in the Qbank

In the Step 1 bank, filter to pathology-heavy items and force yourself to name the pattern (necrosis, hypersensitivity, repair) before the diagnosis. The feed cards that pair a stain or a cell with a disease are the hook. If you cannot say why that cell is there, come back to this table. Do not collect stains as trivia.
