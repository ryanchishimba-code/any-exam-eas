# Pathology and inflammation

> **Study aid only.** Patterns, not a slide collection. Facility diagnoses belong to a pathologist.

## What this chapter covers

The injury patterns that show up in every organ chapter. Pathology is the largest Step 1 discipline in the published ranges. You do not learn it as a separate textbook. You learn a small set of patterns and then recognize them in heart, lung, kidney, and liver.

## The framework

Cell injury is a spectrum. Reversible injury is swelling and fatty change. Irreversible injury is membrane damage, mitochondrial failure, and nuclear change (pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis). Necrosis is the mess. Apoptosis is the orderly ATP-dependent removal.

| Pattern | What broke | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coagulative | Ischemia in solid organs except brain | Ghost architecture, then a scar |
| Liquefactive | Enzymes, or brain infarct | Pus or a cystic cavity |
| Caseous | Granulomatous (often mycobacteria) | Cheese-like, no architecture |
| Fat | Lipase on fat (pancreas, breast trauma) | Saponification, calcium soap |
| Fibrinoid | Vessels in malignant hypertension, vasculitis | Fibrin in the wall |
| Gangrenous | Limb or bowel ischemia, often plus infection | Dry (coagulative) or wet (infected) |

Inflammation is vascular plus cellular. Acute is neutrophils and edema. Chronic is lymphocytes, macrophages, and repair. Granulomas are macrophages that could not clear the antigen: mycobacteria, fungi, foreign body, sarcoidosis.

## Repair, not trivia

Labile tissues (skin, gut, marrow) regenerate. Stable tissues (liver, kidney tubule) regenerate if the framework survives. Permanent tissues (cardiac myocyte, neuron, skeletal muscle) scar. A transmural infarct scars. It does not regrow muscle. That single fact explains aneurysm, rupture, and Dressler timing later.

Hypersensitivity is four mechanisms. Learn the mechanism, then the classic disease falls out.

| Type | Mechanism | Examples worth keeping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | IgE, mast cell, immediate | Anaphylaxis, asthma, some drug rash |
| II | Antibody against a cell or receptor | Autoimmune hemolysis, Goodpasture, Graves, myasthenia |
| III | Immune complexes | Serum sickness, many vasculitides, lupus nephritis patterns |
| IV | T cells, delayed | Contact dermatitis, TB skin test, transplant cellular rejection |

## Neoplasia in one page

Benign is local and well differentiated. Malignant invades and can metastasize. Carcinoma is epithelial. Sarcoma is mesenchymal. Teratoma is more than one germ layer. The grade is how wild the cells look. The stage is how far they have gone. Stage drives outcome more often than grade. Do not swap them.

Tumor suppressors need both copies lost (“two hits”). Oncogenes need one bad copy. p53 stops the cycle for repair or apoptosis. Rb holds E2F. A virus that inactivates both (some HPV proteins) removes two brakes.

## High-yield atlas

Pathology is the largest Step 1 discipline in the published ranges. The exam does not want a stain collection. It wants the pattern, the cell that shows up, and the consequence for the organ that is wearing the pattern.

### Injury, from reversible to nuclear death

A cell that is hurt and still has ATP swells. The sodium pump fails, water enters, and the endoplasmic reticulum balloons. That is hydropic change. Fat accumulates in cells that handle lipid when the pathway is blocked: the liver in alcohol or in a toxin is the usual picture. Both are reversible if the nucleus is intact.

Irreversible injury is membrane failure and mitochondrial failure. Calcium rushes in. Enzymes leak out. That leak is why a troponin, a transaminase, or a lipase in the blood means the cell’s membrane is open. The nucleus then shrinks (pyknosis), fragments (karyorrhexis), or fades (karyolysis).

| Pattern | The tissue | Why it looks that way | The trap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coagulative | Heart, kidney, spleen after ischemia | Architecture remains as a ghost because the enzymes are denatured | Calling a brain infarct coagulative |
| Liquefactive | Brain, or any abscess | Enzymes or neutrophils digest the tissue into a cavity | Calling every cavity a tumor |
| Caseous | Granuloma, often mycobacterial | Cheese-like, no architecture | Calling every granuloma caseous. Sarcoid granulomas are usually noncaseating |
| Fat necrosis | Pancreas, or traumatized breast | Lipase frees fatty acids that bind calcium | Calling it metastatic calcification. This is enzymatic, local |
| Fibrinoid | Vessel walls in malignant hypertension or vasculitis | Fibrin and immune debris in the wall | Calling it an atherosclerotic plaque |
| Gangrene | Limb or bowel | Coagulative plus, if wet, infection | Dry and wet are not two diseases. Wet means bacteria joined |
| Fibrinous | Pericardium, pleura, meninges | A bread-and-butter exudate | Calling it pus. Fibrin is not neutrophils |

**Vignette.** A man with sudden left-chest pain dies two days later. The left ventricular wall is pale and holds its shape. The brain is not involved.
**Reasoning.** Ischemic death in a solid organ outside the brain keeps the outline of the tissue. The diagnosis is coagulative necrosis of myocardium. The trap is liquefactive, because “necrosis” was taught as one picture.

**Hook.** Brain and pus liquefy. Solid organs under ischemia coagulate. Caseation is the granuloma that lost its outline.

### Apoptosis versus necrosis, and the free-radical path

Necrosis is messy, inflammatory, and energy-poor. Apoptosis is ATP-dependent, orderly, and usually does not inflame the neighborhood. The intrinsic path is mitochondrial: cytochrome c, the apoptosome, caspases. BCL2 holds that door shut. The extrinsic path is a death receptor such as FAS. Cytotoxic T cells also use perforin and granzyme. DNA laddering and cell shrinkage are apoptosis. A swollen cell with a ruptured membrane is necrosis.

Free radicals damage membranes (lipid peroxidation), proteins, and DNA. The body spends superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase to quench them. Acetaminophen toxicity consumes glutathione in the liver. Carbon tetrachloride and reperfusion both make radicals. Reperfusion injury is real: restoring flow brings oxygen and neutrophils into already-damaged tissue.

| Word | Meaning | A stem that uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hyperplasia | More cells | Prostate, endometrium under estrogen, a regenerating liver |
| Hypertrophy | Bigger cells | The pressure-loaded ventricle, the pregnant uterus (also has hyperplasia) |
| Atrophy | Smaller cells or fewer | Denervation, aging, loss of hormone, pressure from a neighboring mass |
| Metaplasia | One adult cell type replaces another | Squamous change in a smoker’s bronchus, Barrett esophagus under acid |
| Dysplasia | Disordered growth, still inside the basement membrane | The step that can still be removed before invasion |
| Carcinoma in situ | Full-thickness dysplasia, no invasion | Not yet a metastasis problem |
| Anaplasia | No resemblance to the parent tissue | A grading word, not a stage |

Metaplasia is an adaptation. It is not cancer. It is soil in which dysplasia can start. Barrett esophagus is the example everyone must not call “already adenocarcinoma.”

**Vignette.** Bronchial biopsies from a long-time smoker show squamous epithelium where ciliated columnar cells belong. The basement membrane is intact. There is no mass.
**Reasoning.** The epithelium changed jobs because the irritant made the tougher cell more useful. The diagnosis is squamous metaplasia. The trap is squamous-cell carcinoma, which requires invasion or at least a mass and disordered full-thickness growth. Intact membrane and no mass is not that.

**NBME trap.** They ask which process is reversible. Hypertrophy, hyperplasia, metaplasia, and atrophy can reverse if the stimulus stops. Dysplasia is the one you do not promise will reverse. Anaplasia will not.

### Inflammation, the cell, and the clock

Acute inflammation is vascular leak plus neutrophils. Chronic inflammation is lymphocytes, macrophages, and repair. The chemical mediators are how the stem hides the cell.

| Mediator | What it does | The question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Histamine | Immediate vascular leak | The first minutes of a type I reaction |
| Bradykinin | Pain and leak | Why inflamed tissue hurts |
| Prostaglandins | Fever, pain, vasodilation | Why a cyclooxygenase inhibitor lowers fever |
| Leukotrienes | Bronchoconstriction, more leak | An asthma path that a cyclooxygenase inhibitor does not block, and can worsen |
| C3a and C5a | Anaphylatoxin. C5a also calls neutrophils | A complement question that is not the membrane-attack complex |
| IL-1, IL-6, TNF | Fever, acute-phase proteins, cachexia if sustained | The systemic feeling of inflammation |
| Nitric oxide | Vasodilation, and microbial killing | Septic vasodilation has a mediator, not a mood |

Wound healing by first intention is a clean closed wound. Second intention is an open wound that fills with granulation tissue. Granulation tissue is new vessels and fibroblasts. It is not a granuloma. A granuloma is a macrophage that could not digest its target, often fused into a giant cell, walled by lymphocytes. Caseation points toward mycobacteria. Noncaseating points toward sarcoid, foreign body, or some fungi, and Crohn disease in the gut.

**Vignette.** A lymph node shows clusters of epithelioid macrophages and multinucleated giant cells with no central necrosis. The lungs have bilateral hilar enlargement. A stain for acid-fast bacilli is negative.
**Reasoning.** Macrophages that gave up and fused, without cheese-like necrosis, plus bilateral hilar nodes, is the noncaseating pattern. The diagnosis is sarcoidosis until a culture or a special stain says otherwise. The trap is tuberculosis because “granuloma” was treated as a synonym.

**Pair.** Granulation tissue builds a scar. A granuloma walls off an indigestible antigen. The words share a root and nothing else.

### Hypersensitivity, applied

| Type | The engine | Diseases that are this engine | The trap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | IgE, mast cell, immediate | Anaphylaxis, allergic asthma, some drug rashes | A late-phase cough hours later is still type I, not type IV |
| II | Antibody on a cell or a receptor | Autoimmune hemolysis, Goodpasture, Graves (stimulating), myasthenia (blocking), rheumatic fever (cross-reaction) | Graves is type II even though the gland is overactive. The antibody is on the receptor |
| III | Immune complexes | Serum sickness, many vasculitides, the nephritis of systemic lupus, the Arthus reaction | Low complement means the complexes are consuming it |
| IV | T cells, delayed | Contact dermatitis, the tuberculin skin test, acute cellular transplant rejection | A positive PPD at 48 hours is not an IgE allergy |

**Vignette.** Ten days after a drug, a patient has fever, joint pain, and a falling complement. The urine has protein and red cells. There is no wheeze and no immediate rash at the first dose.
**Reasoning.** A delay of days, consumed complement, and a kidney that is inflamed are complexes, not mast cells. The diagnosis is a type III serum-sickness picture. The trap is anaphylaxis because a drug is involved. Anaphylaxis is minutes, not day ten.

**Hook.** Minutes and IgE. Antibody on a target. Complexes and low complement. Two days and T cells.

### Hemodynamic disease and shock as pathology

Edema is either too much pressure pushing fluid out, too little protein holding it in, leaky vessels, or a lymphatic drain that is blocked. Heart failure is pressure. Nephrotic syndrome and liver failure are protein. Inflammation is leak. A filarial or surgical lymphatic block is the drain.

Thrombi form by Virchow: stasis, endothelial injury, and a hypercoagulable blood. A postmortem clot is chicken-fat and not attached. A true thrombus is attached and has lines of Zahn. Emboli travel. Lung emboli usually come from leg veins. Systemic emboli usually come from the left heart. A paradoxical embolus crosses a right-to-left opening.

Infarcts in loose tissue or with dual supply can be hemorrhagic. Infarcts in solid organs with one supply are pale. The lung is often red. The kidney and the spleen are pale. Reperfusion can turn a pale infarct red at the edges.

**Vignette.** A postoperative patient suddenly cannot breathe. The legs are swollen on one side. The pulmonary artery pressure is high. The bronchial arteries are intact.
**Reasoning.** A clot from a stagnant leg vein lodged in the pulmonary arteries. The lung tissue may not die if the bronchial circulation still feeds it, but the ventilated alveoli have no blood: dead space. The diagnosis is pulmonary thromboembolism. The trap is calling it a primary lung infarct every time. Many emboli cause dead space and strain on the right heart without a wedge-shaped scar.

### Neoplasia, only the rules that answer items

Benign is local, usually well differentiated, and does not metastasize. Malignant invades and can metastasize. Carcinoma is epithelial. Sarcoma is mesenchymal. A teratoma has more than one germ layer. Hamartoma is disorganized tissue that belongs at that site. Choristoma is normal tissue in the wrong place.

Grade is how wild the cells look. Stage is how far they have gone. Stage predicts outcome more often. Do not swap them.

Tumor suppressors need both copies lost. Oncogenes need one gain-of-function copy. p53 stops the cycle for repair or apoptosis. Rb holds E2F. BCL2 blocks apoptosis, so a cell that should die accumulates. A translocation can create a fusion protein or can put a growth gene next to a loud promoter.

Carcinogenesis is initiation (a mutation), promotion (a stimulus that grows the clone), and progression (more mutations, invasion). Initiators are mutagens. Promoters are not always mutagens. Alcohol promoting a smoker’s larynx is the idea.

Paraneoplastic syndromes are a substance the tumor should not be making in that amount: PTH-related peptide and high calcium, ACTH and Cushing, ADH and low sodium, erythropoietin and a high hematocrit. The calcium of bone metastases is local osteolysis and is not the same as a peptide.

| Marker or stain, used as a clue | What it suggests | What it does not prove alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cytokeratin | Carcinoma | The organ |
| Vimentin | Sarcoma, and some carcinomas | A benign versus malignant call |
| S-100 | Melanoma, nerve sheath, and others | A single diagnosis |
| Chromogranin or synaptophysin | Neuroendocrine | Which organ |
| PSA | Prostate origin | That every rise is cancer |
| AFP | Yolk sac, and hepatocellular carcinoma | That every liver bump is cancer |
| beta-hCG | Germ-cell and gestational trophoblast | A normal pregnancy has it too |
| CEA | A tracker for some adenocarcinomas | A screening test. It is not |

**Vignette.** A smoker has a hilar mass, low sodium, and urine that is inappropriately concentrated. The chest film is the only mass.
**Reasoning.** A small-cell neuroendocrine tumor in the lung can make ADH. The low sodium with concentrated urine is the peptide, not psychogenic water drinking, which would suppress ADH and dilute the urine once the person is water-loaded in the usual teaching contrast. The diagnosis is a paraneoplastic ADH syndrome from a pulmonary neuroendocrine carcinoma. The trap is “SIADH from pneumonia” with no mass. The stem gave a mass.

**NBME trap.** They ask the grade and the answer choices are TNM letters, or they ask the stage and the choices are “well differentiated.” Read the noun.

### Amyloid, pigments, and calcification

Amyloid is misfolded protein in a beta-pleated sheet. Congo red and apple-green birefringence are the pair. AL is immunoglobulin light chain, plasma-cell disease. AA is serum amyloid A, chronic inflammation. ATTR is transthyretin, the heart and the nerves in older adults, and a mutant form in families. Beta-amyloid is the Alzheimer plaque and is not the systemic disease.

Dystrophic calcification is calcium in damaged tissue with a normal blood calcium. Metastatic calcification is calcium deposited because the blood calcium or phosphate is high, including in normal tissues. The names are easy to swap. Damaged tissue, normal labs: dystrophic. Normal tissue, high product: metastatic.

| Pigment | Where it comes from | The disease it marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hemosiderin | Iron from broken blood | Heart-failure cells in the lung, a bruise, hemochromatosis if widespread |
| Bilirubin | Heme breakdown | Jaundice. It is not iron |
| Lipofuscin | Wear-and-tear lipid peroxidation | Aging. It does not injure the cell by itself |
| Melanin | Melanocytes | A nevus or a melanoma. A tattoo is carbon, not melanin |
| Carbon | Inhaled soot | Anthracosis. Not melanoma |

**Vignette.** An older adult has a calcified aortic valve and a normal calcium on the chemistry panel.
**Reasoning.** Calcium sat down in a damaged valve. The blood calcium did not have to be high. The diagnosis is dystrophic calcification. The trap is metastatic calcification, which needs a high calcium-phosphate product and shows up in places like the stomach, the kidney, and the lung interstitium.

### Worked pairs the items love

| They look alike | Split them |
| --- | --- |
| Coagulative versus liquefactive | Organ. Brain liquefies |
| Granulation versus granuloma | New vessels versus walled-off macrophages |
| Metaplasia versus dysplasia | An organized new cell type versus disordered growth |
| Grade versus stage | Looks versus spread |
| Dystrophic versus metastatic calcium | Damaged tissue versus a high serum product |
| Type II versus type III | Antibody stuck to a target versus complexes floating and landing |
| Hyperplasia versus neoplasia | Stops when the stimulus stops versus grows past it |
| Caseating versus noncaseating | Mycobacteria more likely versus sarcoid more likely |
| Transudate versus exudate | Pressure or protein versus inflammation. Exudate is the leaky, cell-rich one |
| Initiation versus promotion | Mutation versus a growth push that need not mutate |

**Vignette.** A woman has a painless breast lump. A biopsy shows fat cells with chalky calcium and no atypia. She remembers a seatbelt injury.
**Reasoning.** Trauma, fat, and local calcium without malignant cells is enzymatic and traumatic fat necrosis. The diagnosis is fat necrosis. The trap is ductal carcinoma because the lump is painless. Carcinoma is still possible in life and must be excluded by the tissue, but the stem’s chalky fat and the trauma are the teaching point.

**Vignette.** Complement is low. A skin biopsy of a vessel shows fibrinoid change. The illness began a week after a drug, with fever and joint pain.
**Reasoning.** Complexes landed in vessels, fixed complement, and left fibrinoid damage. The diagnosis is a type III vasculitis picture. The trap is type II, because “antibody” is involved in both. Type II is aimed at a cell you can name. Type III is a complex that lands where flow and filtration allow it.

### What this atlas refuses to be

A list of every tumor marker, every stain, and every eponym is how a chapter becomes trivia. If a marker is in the table above, it is there because a stem uses it to pick a lineage. If a disease is missing, ask whether the question is really the pattern (necrosis, hypersensitivity, adaptation) wearing that disease as a costume. The feed cards hold single stain-to-disease hooks. Use them after you can say which pattern the stain is proving.

## NBME worked set

Patterns the stem will dress up as a disease. Name the pattern first.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** A liver biopsy after chronic hepatitis shows nodules of regenerating hepatocytes surrounded by fibrosis. The spleen is large.
**Reasoning.** Architecture was destroyed and rebuilt in nodules. That is cirrhosis, not a metastasis. Portal pressure explains the spleen. The trap is calling every nodular liver cancer.

**Vignette 2.** A lymph node has caseous necrosis and Langhans giant cells. The patient has a chronic cough and a upper-lobe cavity.
**Reasoning.** Caseation plus a cavity is mycobacterial disease until a stain and a culture say otherwise. Noncaseating granulomas would have pushed you toward sarcoid. The trap is treating every giant cell as a tumor.

**Vignette 3.** A smoker’s bronchial biopsy shows orderly squamous epithelium replacing ciliated columnar cells. The basement membrane is intact.
**Reasoning.** This is metaplasia, an adaptation. Carcinoma needs disordered growth and invasion. The trap is the word squamous.

**Vignette 4.** Ten days after a drug, complement is low, joints hurt, and the urine has red cells. There was no wheeze at the first dose.
**Reasoning.** Complexes took days and consumed complement. Type III. Anaphylaxis is minutes and IgE. The trap is calling every drug reaction type I.

**Vignette 5.** A valve is calcified. The serum calcium is normal.
**Reasoning.** Calcium sat in damaged tissue. Dystrophic, not metastatic. Metastatic calcification needs a high calcium-phosphate product and lands in tissues that were not the primary injury.

**Vignette 6.** A brain infarct becomes a fluid-filled cavity. A kidney infarct stays pale and keeps its outline.
**Reasoning.** The brain liquefies. Solid organs under ischemia coagulate. The trap is one word, necrosis, for both pictures.

**Vignette 7.** A breast biopsy after a seatbelt injury shows chalky fat and no atypia.
**Reasoning.** Enzymatic fat necrosis. The trap is calling a painless lump carcinoma before the tissue is back. The tissue here is fat and calcium, not malignant glands.

**Vignette 8.** A tumor’s cells ignore their neighbors and have invaded through the basement membrane. The nuclei are wild. The nodes are still negative.
**Reasoning.** Invasion makes it malignant. Wild nuclei are grade. Negative nodes are part of stage, not a reason to call it benign. The trap is using grade words to answer a stage question.

**Vignette 9.** Congo red stains a deposit that glows apple green under polarized light. The patient has a plasma-cell disorder.
**Reasoning.** AL amyloid from light chains. AA would have been chronic inflammation. The trap is calling every eosinophilic deposit amyloid without the birefringence.

**Vignette 10.** A wound is full of new capillaries and fibroblasts on day five. There are no giant cells.
**Reasoning.** Granulation tissue. A granuloma is macrophages that could not digest their target. The shared root is the trap.

## Stem craft

A second pass on the patterns that get renamed in the stem.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** A man dies two days after chest pain. The left ventricular wall is pale and still shaped like muscle. The brain is normal.
**Reasoning.** Ischemia in a solid organ outside the brain denatures enzymes and keeps the outline. That is coagulative necrosis. Liquefactive would be brain or pus. The time, two days, is neutrophils arriving, not a scar.

**Vignette 2.** An abscess cavity in the liver is full of neutrophils. The surrounding liver cells are intact.
**Reasoning.** This cavity was digested by neutrophils, so it is liquefactive. It is not a tumor cyst and it is not the coagulative ghost of an infarct. The trap is one word, necrosis.

**Vignette 3.** A transplant kidney stops working on day ten. The biopsy is lymphocytes in the tubules. The preformed antibodies were negative before surgery.
**Reasoning.** T cells took days. That is type IV, acute cellular rejection. Hyperacute rejection is minutes and preformed antibody, type II. The calendar is the split.

**Vignette 4.** A scar in the heart is dense collagen. The myocytes did not return.
**Reasoning.** Cardiac muscle is permanent tissue. It scars. Liver can regenerate if the framework survives. The trap is expecting the infarct to refill with muscle.

**Vignette 5.** A promoter is applied to skin that was already mutated. Tumors appear only where both happened.
**Reasoning.** Initiation is the mutation. Promotion is the growth push and need not be a mutagen. The trap is calling every cancer-causing exposure a mutagen.

## Filing the stem

A last set of stems that are easy to misfile.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Apple-green birefringence after Congo red. The patient has chronic rheumatoid arthritis, not a plasma-cell disease.
**Reasoning.** AA amyloid from serum amyloid A. AL would be light chains. The underlying disease picks the protein. The stain only says amyloid.

**Vignette 2.** Calcium in the lung and the stomach. The serum calcium is high. The tissues were not previously injured.
**Reasoning.** Metastatic calcification. Dystrophic calcification sits in damaged tissue and the serum calcium is normal. The labs are the split.

**Vignette 3.** A positive Nikolsky is not in this stem. A child has a sunburn-like rash after a staphylococcal infection. The split is in the epidermis.
**Reasoning.** Exfoliatin. Staphylococcal scalded skin. Pemphigus is an adult autoimmune split at a similar level. The age and the infection are the split.

**Vignette 4.** A granuloma with no necrosis in a person with bilateral hilar nodes and a negative acid-fast stain.
**Reasoning.** Noncaseating granulomas. Sarcoid until a culture says otherwise. Caseation would have pushed harder toward mycobacteria.

## Checklist

- [ ] Name the necrosis pattern before the organ.
- [ ] Separate acute, chronic, and granulomatous by the cell, not the adjective.
- [ ] Permanent tissue scars. Say that out loud for heart and CNS.
- [ ] Type II is antibody on a target. Type III is complexes. Do not merge them.
- [ ] Stage is spread. Grade is appearance.

## Common traps

- Calling every brain infarct coagulative. Brain liquefies.
- Calling every granuloma tuberculosis. Sarcoid, fungi, and foreign body do it too. Caseation pushes you toward mycobacteria.
- Treating metaplasia as cancer. It is an adaptation. It can be the soil for dysplasia later.
- Memorizing stain colors without the question the stain answers. Congo red is for amyloid because the question is “which deposit.”

## Practice this in the Qbank

In the Step 1 bank, filter to pathology-heavy items and force yourself to name the pattern (necrosis, hypersensitivity, repair) before the diagnosis. The feed cards that pair a stain or a cell with a disease are the hook. If you cannot say why that cell is there, come back to this table. Do not collect stains as trivia.
