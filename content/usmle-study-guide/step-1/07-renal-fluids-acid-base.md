# Renal, fluids, and acid-base

> **Study aid only.** The nephron as a machine. When to dialyze or which fluid bag to hang is a later Step, and it follows a current KDIGO or critical-care source.

## What this chapter covers

Where salt and water move, how acid is kept, and the patterns of acute and chronic kidney injury as physiology. Renal content shares the 11–15% respiratory-and-renal range on Step 1.

## The framework

![Where the nephron works](visuals/nephron-sites.svg)

Proximal tubule reabsorbs the bulk: sodium, bicarbonate, glucose, amino acids. Carbonic anhydrase is here. Thick ascending limb is the diluting segment and is water-impermeable. NKCC2 is the loop-diuretic target. Distal convoluted tubule is NCC, the thiazide target, and it reabsorbs calcium when sodium delivery is handled this way. Collecting duct principal cells do aldosterone (ENaC, sodium in, potassium out) and ADH (aquaporins, water in). Intercalated cells move acid.

ADH is the water hormone. Aldosterone is the sodium-saving, potassium-wasting hormone. They are not synonyms. A patient can be volume-overloaded and still hold water if ADH is on (low effective volume, or an inappropriate ADH).

| Disorder | Primary change | Compensation you should expect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metabolic acidosis | Bicarbonate down | Hyperventilation. Winter’s formula estimates the expected PCO2. If the PCO2 is higher, a respiratory acidosis is also present. |
| Metabolic alkalosis | Bicarbonate up | Hypoventilation, limited by hypoxia. |
| Respiratory acidosis | PCO2 up | Kidneys raise bicarbonate, slowly. |
| Respiratory alkalosis | PCO2 down | Kidneys lower bicarbonate, slowly. |

Anion gap is sodium minus (chloride plus bicarbonate). Albumin is a normal unmeasured anion, so a low albumin hides a gap. A gap acidosis means an added acid: lactate, ketones, toxins, or retained anions of kidney failure. A normal-gap acidosis means bicarbonate was lost or replaced by chloride: diarrhea, saline, or a renal tubular acidosis.

| RTA | Defect | A clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type 2, proximal | Cannot reclaim bicarbonate | Fanconi features. Hypokalemia. |
| Type 1, distal | Cannot acidify the urine | Urine pH stays high. Stones. Hypokalemia. |
| Type 4 | Aldosterone low or resisted | Hyperkalemia. The acidosis is mild. |

## Injury patterns

Prerenal physiology is a kidney that is underfilled and still working: sodium is conserved, urine is concentrated. Acute tubular injury is a kidney that cannot conserve: muddy casts, sodium wasted. Postrenal is obstruction. Both kidneys, or one kidney if it is the only one, must be blocked before the creatinine must rise. Glomerular disease is the urine sediment: red-cell casts mean glomerular inflammation until proven otherwise. Nephrotic range protein is a barrier failure, with edema and lipiduria, and a thrombotic tendency because anticoagulant proteins are lost in the urine.

## High-yield atlas

The kidney question is a segment, a transporter, and then either a gap or a sediment. Say where the salt and the acid are moving before you name a syndrome.

### The nephron, transporter by transporter

![Where the nephron works](visuals/nephron-sites.svg)

| Segment | What crosses | What blocks it | What the body loses or keeps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proximal tubule | The bulk of sodium, bicarbonate, glucose, amino acids, phosphate | Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. SGLT2 inhibitors stop the glucose cotransport | Bicarbonate wasting, and a diuresis that is partly self-limited because downstream segments compensate. Glucose in the urine when the threshold is passed or the transporter is blocked |
| Thin descending limb | Water out, into the salty medulla | — | Concentrates the tubular fluid. No active salt pump here |
| Thick ascending limb | NKCC2. Impermeable to water. The diluting segment | Loop diuretics | Salt wasting, a high urine output, calcium wasting, and a lower medullary gradient so water cannot be reabsorbed later even if ADH is present |
| Distal convoluted tubule | NCC | Thiazides | Salt wasting with calcium retention. The urine is diluted further |
| Principal cell | ENaC in, ROMK potassium out, aquaporins if ADH is on | Amiloride and triamterene block ENaC. Spironolactone and eplerenone block the aldosterone receptor. Lithium and demeclocycline interfere with the ADH effect | Aldosterone saves sodium and wastes potassium. ADH saves water. They are not the same hormone |
| Intercalated cell | Acid out, or bicarbonate out, depending on the subtype | — | The distal acidification that type 1 renal tubular acidosis cannot do |

**Vignette.** A woman on a new diuretic has a low potassium, a mildly high calcium, and gouty pain. Her urine calcium is low.
**Reasoning.** A transporter in the distal convoluted tubule was blocked. Calcium reabsorption rose, which is the opposite of a loop diuretic. The diagnosis is a thiazide effect. The trap is “loop diuretic” because both waste potassium. The calcium is the split.

**Hook.** Loops waste calcium. Thiazides save it. Acetazolamide wastes bicarbonate. Potassium-sparing drugs sit in the collecting duct.

### ADH, aldosterone, and the volume sensors

ADH is the water hormone. It is released when osmolality is high or when volume is low enough to override osmolality. Aldosterone is the sodium-saving, potassium-wasting hormone. Renin rises when the juxtaglomerular cells see low perfusion, when the macula densa sees less salt, or when sympathetic tone is high. Angiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole, which props up filtration when flow is low, and it tells the adrenal to release aldosterone. ANP is the atrial opposite: it dumps sodium when the atrium is stretched.

A person can hold water and still have a low effective volume. Heart failure, cirrhosis, and volume depletion all turn ADH and aldosterone on. The urine sodium is low in those states unless a diuretic is already in the story.

| State | Serum sodium | Urine | The hormone that is “wrong” or right |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dehydration | High | Concentrated, low sodium | ADH is doing its job |
| Diabetes insipidus, untreated | High | Dilute | ADH is missing or ignored |
| SIADH | Low | Inappropriately concentrated, and the urine sodium is not low | ADH is on while the person is not volume-depleted |
| Psychogenic polydipsia | Low | Maximally dilute | ADH is off. The person simply drank past the kidney |
| Hyperaldosteronism | Normal or slightly high | Potassium wasted, renin low if primary | Aldosterone is on while volume is expanded |
| Hypoaldosteronism | Low or normal | Potassium retained | Type 4 picture. The acidosis is mild |

**Vignette.** After a head injury, a patient puts out liters of dilute urine. The serum sodium climbs. A dose of desmopressin concentrates the urine.
**Reasoning.** The posterior pituitary signal is missing, and the kidney still answers when the hormone is replaced. The diagnosis is central diabetes insipidus. The trap is nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, which would not answer desmopressin, and SIADH, which is the opposite sodium problem.

**Pair.** SIADH is too much water held, concentrated urine, low serum osmolality, clinically euvolemic. Diabetes insipidus is water lost, dilute urine, high serum sodium. Do not treat them with each other’s logic.

### Acid-base, worked as a sequence

Do it in order. pH first. Then which process is primary. Then whether the compensation is in the right direction and roughly the right size. Then the anion gap if the process is a metabolic acidosis. Then whether a second process is hiding.

The anion gap is sodium minus chloride minus bicarbonate. Albumin is a normal unmeasured anion, so a low albumin hides a gap. A gap means an added acid: lactate, ketones, some toxins (methanol, ethylene glycol), or the retained anions of kidney failure. A normal-gap acidosis means bicarbonate was lost or replaced by chloride: diarrhea, saline, or a renal tubular acidosis.

Winter’s formula estimates the PCO2 you should see in a metabolic acidosis: about 1.5 times the bicarbonate plus 8, with a small range around it. If the measured PCO2 is much higher, the person is also failing to ventilate. If it is much lower, there is an extra respiratory alkalosis. A physician should confirm the formula before using it as a bedside law. The direction is what the exam wants.

| Disorder | Primary move | Compensation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metabolic acidosis | Bicarbonate down | Hyperventilation. The PCO2 should fall |
| Metabolic alkalosis | Bicarbonate up | Hypoventilation, limited by the need for oxygen |
| Respiratory acidosis | PCO2 up | Kidneys raise bicarbonate, slowly. An acute rise is mostly buffering. A chronic rise is a higher bicarbonate |
| Respiratory alkalosis | PCO2 down | Kidneys lower bicarbonate, slowly |

| RTA | The defect | Potassium | Urine pH clue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Type 2, proximal | Cannot reclaim bicarbonate | Low | Can acidify once the plasma bicarbonate is low enough that the distal tubule keeps up |
| Type 1, distal | Cannot pump acid | Low | Stays high. Stones, because citrate is low and calcium salts precipitate |
| Type 4 | Aldosterone low or resisted | High | The acidosis is mild. The potassium is the headline |

**Vignette.** A patient with diarrhea has a low pH, a low bicarbonate, a high chloride, and a normal anion gap. The breathing is deep.
**Reasoning.** Bicarbonate left the gut. Chloride rose to fill the charge. The lungs are compensating. The diagnosis is a normal-gap metabolic acidosis from bicarbonate loss. The trap is a renal tubular acidosis, which can look the same on the electrolytes. The history of diarrhea is the split. The urine anion gap, when you are shown it, is negative in diarrhea (the kidney is answering with ammonium) and not helpful in that way in a distal RTA.

**Vignette.** A patient on an ACE inhibitor and a potassium-sparing diuretic has a mild acidosis and a potassium of 6.2. The urine pH is not stuck at 8.
**Reasoning.** Aldosterone’s job is missing or blocked, so potassium stays in and acid is not fully excreted. The diagnosis is a type 4 picture. The trap is type 1, which wastes potassium.

**NBME trap.** They give a high PCO2 and a high bicarbonate and ask acute versus chronic. A very high bicarbonate means the kidneys have had days. An acute respiratory acidosis has only a small bicarbonate bump from buffering.

### Glomeruli and the sediment

Nephritic is blood, red-cell casts, and hypertension: an inflamed glomerulus. Nephrotic is heavy protein, edema, lipiduria, and a thrombotic tendency because anticoagulant proteins are lost in the urine. Some diseases do both.

| Pattern | The mechanism worth keeping | The stem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minimal change | Foot-process effacement. Cytokine, often after a child is ill. Light microscopy looks normal | A child, sudden edema, selective protein |
| Focal segmental sclerosis | Scarring of some glomeruli. Can follow a loss of renal mass, obesity, or some viruses and some genes | An adult, or a person of African ancestry in the classic risk teaching, heavier protein, does not always answer steroids |
| Membranous | Immune complexes on the epithelial side. Thick membranes | An adult. Cancer, hepatitis B, lupus, and some drugs are the associations. A thrombotic tendency is real |
| Membranoproliferative | Tram tracks. Complement is often low | Hepatitis C and other chronic antigens |
| IgA nephropathy | IgA complexes days after a mucosal infection, not weeks | Synpharyngitic hematuria. Not the post-strep delay |
| Post-streptococcal | Immune complexes weeks after a nephritogenic strain | Smoky urine, low complement, a child |
| Anti-GBM | Antibody on the basement membrane | Lung bleed plus a nephritic urine. Linear immunofluorescence |
| ANCA vasculitis | A pauci-immune crescent | Lung and kidney, or a systemic small-vessel disease, few immune deposits |
| Lupus nephritis | Several patterns. Complement is low | A young woman, other lupus clues, a full-house immunofluorescence in the classic teaching |

**Vignette.** Three days after a sore throat, a young adult has visible blood in the urine. Complement is normal. There is no edema.
**Reasoning.** The timing is days, not weeks, so this is not the post-streptococcal complex. IgA that was already in the mucosa landed in the mesangium. The diagnosis is IgA nephropathy. The trap is post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis because both follow a throat. The clock and the complement are the split.

**Pair.** Anti-GBM is linear antibody on the membrane and can bleed into the lung. Immune-complex diseases are granular and often drop complement. ANCA disease is crescents with little immunoglobulin.

### Acute injury, chronic scar, and the stones

Prerenal physiology is a kidney that is underfilled and still working: sodium is conserved, urine is concentrated. Acute tubular injury cannot conserve: muddy brown casts, sodium wasted. Postrenal is obstruction. Both kidneys, or a single working kidney, must be blocked before the creatinine must rise. A bladder scan is the fastest split.

| Cast | The place it came from |
| --- | --- |
| Hyaline | Tamm-Horsfall protein. Concentrated urine. Not a diagnosis |
| Red-cell | Glomerulus |
| White-cell | Inflammation in the tubule or the interstitium. Pyelonephritis, or an interstitial nephritis |
| Muddy brown | Tubular cells. Ischemic or toxic injury |
| Waxy or broad | A chronic, dilated tubule |
| Fatty | Nephrotic-range protein |

Stones follow a chemistry. Calcium oxalate is the common one. Calcium phosphate likes an alkaline urine. Struvite is infection with a urease organism and a staghorn. Uric acid is an acidic urine and is invisible on a plain film. Cystine is a genetic transporter defect and a hexagonal crystal.

**Vignette.** After a course of an antibiotic, a patient has a rising creatinine, a rash, and white cells in the urine, including white-cell casts. The bladder scan is empty.
**Reasoning.** The kidney is inflamed, not just infected in the bladder, and not blocked. A rash plus the drug history is an allergic interstitial nephritis. The diagnosis is acute interstitial nephritis. The trap is pyelonephritis, which also has white cells but usually has fever and a sicker urinary story without the rash as the headline. Eosinophils in the urine are neither sensitive nor required.

**Vignette.** A man with gout and an acidic urine has a stone that did not show on a plain radiograph. The pain is colicky.
**Reasoning.** A radiolucent stone in an acidic urine is uric acid until a CT says otherwise. The diagnosis is uric acid nephrolithiasis. The trap is calcium oxalate, which is radio-opaque and is the common stone, but the film and the urine pH were given for a reason.

### The hormones the kidney makes or activates

Erythropoietin comes from interstitial cells that sense oxygen. Chronic kidney disease is a low-erythropoietin anemia, usually normocytic. Vitamin D is activated by 1-alpha hydroxylase in the proximal tubule, and PTH turns that enzyme on. Kidney failure therefore causes low active vitamin D, low calcium, high phosphate, and a secondary rise in PTH. That is renal osteodystrophy’s mechanism. Do not call the high PTH primary. Primary hyperparathyroidism has a high calcium.

**Vignette.** A patient with a low GFR has a low calcium, a high phosphate, a high PTH, and bone pain.
**Reasoning.** The failing tubule did not activate vitamin D and did not excrete phosphate. PTH rose to defend the calcium and made the bone pay. The diagnosis is secondary hyperparathyroidism of chronic kidney disease. The trap is primary hyperparathyroidism, which raises calcium.

### More worked vignettes

**Vignette.** A child has rickets, glucose and amino acids in the urine, and a normal-gap acidosis. The urine can still become acidic when the plasma bicarbonate is very low.
**Reasoning.** The proximal tubule is failing at several jobs at once. The diagnosis is a Fanconi picture with type 2 renal tubular acidosis. The trap is type 1, which cannot acidify the urine at all and does not spill glucose.

**Vignette.** Urine output falls the day after a contrast study. The sediment has muddy brown casts. The bladder is empty. The urine sodium is high.
**Reasoning.** The tubule was injured and cannot conserve sodium. The diagnosis is acute tubular injury, here toxic. The trap is prerenal azotemia, which would conserve sodium and concentrate the urine.

**Vignette.** A pregnant woman late in pregnancy has hypertension, edema, and protein. The platelets are falling and the liver enzymes are rising.
**Reasoning.** This is no longer “a little protein of pregnancy.” The diagnosis is a preeclampsia-spectrum illness with a thrombotic microangiopathy flavor. The mechanism on Step 1 is endothelial injury. The management is the obstetrics chapter. The trap is primary nephrotic syndrome, which does not drop platelets and raise liver enzymes this way.

**Reasoning.** Thiazide. A loop would waste calcium. Both waste potassium. The calcium is the split.

**Vignette 2.** After a head injury, liters of dilute urine and a rising sodium. Desmopressin concentrates the urine.
**Reasoning.** Central diabetes insipidus. Nephrogenic disease would not answer the drug. SIADH is the opposite sodium problem.

**Reasoning.** Bicarbonate lost in the stool. The lungs are compensating. Distal RTA can look similar. The history is the split, and the urine anion gap, if shown, is negative when the kidney is answering with ammonium.

**Reasoning.** Type 4 physiology. Aldosterone’s job is blocked. Type 1 wastes potassium. The trap is the word RTA without the potassium.

**Vignette 5.** Visible blood in the urine three days after a sore throat. Complement is normal.
**Reasoning.** IgA nephropathy. Post-streptococcal disease waits weeks and drops complement. The clock is the trap.

**Vignette 6.** Smoky urine two weeks after a sore throat, low complement, a child.
**Reasoning.** Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. IgA is days, not weeks. Antibiotics do not reliably prevent this kidney.

**Vignette 7.** Lung bleeding and a nephritic urine. Immunofluorescence is linear.
**Reasoning.** Anti-GBM disease. Immune complexes are granular and often drop complement. ANCA disease is pauci-immune.

**Vignette 8.** Muddy brown casts the day after contrast. Urine sodium is high. The bladder is empty.
**Reasoning.** Acute tubular injury. Prerenal physiology would conserve sodium. Obstruction would fill the bladder or the pelvis.

**Reasoning.** Acute interstitial nephritis. Pyelonephritis is on the list, but the rash is the drug. Eosinophils are not required.

**Vignette 10.** Low calcium, high phosphate, high PTH, and a low GFR. Bone pain.
**Reasoning.** Secondary hyperparathyroidism. Primary disease would raise the calcium. The failing kidney did not activate vitamin D and did not clear phosphate.

## Stem craft

Electrolyte stems where the hormone is the hidden subject.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Low sodium, concentrated urine, euvolemic exam, and a urine sodium that is not low. The patient has a small-cell lung cancer.
**Reasoning.** ADH is on without a volume reason. SIADH. Heart failure would look wet and the urine sodium would be low. Psychogenic polydipsia would dilute the urine. The cancer is making the hormone or a mimic.

**Vignette 2.** High sodium, dilute urine, and no response to desmopressin. Lithium was started months ago.
**Reasoning.** The collecting duct will not answer ADH. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Central disease would respond to the drug. The trap is stopping the desmopressin trial before you see that it failed.

**Vignette 3.** A gap acidosis, an osmolar gap, and a urine that fluoresces or shows crystals, after a person drank a sweet antifreeze.
**Reasoning.** Ethylene glycol. Methanol is the visual one and does not make those crystals. Both are gap-plus-osmolar-gap. The kidney and the crystals point at the diol. The trap is diabetic ketoacidosis, which has no osmolar gap from an alcohol.

**Vignette 4.** A child with rickets spills glucose, amino acids, and bicarbonate. The urine can acidify when the plasma bicarbonate is very low.
**Reasoning.** The whole proximal tubule is failing. Fanconi with a type 2 acidosis. Type 1 cannot acidify at all and does not spill glucose.

**Vignette 5.** Struvite in a staghorn and a urine pH of 8. The culture grows a urease-positive rod.
**Reasoning.** Urea was split to ammonia. Proteus is the classic. Calcium oxalate likes a different chemistry and is not a staghorn from infection. The trap is calling every staghorn uric acid. Uric acid is acidic and radiolucent.

## Checklist

- [ ] Name the segment before the diuretic.
- [ ] ADH is water. Aldosterone is sodium and potassium.
- [ ] Gap versus non-gap before the toxin list.
- [ ] Type 4 is the hyperkalemic one.
- [ ] Red-cell casts are glomerular until the sediment says otherwise.

## Common traps

- Using urine sodium alone. A single number without osmolality and the clinical volume will lie.
- Calling every metabolic alkalosis “vomiting.” Contraction, diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, and antacid excess are different mechanisms.
- Forgetting that loop diuretics waste calcium and thiazides tend to save it. Stones care.
- Treating chronic kidney disease staging here. eGFR and albuminuria categories are a KDIGO clinical tool, used in the later books.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Acid-base items are a sequence: pH, which process is primary, whether compensation is adequate, then the gap. Do them in that order every time. Feed cards on diuretic site and on RTA type should match this nephron, not a nickname.
