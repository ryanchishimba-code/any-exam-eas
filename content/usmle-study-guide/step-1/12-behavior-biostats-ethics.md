# Behavior, biostatistics, and ethics

> **Study aid only.** How a study is built, and the ethical move the item wants. This is not legal advice and it is not a promise about any exam result.

## What this chapter covers

The statistics and study designs that are about 4–6% of Step 1, the communication tasks that are about 6–9%, and the behavioral diagnoses as mechanisms rather than checklists. These sections are small in the table and large in the number of “easy” points people postpone.

## The framework

Every biostatistics item is a 2-by-2 table plus one question: “Of whom?”

| Measure | Of whom |
| --- | --- |
| Sensitivity | Of the people who have the disease, how many test positive. A sensitive test, when negative, helps rule a disease out (few false negatives). |
| Specificity | Of the people who do not have the disease, how many test negative. A specific test, when positive, helps rule a disease in. |
| Positive predictive value | Of the people who test positive, how many have the disease. This falls when the disease is rare. |
| Negative predictive value | Of the people who test negative, how many are free of disease. This rises when the disease is rare. |
| Number needed to treat | 1 divided by the absolute risk reduction. Not 1 divided by the relative risk. |
| Odds ratio | The case-control measure. You cannot get incidence from a case-control study. |
| Relative risk | The cohort measure. Incidence in exposed over incidence in unexposed. |

A confidence interval that crosses the null (1 for a ratio, 0 for a difference) is a result that has not excluded “no effect.” A p value is not the probability that the null is true. It is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null were true. Power is the chance of finding a real effect. Low power makes a negative study uninformative, not reassuring.

| Design | What it can do | What it cannot do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Randomized trial | Assign the exposure. Best shot at causality if it is large and blinded. | Still can be biased if people drop out differently. |
| Cohort | Follow exposed and unexposed forward. Relative risk. | Confounding. Rare diseases are painful. |
| Case-control | Start with disease. Odds ratio. Good for rare diseases. | Cannot give you incidence. Recall bias. |
| Cross-sectional | A snapshot. Prevalence. | Cannot order cause and effect. |
| Case series | Describes. | Proves nothing about cause. |

Bias is a systematic miss, not random noise. Selection bias is who got into the study. Recall bias is who remembers. Lead-time bias makes a screened disease look like it lasts longer because you found it earlier. Length-time bias makes screening look better because slow diseases are easier to catch.

## Ethics as a sequence

1. Does this person have capacity for this decision? Capacity is clinical and decision-specific. Competence is a legal finding. Do not swap the words.
2. If yes, their informed refusal stands, even if you disagree, once you have explained the consequence.
3. If no, use a surrogate and the patient’s previously stated wishes, then best interest.
4. Confidentiality yields when a specific person is at serious risk or when a reportable duty exists. It does not yield to curiosity.
5. A gift, a relationship, or a research payment that changes your recommendation is a conflict. Disclose it and step away from the decision when you can.
6. You do not invent a result to motivate someone. Truth is the default. How you say a hard truth is the communication skill.

Behavioral items on Step 1 are often the neurotransmitter or the conditioning schedule, not the full DSM interview. Positive reinforcement adds a reward. Negative reinforcement removes a pain. Punishment adds a pain or removes a reward. Extinction is what happens when the reinforcement stops. Classical conditioning pairs two stimuli. Operant conditioning pairs a behavior with a consequence.

## Checklist

- [ ] Say “of whom” before you compute.
- [ ] Predictive value moves with prevalence. Sensitivity does not.
- [ ] Case-control gives odds, not incidence.
- [ ] Capacity is this decision, today. It is not a personality judgment.
- [ ] Lead time is not survival.

## Common traps

- Treating a relative risk of 0.5 as “50% of people are saved.” That is the absolute reduction’s job, and only if you were given the baseline risk.
- Calling a wide confidence interval “significant” because the point estimate looks dramatic.
- Breaking confidentiality for a family member who is worried but not at risk.
- Using a screening test in a population where the disease is so rare that almost every positive is false, and then calling the test “bad” rather than calling the prevalence low.

Do calculation items with a drawn table, not with a memorized phrase. Then do one ethics item and name the principle you used (capacity, truth, confidentiality) in four words. Feed cards that are only a formula are fine after you can say “of whom.” This book does not invent a passing percentage or an item count to motivate you.
