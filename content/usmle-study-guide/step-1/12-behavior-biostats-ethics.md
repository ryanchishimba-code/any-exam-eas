# Behavior, biostatistics, and ethics

> **Study aid only.** How a study is built, and the ethical move the item wants. This is not legal advice and it is not a promise about any exam result.

## What this chapter covers

The statistics and study designs that are about 4–6% of Step 1, the communication tasks that are about 6–9%, and the behavioral diagnoses as mechanisms rather than checklists. These sections are small in the table and large in the number of “easy” points people postpone.

## The framework

Every biostatistics item is a 2-by-2 table plus one question: “Of whom?”

| Measure | Of whom |
| --- | --- |
| Sensitivity | Of the people who have the disease, how many test positive. A sensitive test, when negative, helps rule a disease out (few false negatives). |
| Specificity | Of the people who do not have the disease, how many test negative. A specific test, when positive, helps rule a disease in. |
| Positive predictive value | Of the people who test positive, how many have the disease. This falls when the disease is rare. |
| Negative predictive value | Of the people who test negative, how many are free of disease. This rises when the disease is rare. |
| Number needed to treat | 1 divided by the absolute risk reduction. Not 1 divided by the relative risk. |
| Odds ratio | The case-control measure. You cannot get incidence from a case-control study. |
| Relative risk | The cohort measure. Incidence in exposed over incidence in unexposed. |

A confidence interval that crosses the null (1 for a ratio, 0 for a difference) is a result that has not excluded “no effect.” A p value is not the probability that the null is true. It is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null were true. Power is the chance of finding a real effect. Low power makes a negative study uninformative, not reassuring.

| Design | What it can do | What it cannot do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Randomized trial | Assign the exposure. Best shot at causality if it is large and blinded. | Still can be biased if people drop out differently. |
| Cohort | Follow exposed and unexposed forward. Relative risk. | Confounding. Rare diseases are painful. |
| Case-control | Start with disease. Odds ratio. Good for rare diseases. | Cannot give you incidence. Recall bias. |
| Cross-sectional | A snapshot. Prevalence. | Cannot order cause and effect. |
| Case series | Describes. | Proves nothing about cause. |

Bias is a systematic miss, not random noise. Selection bias is who got into the study. Recall bias is who remembers. Lead-time bias makes a screened disease look like it lasts longer because you found it earlier. Length-time bias makes screening look better because slow diseases are easier to catch.

## Ethics as a sequence

1. Does this person have capacity for this decision? Capacity is clinical and decision-specific. Competence is a legal finding. Do not swap the words.
2. If yes, their informed refusal stands, even if you disagree, once you have explained the consequence.
3. If no, use a surrogate and the patient’s previously stated wishes, then best interest.
4. Confidentiality yields when a specific person is at serious risk or when a reportable duty exists. It does not yield to curiosity.
5. A gift, a relationship, or a research payment that changes your recommendation is a conflict. Disclose it and step away from the decision when you can.
6. You do not invent a result to motivate someone. Truth is the default. How you say a hard truth is the communication skill.

Behavioral items on Step 1 are often the neurotransmitter or the conditioning schedule, not the full DSM interview. Positive reinforcement adds a reward. Negative reinforcement removes a pain. Punishment adds a pain or removes a reward. Extinction is what happens when the reinforcement stops. Classical conditioning pairs two stimuli. Operant conditioning pairs a behavior with a consequence.

### Sensitivity and the rule-out

Sensitivity is true positives over everyone who has the disease. A sensitive test, when negative, helps rule a disease out because false negatives are rare.

**Vignette.** A screening test is described as very sensitive and only fairly specific. The disease is uncommon. The patient just tested positive.
**Reasoning.** A positive result in a rare disease is often a false positive. Sensitivity does not rescue the positive predictive value. The diagnosis is Sensitivity and the rule-out.
**Pair.** Specificity, when high, makes a positive result more believable. Sensitivity makes a negative result more believable.
**NBME trap.** Telling a patient with a positive sensitive test that they have the disease.
**Hook.** SnOut. SpIn. Predictive value still needs prevalence.

### Predictive value and prevalence

Positive predictive value is true positives over everyone who tested positive. It falls when the disease is rare. Negative predictive value rises when the disease is rare.

**Vignette.** A test with unchanged lab performance has a much lower positive predictive value when it is used on healthy volunteers.
**Reasoning.** The prevalence fell. The test did not. Most positives are now false. The diagnosis is Predictive value and prevalence.
**Pair.** Sensitivity is a property of the test in the sick. Predictive value is a property of the test in this population.
**NBME trap.** Blaming the assay for a prevalence problem.
**Hook.** The test stays the same. The room changes the meaning of a positive.

### Number needed to treat

Absolute risk reduction is 3 percentage points. Number needed to treat is 1 divided by that difference, about 33. A relative risk reduction of 37% is the headline and is not the counseling number.

**Vignette.** A trial reports a 50% relative reduction. The control event rate was 2% and the treated rate was 1%.
**Reasoning.** One person in a hundred was spared. You treat about 100 to prevent one event. The diagnosis of the statistic is a small absolute benefit wearing a large relative headline. The diagnosis is Number needed to treat.
**Pair.** Number needed to harm uses the absolute increase in a bad event.
**NBME trap.** Saying half the patients were saved.
**Hook.** Subtract the percents, then invert.

### Case-control versus cohort

You cannot get incidence or a relative risk from a case-control study. You get an odds ratio. A cohort follows exposed and unexposed people and can give incidence.

**Vignette.** A study starts with 200 people who already have a rare sarcoma and 200 who do not, and asks about an old chemical.
**Reasoning.** Disease was the starting point. Time did not run forward from an exposure. The measure is an odds ratio. Recall bias is the built-in threat. The diagnosis is Case-control versus cohort.
**Pair.** A cohort starts with the exposure. A trial assigns it.
**NBME trap.** Computing incidence from cases and controls.
**Hook.** Cases first means odds. Exposure first means risk.

### Bias, named

Lead time makes survival look longer because the clock started earlier. Length time makes screening look better because slow diseases are easier to catch. Recall bias is memory that depends on the outcome. Selection bias is who got into the study.

**Vignette.** A new scan finds tumors two years earlier. The percent alive five years after diagnosis rises. The age at death does not change.
**Reasoning.** The diagnosis moved earlier. Life did not get longer. The diagnosis of the statistic is lead-time bias. The diagnosis is Bias, named.
**Pair.** Confounding is a third variable mixed into the exposure and the outcome. Bias is a systematic error in who was measured or how.
**NBME trap.** Celebrating a survival gain that is only an earlier clock.
**Hook.** Lead time is an earlier start. Length time is catching the slow ones.

### Confidence interval and the null

The interval includes 1, so the data have not excluded no effect. A p value is the chance of data at least this extreme if the null were true. It is not the chance the null is true.

**Vignette.** A small trial finds a dramatic drop in events and a p value of 0.2. The interval is wide.
**Reasoning.** The study was too small to be sure. A nonsignificant result is not proof of no effect. It is an uninformative study if power was low. The diagnosis is Confidence interval and the null.
**Pair.** A huge trial can make a tiny, useless difference statistically significant.
**NBME trap.** Equating nonsignificant with safe.
**Hook.** If the interval crosses the null, you have not won.

### Capacity for this decision

Capacity is clinical and decision-specific. It is not a diagnosis and not a legal finding. Competence is the legal word. A person with a serious mental illness can have capacity for this choice.

**Vignette.** A man with well-described schizophrenia refuses a hernia repair. He repeats the risks and the reason he prefers to wait, in his own words.
**Reasoning.** He understands this decision. The diagnosis does not erase that. The refusal stands. The diagnosis is Capacity for this decision.
**Pair.** A delirious person who cannot stay on the topic lacks capacity for a new complex decision even if they were capable last month.
**NBME trap.** Letting a family member overrule a capacitated patient.
**Hook.** This decision, today, in their own words.

### Confidentiality and its edges

Confidentiality yields to a defined duty to protect a named third party or to a reportable condition. It does not yield to a curious relative.

**Vignette.** An adult patient asks you not to tell his spouse the diagnosis. He understands it. He is not threatening anyone.
**Reasoning.** The information is his. Worry is not a duty to breach. The diagnosis of the ethics problem is a request for privacy that you honor. The diagnosis is Confidentiality and its edges.
**Pair.** A credible threat against a named person is a different duty. The jurisdiction defines the steps. Do not invent a statute.
**NBME trap.** Calling the spouse because it would be kinder.
**Hook.** A named person in danger is not the same as a worried relative.

### Reinforcement schedules

Positive reinforcement adds a reward. Negative reinforcement removes a pain. Punishment adds a pain or removes a reward. Extinction is what happens when the reinforcement stops. Variable-ratio schedules are hard to extinguish.

**Vignette.** A child cleans the room and the parent stops nagging. The cleaning increases.
**Reasoning.** A pain was removed after the behavior. That is negative reinforcement, not punishment. Punishment would have decreased the behavior. The diagnosis is Reinforcement schedules.
**Pair.** Classical conditioning pairs two stimuli. Operant conditioning pairs a behavior with a consequence.
**NBME trap.** Using negative to mean bad.
**Hook.** Negative reinforcement still increases the behavior.

### Defense mechanisms that items use

Splitting keeps people all good or all bad. Reaction formation does the opposite of the forbidden feeling. Projection gives your feeling to someone else. Sublimation turns the impulse into a useful act. Denial refuses the fact. These are descriptions, not insults.

**Vignette.** A man who wants to shout at his boss goes home and organizes a charity drive with unusual energy.
**Reasoning.** The aggressive impulse was turned into a socially valued act. The diagnosis of the mechanism is sublimation. The trap is reaction formation, which would be exaggerated politeness toward the boss, not a separate good project. The diagnosis is Defense mechanisms that items use.
**Pair.** Reaction formation stays aimed at the same object. Sublimation changes the channel.
**NBME trap.** Pathologizing a mature defense.
**Hook.** Sublimation builds something. Reaction formation flips the sign.

### Developmental tasks the exam still asks

Erikson’s stages are a vocabulary the exam uses: trust, autonomy, initiative, industry, identity. Piaget: sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete, formal. You need the age-to-task match, not the biography.

**Vignette.** A seven-year-old is proud of collecting every badge at school and crushed by a missed homework.
**Reasoning.** The work of the school-age child is competence. The diagnosis of the stage is industry versus inferiority. The trap is identity, which is the adolescent task. The diagnosis is Developmental tasks the exam still asks.
**Pair.** Autonomy is the toddler’s no. Initiative is the preschooler’s plans. Industry is the schoolchild’s skill.
**NBME trap.** Using a stage name from the wrong decade of life.
**Hook.** No, then plans, then skills, then identity.

### Study designs in one grid

A trial assigns. A cohort follows. A case-control looks back from disease. A cross-section is a snapshot and cannot order cause. A case series describes and proves nothing about cause.

**Vignette.** Everyone in a town is asked, once, whether they have a cough and whether they smoke.
**Reasoning.** One moment, both the exposure and the outcome. You cannot tell which came first. The diagnosis of the design is cross-sectional. The diagnosis is Study designs in one grid.
**Pair.** A cohort would have started with smokers and nonsmokers and waited for coughs.
**NBME trap.** Inferring causation from a single survey.
**Hook.** One time point cannot order a cause.

## Stem craft

A table, a design, or a decision. Say of whom before you compute.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Control event rate 10%, treated event rate 6%. The abstract says the risk was reduced by 40%.
**Reasoning.** Absolute reduction is 4 points. You treat 25 people to prevent one event. Forty percent is the relative headline. The trap is telling the patient their risk fell by almost half in a way that sounds like 40 people in 100.

**Vignette 2.** A trial assigns the drug. People who stop the drug are removed from the analysis, and the benefit looks larger.
**Reasoning.** That is per-protocol, and it flatters the drug. Intention to treat keeps them in the assigned group. The trap is believing the cleaned analysis is the more honest one.

**Vignette 3.** Mothers of children with a birth defect remember a drug more often than mothers of healthy children, in a retrospective interview.
**Reasoning.** Recall bias. A cohort that recorded the drug before the birth would not have this problem. The trap is calling it confounding.

**Vignette 4.** A patient with delirium cannot repeat the risks of a surgery. Last month, before the infection, he could.
**Reasoning.** Capacity is this decision today. It can return. The trap is a permanent label of incompetence from one delirious afternoon.

**Vignette 5.** A behavior that is rewarded unpredictably is very hard to stop.
**Reasoning.** Variable-ratio schedule. A weekly paycheck is fixed interval and extinguishes more easily. The trap is calling every persistent behavior an addiction.

## Another pass on the stems

More table and ethics stems.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** A relative risk of 2.0 with a confidence interval from 0.9 to 4.5.
**Reasoning.** The point estimate looks like a doubling. The interval includes 1. You have not excluded no effect. The trap is the point estimate alone.

**Vignette 2.** Screening finds cancers that are slow. The case fatality among screen-detected cancers looks wonderful. Fast cancers were already symptomatic.
**Reasoning.** Length-time bias. Lead time is a different trick: the clock starts earlier. The trap is calling the program a cure.

**Vignette 3.** A capacitated patient refuses a transfusion. The family demands it. He understands he may die.
**Reasoning.** His refusal stands. The family is not the decision maker while he has capacity. The trap is treating family distress as consent.

**Vignette 4.** A child is rewarded every fifth time he shares, on average, but not on a fixed count. The sharing is very persistent.
**Reasoning.** Variable ratio. Fixed ratio extinguishes more cleanly when the reward stops. The trap is calling this negative reinforcement. Nothing painful was removed.

## Checklist

- [ ] Say “of whom” before you compute.
- [ ] Predictive value moves with prevalence. Sensitivity does not.
- [ ] Case-control gives odds, not incidence.
- [ ] Capacity is this decision, today. It is not a personality judgment.
- [ ] Lead time is not survival.

## Common traps

- Treating a relative risk of 0.5 as “50% of people are saved.” That is the absolute reduction’s job, and only if you were given the baseline risk.
- Calling a wide confidence interval “significant” because the point estimate looks dramatic.
- Breaking confidentiality for a family member who is worried but not at risk.
- Using a screening test in a population where the disease is so rare that almost every positive is false, and then calling the test “bad” rather than calling the prevalence low.
