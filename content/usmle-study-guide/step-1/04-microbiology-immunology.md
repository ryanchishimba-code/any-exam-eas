# Microbiology and immunology

> **Study aid only.** Organism to mechanism to host defense. Treatment choice by name of guideline is a Step 2 and Step 3 skill.

## What this chapter covers

How to file an organism in one line, how the immune system actually clears it, and the immunodeficiency patterns that are pure mechanism. Blood and immune content is a large Step 1 system range. Microbiology as a discipline is listed at about 10–20%.

## The framework

File every organism with four words: Gram or not, shape, where it lives (inside or outside cells), and the host defense that matters. If you know the defense, the immunodeficiency and the vaccine logic follow.

| Group | Defense that clears it | When that defense is gone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Extracellular bacteria | Antibody, complement, neutrophils | Encapsulated organisms after the spleen is gone. Neisseria when terminal complement is gone. |
| Intracellular bacteria and fungi | T cells and macrophages | Granulomatous disease, IFN-gamma problems, HIV with low CD4 |
| Viruses | CD8, NK, interferon, antibody for prevention | T-cell defects bring viruses and opportunistic infection |
| Parasites, some | Eosinophils and IgE for helminths | Not every parasite. Protozoa are a different story. |

Gram-positive cocci in clusters that are catalase-positive are staphylococci. Coagulase splits aureus from the rest. Gram-positive cocci in chains or pairs that are catalase-negative are streptococci. Hemolysis and the lancefield group do the rest. Do not start from the disease name.

## Immune system in one pass

Innate is fast and not antigen-specific: barriers, neutrophils, macrophages, NK, complement. Adaptive is slower and specific: B cells (antibody) and T cells.

MHC I is on all nucleated cells and shows intracellular peptides to CD8. MHC II is on antigen-presenting cells and shows extracellular peptides to CD4. A virus that lives in the cytosol is a CD8 problem. A bacterium in a vesicle of a macrophage is a CD4 and IFN-gamma problem. Chronic granulomatous disease is neutrophils that cannot make the oxidative burst, so catalase-positive organisms persist. Leukocyte adhesion deficiency is neutrophils that cannot get to the tissue, so the leukocyte count is high and pus is absent.

Complement: classical needs antibody, alternative can start on a microbial surface, lectin uses mannose-binding lectin. All three meet at C3. Terminal complement (the membrane attack complex) is why terminal-complement deficiency presents with Neisseria.

Antibodies: IgM is the early pentamer and fixes complement. IgG crosses the placenta and is the memory response. IgA is mucosal. IgE is mast cells and helminths.

Vaccines follow the same logic. A polysaccharide vaccine is a T-independent antigen and a poor response in infants, which is why conjugates exist. A live vaccine needs a working T-cell system. A toxoid teaches antibody against the toxin, not against colonization.

## High-yield atlas

File every organism in one line: what it looks like, whether it lives inside cells, and which host defense would have cleared it. The disease name comes last. Treatment regimens are a Step 2 and Step 3 skill and follow IDSA or CDC. This page is the mechanism.

### The Gram stain is a decision tree, not a color

| What you see | Next split | The organisms that live there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gram-positive cocci in clusters, catalase positive | Coagulase | Positive is Staphylococcus aureus. Negative is the skin staphylococci |
| Gram-positive cocci in chains or pairs, catalase negative | Hemolysis, then the group | Group A strep is bacitracin-sensitive and causes the pyogenic and the immune-late diseases. Group B colonizes the vagina and sickens newborns. Enterococcus grows in bile and salt. Pneumococcus is optochin-sensitive and is the encapsulated diplococcus |
| Gram-positive rods | Spores, branching, or diphtheria-shaped | Clostridia are anaerobic spore-formers. Bacillus is aerobic. Listeria tumbles and likes the cold and the placenta. Corynebacterium makes a toxin that ADP-ribosylates elongation factor 2. Actinomyces is anaerobic and filamentous. Nocardia is aerobic, partially acid-fast, and filamentous |
| Gram-negative diplococci | Maltose, or the clinical site | Meningococcus ferments maltose and can devastate a person with a terminal-complement defect. Gonococcus does not ferment maltose and is a mucosal pathogen |
| Gram-negative rods, enteric | Lactose, oxidase, and the capsule | E. coli is the usual lactose fermenter and the usual urinary organism. Klebsiella is mucoid. Proteus swarms and makes urease and struvite stones. Salmonella and Shigella do not ferment lactose. Pseudomonas is oxidase positive, aerobic, and a water organism |
| Gram-negative curved rods | The syndrome | Vibrio is rice-water and shellfish. Campylobacter is bloody diarrhea after poultry and a Guillain-Barré association. Helicobacter is the urease in the stomach |
| No Gram reaction that helps | The special stain or the cell it lives in | Mycobacteria are acid-fast. Mycoplasma has no wall. Chlamydia is an intracellular bacterium with a odd cycle. Rickettsia and Ehrlichia live inside cells and are tick- or louse-borne. Spirochetes need darkfield or a serology |

**Vignette.** A teenager in a dormitory has fever, a stiff neck, and a rapidly spreading purple rash. A roommate was well yesterday.
**Reasoning.** A fast meningitic illness with purpura is an encapsulated organism that can ride the blood and consume the adrenal or the skin vessels. The diagnosis is meningococcal disease until the culture says otherwise. The trap is “any pneumonia coccus,” which is also encapsulated but does not own this rash. Terminal complement deficiency is the host clue when the infections repeat.

**Hook.** Clusters and catalase: staph. Chains and no catalase: strep. Maltose splits the two neisseriae.

### Toxins that are the whole question

| Organism | The toxin’s trick | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corynebacterium diphtheriae | ADP-ribosylates EF-2, stops protein synthesis | A pharyngeal membrane and a myocarditis or a neuropathy. The gene is from a phage |
| Pseudomonas aeruginosa | The same EF-2 trick, plus a capsule and a pigment | Pneumonia in a ventilated or a neutropenic patient, otitis in a swimmer, a hot-tub folliculitis |
| Vibrio cholerae | ADP-ribosylates a Gs protein, cAMP stays high, chloride pours into the gut | Rice-water stool. No invasion, so no fecal leukocytes |
| E. coli, heat-labile toxin | The same Gs trick | Watery traveler’s diarrhea |
| E. coli, heat-stable toxin | Turns on guanylate cyclase | Also watery. The messenger is cGMP, not cAMP |
| Bordetella pertussis | ADP-ribosylates Gi, so adenylate cyclase stays active | Whooping cough. The lymphocyte count is high because the toxin blocks them from entering nodes |
| Clostridium tetani | Blocks glycine and GABA release | Lockjaw and spasms. The sensory system is intact |
| Clostridium botulinum | Blocks acetylcholine release | Descending flaccid paralysis. The can was bulging, or the honey was given to an infant |
| Clostridium perfringens | An alpha toxin that is a lecithinase | Gas gangrene, or a late diarrhea after food left warm |
| Clostridium difficile | Toxins that glycosylate cytoskeletal regulators | Colitis after antibiotics. The drug names change. The mechanism is a toxin, and the inciting antibiotic should stop |
| Shigella and enterohemorrhagic E. coli | Shiga toxin stops the 60S ribosome | Bloody diarrhea. The E. coli version is linked to hemolytic uremic syndrome. Antibiotics for that E. coli are not a reflex |
| Streptococcus pyogenes | Spe toxins are superantigens | Scarlet fever, or a toxic shock. Rheumatic fever and post-strep glomerulonephritis are immune, not the toxin sitting in the organ |
| Staphylococcus aureus | TSST-1 is a superantigen. Enterotoxin is preformed in food. Exfoliatin splits the skin | Toxic shock with a retained packing or a wound. Vomiting within hours of a picnic. A scalded-skin picture in a child |

**Vignette.** Six hours after a picnic potato salad, several people vomit. Nobody has a fever. They are better by morning.
**Reasoning.** A toxin that was already made in the food hits fast and does not need the organism to colonize. The diagnosis is staphylococcal food poisoning. The trap is Salmonella, which invades, takes a day or more, and causes fever. Bacillus cereus fried rice can look the same and is the other preformed-toxin vomit.

**Pair.** Tetanus is too much motor firing because inhibition was blocked. Botulism is no firing because acetylcholine never left the nerve. Both are clostridial. The muscle exam is the opposite.

**NBME trap.** They say “ADP-ribosylation” and offer four bacteria. Split them by the target: EF-2 (diphtheria, Pseudomonas), Gs (cholera, labile E. coli), Gi (pertussis).

### Viruses by the one fact that sorts them

You do not need every virus. You need the family trick.

| Family trick | Examples | The stem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Double-stranded DNA, a latency in a ganglion | Herpes simplex, varicella zoster | A recurrent blister on a lip or a dermatome. The virus was never gone |
| Double-stranded DNA, a CD21 receptor, heterophile-positive mono | Epstein-Barr | A teenager, sore throat, posterior nodes, and a spleen you must not tackle. A rash if someone gave ampicillin |
| Double-stranded DNA, a negative monospot, congenital hearing loss | Cytomegalovirus | An owl’s-eye inclusion. A transplant patient. A newborn who failed the hearing screen |
| Double-stranded DNA, a slap-cheek then a lacy rash, and a sickle crisis | Parvovirus B19 | Fifth disease. Pure red-cell aplasia in a person who needed those precursors |
| Double-stranded DNA, a vaccine that changed a cancer’s epidemiology | Human papillomavirus | Types that integrate and inactivate Rb and p53. The wart types are not the cancer types |
| Partial double-stranded DNA, a reverse transcriptase, a vaccine | Hepatitis B | Ground-glass hepatocytes. The surface antigen is the vaccine. The e antigen means infectious |
| Positive-sense RNA, a fecal-oral hepatitis that does not become chronic | Hepatitis A | A traveler or a shellfish story. No carrier state |
| Positive-sense RNA, a flavivirus, chronic, and a drug target | Hepatitis C | Blood exposure. Cryoglobulins and a porphyria cutanea tarda association |
| Negative-sense segmented RNA | Influenza | Drift is a small change. Shift is a reassortment and a pandemic |
| Negative-sense bullet, Negri bodies | Rabies | A bite, then hydrophobia. The vaccine and immunoglobulin are time-sensitive. Open the CDC page for the protocol |
| Double-stranded segmented RNA, a winter infant diarrhea | Rotavirus | A toxin-like effect on the gut. A vaccine exists |
| Retrovirus, CD4 and a progressive loss of cell-mediated defense | HIV | The acute flu-like illness, then a latent period, then organisms that T cells used to clear |

**Vignette.** A college student has sore throat, fatigue, and posterior cervical nodes. The spleen tip is palpable. A rapid strep test is negative. Someone prescribes ampicillin and a rash appears.
**Reasoning.** Posterior nodes and a spleen, plus a rash when ampicillin is added, is the classic Epstein-Barr picture. The rash is not a true penicillin allergy. The diagnosis is infectious mononucleosis. The trap is stopping “all beta-lactams forever.” The spleen is why contact sports wait.

**Hook.** Latency in a nerve: herpes and zoster. Latency in a B cell: Epstein-Barr. A segmented genome that can reassort: influenza.

### Fungi and parasites by the picture, not the life cycle poster

| Picture | Organism | Host clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Broad-based budding yeast in the lung, then the bone or the skin | Blastomyces | The Ohio and Mississippi valleys, and the Great Lakes |
| Intracellular yeast in a macrophage | Histoplasma | The same river valleys. A bird or bat exposure. Looks like tuberculosis |
| Spherules packed with endospores | Coccidioides | The desert. Erythema nodosum can mean the immune system is winning |
| Soap-bubble yeast in an AIDS brain | Cryptococcus | A positive ink or an antigen. Pigeons are the association, not a requirement |
| Acute-angle branching septate hyphae in a neutropenic sinus or lung | Aspergillus | A fungus ball is the saprophytic version in an old cavity |
| Right-angle, ribbon-like hyphae in a ketoacidotic or a neutropenic face | Mucor | Invades vessels. The glucose and the iron stories matter |
| Spaghetti-and-meatballs on a skin scraping | Malassezia | A blotchy trunk after sun. Not a systemic disease |
| Banana gametocytes, fever in cycles | Plasmodium | Travel. Falciparum is the one that clogs capillaries and does not wait |
| A migrating larva and an iron deficiency | Hookworm | Bare feet. The anemia is the point |
| Perianal itch at night, a tape test | Pinworm | The whole household. Not a cause of iron deficiency |
| A cyst in the liver or a seizure with a cystic brain lesion after pork or after dog exposure | Taenia solium or Echinococcus | The cyst’s location is the disease. Do not rupture a hydatid cyst casually |

**Vignette.** A person with diabetic ketoacidosis has a black eschar on the palate and a cranial nerve palsy. The hyphae in the tissue are broad and barely septate.
**Reasoning.** An acidotic, high-sugar host plus a vessel-invading mold in the face is mucormycosis. The diagnosis is that mold. The trap is Aspergillus, which branches at a narrow angle and prefers neutropenia over ketoacidosis, though both can invade. The angle of the branch is the split.

### Immunodeficiency is an organism list

| What is missing | The infections | The other clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antibodies, or the spleen | Encapsulated bacteria | A missing spleen also means Howell-Jolly bodies and a vaccine problem |
| Terminal complement | Neisseria | Meningococcus and gonococcus, repeatedly |
| Neutrophils, or their oxidative burst | Catalase-positive organisms and fungi | Chronic granulomatous disease. A negative nitroblue tetrazolium or the modern oxidative test |
| Leukocyte adhesion | A high neutrophil count that cannot get to the tissue | No pus. Delayed umbilical separation |
| C3 | Severe pyogenic infections | The shared step of all three complement paths |
| T cells | Viruses, fungi, Pneumocystis, intracellular bacteria | A low CD4. Live vaccines are unsafe |
| Phagocyte AND T cell, as in a severe combined defect | Almost everything, early | A baby who does not thrive and cannot be given live vaccines |
| NADPH oxidase specifically | The catalase-positive list: Staph, Burkholderia, Serratia, Nocardia, Aspergillus | The organisms that bring their own catalase survive inside the neutrophil |

**Vignette.** A boy has had abscesses with Staphylococcus and Serratia and a pneumonia with Aspergillus. His neutrophil count is normal. The abscesses are full of organisms and a disordered granuloma.
**Reasoning.** The cells arrive but cannot make the oxidative burst, so catalase-positive organisms are not killed. The diagnosis is chronic granulomatous disease. The trap is neutropenia, which would show a low count. The count here is normal.

**NBME trap.** They mix up “no pus” (adhesion) and “pus full of uneaten organisms” (oxidative burst). The leukocyte count is high in the adhesion defect and normal in the burst defect.

### Vaccines follow the same defense

A polysaccharide is a T-independent antigen and a poor infant response, which is why conjugates exist: the protein recruits T cells. A toxoid teaches antibody against the toxin, not against colonization (tetanus, diphtheria). A live vaccine needs working T cells and is not for pregnancy or for severe immunosuppression. A killed or a subunit vaccine can be given when live vaccines cannot. Hepatitis B surface antigen is a subunit. The HPV vaccine is a subunit of the capsid and does not contain viral DNA.

**Vignette.** An asplenic adult is due for vaccines. Someone offers the old polysaccharide pneumococcal vaccine alone and skips the conjugate.
**Reasoning.** The conjugate recruits T-cell help and is the backbone of current schedules. The polysaccharide alone is the weaker teacher. The diagnosis of the error is using a T-independent antigen as the whole plan. Confirm the current CDC sequence. The trap is “any pneumococcal vaccine is the same.”

### Worked pairs

| A | B | Split |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food poisoning, hours, vomiting | Invasive diarrhea, a day later, fever | Preformed toxin versus a growing organism |
| Tetanus | Botulism | Spasm versus flaccid paralysis |
| Meningococcus | Gonococcus | Maltose, purpura, and complement versus mucosa |
| Aspergillus | Mucor | Narrow septate hyphae and neutropenia versus broad hyphae and ketoacidosis |
| Epstein-Barr | Cytomegalovirus | Heterophile positive versus negative, and the newborn hearing story |
| Toxic shock | Septic shock from any bacterium | A superantigen and sometimes a retained foreign body, versus a bloodstream organism |
| Rheumatic fever | Post-strep glomerulonephritis | Antibodies after pharyngitis, a heart and a joint, versus a kidney that can follow skin or throat, and antibiotics that prevent only the rheumatic path |
| Live vaccine | Subunit vaccine | Needs T cells versus safe when T cells are gone |

**Vignette.** Three weeks after a sore throat that was not treated, a child has smoky urine, a little edema, and a low complement. The heart is normal.
**Reasoning.** Immune complexes after a nephritogenic strain land in the glomerulus. The diagnosis is post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. The trap is rheumatic fever, which wants the heart, the joint, or the movement disorder, and which antibiotics can prevent. Antibiotics do not reliably prevent this kidney. The timing of weeks and the low complement are the complex, a type III picture.

## NBME worked set

The stain, the toxin target, and the defense that failed.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Six hours after a picnic, several people vomit and have no fever. They are well by morning.
**Reasoning.** A preformed toxin. Staphylococcal food poisoning or Bacillus cereus. Salmonella takes a day and causes fever. The trap is an invasive organism for a six-hour illness.

**Vignette 2.** Rice-water stool, no fecal white cells, and a Gs protein that will not turn off.
**Reasoning.** Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates Gs. Labile E. coli toxin does the same. The trap is an invasive dysentery, which would show white cells.

**Vignette 3.** A pharyngeal membrane and a myocarditis. The toxin ADP-ribosylates EF-2.
**Reasoning.** Diphtheria. Pseudomonas uses the same trick on EF-2 but the stem is a wound or a ventilated lung, not a membrane. The trap is lumping every ADP-ribosylation as cholera.

**Vignette 4.** Lockjaw after a dirty puncture. Sensation is intact.
**Reasoning.** Tetanus blocks inhibitory transmitters. Botulism would be flaccid and descending. The trap is a seizure.

**Vignette 5.** A teenager in a dorm has fever, neck stiffness, and purpura. A classmate was well yesterday.
**Reasoning.** Meningococcus. Pneumococcus is also encapsulated but does not own this rash. Terminal complement deficiency is the repeat-infection clue.

**Vignette 6.** A college student gets a rash after ampicillin for a sore throat. The spleen tip is palpable. A rapid strep test is negative.
**Reasoning.** Epstein-Barr virus. The rash is not a lifelong penicillin allergy. The trap is labeling the allergy and missing the spleen.

**Vignette 7.** Black eschar on the palate of a patient in ketoacidosis. Hyphae are broad and barely septate.
**Reasoning.** Mucor. Aspergillus is narrow and septate and prefers neutropenia. The angle of the branch is the split.

**Vignette 8.** A boy has abscesses with Serratia and a pneumonia with Aspergillus. The neutrophil count is normal.
**Reasoning.** Chronic granulomatous disease. The cells cannot make the oxidative burst. Leukocyte adhesion deficiency would have a high count and no pus. The trap is neutropenia.

**Vignette 9.** Bloody diarrhea, then a low platelet count and a rising creatinine. Antibiotics were being considered for the E. coli.
**Reasoning.** Shiga toxin and hemolytic uremic syndrome. Antibiotics are not a reflex here. The trap is treating it like ordinary traveler’s diarrhea.

**Vignette 10.** An asplenic patient is offered only an old polysaccharide vaccine.
**Reasoning.** A conjugate recruits T cells. Polysaccharide alone is a weak teacher in this host. Confirm the current CDC sequence. The trap is any pneumococcal vaccine is the same.

## Stem craft

Toxin targets and the defense that failed.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** A whooping cough and a very high lymphocyte count. The toxin ADP-ribosylates Gi.
**Reasoning.** Pertussis. Cholera hits Gs and causes diarrhea, not a cough. The lymphocytes cannot enter nodes, so they sit in the blood. The trap is a leukemia for the high count.

**Vignette 2.** Descending paralysis and big pupils after home-canned food.
**Reasoning.** Botulinum blocked acetylcholine release. Tetanus would be spasms. Guillain-Barré ascends and the pupils are spared. The trap is myasthenia, which fluctuates and is not epidemic in a household.

**Vignette 3.** A target-shaped rash after a hike, then a facial palsy weeks later.
**Reasoning.** Borrelia. The rash is the early localization. The palsy is the dissemination. The trap is Bell palsy as a primary neurologic disease when the rash was in the stem.

**Vignette 4.** Owl-eye inclusions in a transplant lung, and a newborn who failed a hearing screen in a different stem that uses the same virus.
**Reasoning.** Cytomegalovirus. Epstein-Barr is the heterophile-positive mono. The trap is calling every mono-like illness Epstein-Barr.

**Vignette 5.** A child with sickle cell disease has aplastic crisis after a slapped-cheek rash in a sibling.
**Reasoning.** Parvovirus B19 stopped the red-cell precursors. In a normal marrow you do not notice. In a marrow that turns over fast, the hemoglobin falls. The trap is a pain crisis only.

## Checklist

- [ ] Intracellular versus extracellular before the drug class.
- [ ] Spleen loss: encapsulated bacteria. Terminal complement: Neisseria.
- [ ] CD8 reads MHC I. CD4 reads MHC II.
- [ ] Catalase and coagulase before the species name.
- [ ] Live vaccine is unsafe when T cells are gone.

## Common traps

- Treating every immunodeficiency as “more infections.” The organism list is the diagnosis.
- Calling IgM the memory antibody. Memory that crosses the placenta and lasts is IgG.
- Forgetting that a conjugate vaccine exists to recruit T cells. The polysaccharide alone does not.
- Listing opportunistic organisms by CD4 number you are not sure of. Learn the mechanism (cell-mediated defense lost) and confirm current thresholds in a Step 2 resource before you quote a count.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Step 1 microbiology items often give the stain, the shape, and one host clue. Answer with the defense that failed, then the organism. Feed cards that pair a toxin with a bug are the hook. If you cannot say whether the organism lives inside the cell, the card will not transfer to a new stem.
