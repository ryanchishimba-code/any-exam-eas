# Microbiology and immunology

> **Study aid only.** Organism to mechanism to host defense. Treatment choice by name of guideline is a Step 2 and Step 3 skill.

## What this chapter covers

How to file an organism in one line, how the immune system actually clears it, and the immunodeficiency patterns that are pure mechanism. Blood and immune content is a large Step 1 system range. Microbiology as a discipline is listed at about 10–20%.

## The framework

File every organism with four words: Gram or not, shape, where it lives (inside or outside cells), and the host defense that matters. If you know the defense, the immunodeficiency and the vaccine logic follow.

| Group | Defense that clears it | When that defense is gone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Extracellular bacteria | Antibody, complement, neutrophils | Encapsulated organisms after the spleen is gone. Neisseria when terminal complement is gone. |
| Intracellular bacteria and fungi | T cells and macrophages | Granulomatous disease, IFN-gamma problems, HIV with low CD4 |
| Viruses | CD8, NK, interferon, antibody for prevention | T-cell defects bring viruses and opportunistic infection |
| Parasites, some | Eosinophils and IgE for helminths | Not every parasite. Protozoa are a different story. |

Gram-positive cocci in clusters that are catalase-positive are staphylococci. Coagulase splits aureus from the rest. Gram-positive cocci in chains or pairs that are catalase-negative are streptococci. Hemolysis and the lancefield group do the rest. Do not start from the disease name.

## Immune system in one pass

Innate is fast and not antigen-specific: barriers, neutrophils, macrophages, NK, complement. Adaptive is slower and specific: B cells (antibody) and T cells.

MHC I is on all nucleated cells and shows intracellular peptides to CD8. MHC II is on antigen-presenting cells and shows extracellular peptides to CD4. A virus that lives in the cytosol is a CD8 problem. A bacterium in a vesicle of a macrophage is a CD4 and IFN-gamma problem. Chronic granulomatous disease is neutrophils that cannot make the oxidative burst, so catalase-positive organisms persist. Leukocyte adhesion deficiency is neutrophils that cannot get to the tissue, so the leukocyte count is high and pus is absent.

Complement: classical needs antibody, alternative can start on a microbial surface, lectin uses mannose-binding lectin. All three meet at C3. Terminal complement (the membrane attack complex) is why terminal-complement deficiency presents with Neisseria.

Antibodies: IgM is the early pentamer and fixes complement. IgG crosses the placenta and is the memory response. IgA is mucosal. IgE is mast cells and helminths.

Vaccines follow the same logic. A polysaccharide vaccine is a T-independent antigen and a poor response in infants, which is why conjugates exist. A live vaccine needs a working T-cell system. A toxoid teaches antibody against the toxin, not against colonization.

## Checklist

- [ ] Intracellular versus extracellular before the drug class.
- [ ] Spleen loss: encapsulated bacteria. Terminal complement: Neisseria.
- [ ] CD8 reads MHC I. CD4 reads MHC II.
- [ ] Catalase and coagulase before the species name.
- [ ] Live vaccine is unsafe when T cells are gone.

## Common traps

- Treating every immunodeficiency as “more infections.” The organism list is the diagnosis.
- Calling IgM the memory antibody. Memory that crosses the placenta and lasts is IgG.
- Forgetting that a conjugate vaccine exists to recruit T cells. The polysaccharide alone does not.
- Listing opportunistic organisms by CD4 number you are not sure of. Learn the mechanism (cell-mediated defense lost) and confirm current thresholds in a Step 2 resource before you quote a count.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Step 1 microbiology items often give the stain, the shape, and one host clue. Answer with the defense that failed, then the organism. Feed cards that pair a toxin with a bug are the hook. If you cannot say whether the organism lives inside the cell, the card will not transfer to a new stem.
