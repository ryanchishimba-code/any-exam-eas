# Pharmacology mechanisms

> **Study aid only.** Mechanisms and the danger that follows from the mechanism. Doses and “which drug to start” are Step 2 and Step 3 decisions.

## What this chapter covers

Autonomic receptors, the kinetics that change a graph, and the adverse effects that are the mechanism continuing one step too far. Step 1 pharmacology is about 10–20% of the form and is scattered through organ systems.

## The framework

A drug item is a receptor, a second messenger, and a tissue effect. If you know those three, the “side effect” is not a list. It is the same effect in the wrong tissue.

| Receptor | Messenger | Prototype effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha-1 | Gq, calcium up | Vessel constriction, pupil dilates, bladder sphincter tightens |
| Alpha-2 | Gi, cAMP down | Less sympathetic outflow, less insulin |
| Beta-1 | Gs, cAMP up | Heart rate and contractility up, renin up |
| Beta-2 | Gs, cAMP up | Bronchodilation, vasodilation, uterus relaxes, potassium shifts in |
| M1, M3 | Gq | Gland secretion, bronchoconstriction, detrusor contraction, pupil constricts |
| M2 | Gi | Heart rate down |
| Nicotinic | Ion channel | Ganglia and neuromuscular junction |

Gq is the “cut” pathway: phospholipase C, IP3, calcium. Gs makes cAMP. Gi lowers it. Learn the messenger once. Every later drug reuses it.

## Kinetics that show up as graphs

| Idea | What the graph is doing |
| --- | --- |
| Efficacy | How tall the curve gets. A partial agonist never reaches a full agonist. |
| Potency | Where the curve sits left or right. A smaller dose for the same effect is more potent. It is not “stronger.” |
| Competitive antagonist | Parallel right shift. More agonist can still win. |
| Noncompetitive antagonist | The ceiling falls. More agonist cannot fully win. |
| Therapeutic index | Distance between useful effect and toxicity. Narrow index means levels matter. |
| Zero-order elimination | A constant amount per hour. Phenytoin, ethanol, and high-dose aspirin are the classic examples. The half-life is not constant. |
| First-order elimination | A constant fraction per hour. Most drugs. Half-life is constant. |
| Volume of distribution | High when the drug leaves the blood for tissue. Loading dose cares about this. |
| Clearance | Maintenance dose cares about this. Liver, kidney, or both. |

Loading dose follows volume. Maintenance dose follows clearance. Do not swap them when a stem changes renal function: the maintenance dose changes, the loading dose mostly does not.

## Danger that is just the mechanism

- Muscarinic agonist: wet (saliva, tears, diarrhea, bronchospasm, bradycardia). Atropine is the competitive block.
- Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor: the same wet picture, plus muscle if it reaches the nicotinic junction. Organophosphates age. Pralidoxime before aging, plus atropine.
- Nondepolarizing neuromuscular block: competitive, train-of-four fades, reversed by raising acetylcholine. Succinylcholine is depolarizing: fasciculations, then a block that is not reversed the same way, and potassium leaves muscle.
- Beta-2 agonist: potassium goes into cells. Tremor and tachycardia are the price.
- Drug reaction types worth separating: exaggerated effect, organ toxicity, immune reaction, and a reaction that is neither dose nor immune (anaphylactoid, for example). Name the type before the antidote.

## High-yield atlas

Pharmacology on Step 1 is a receptor, a messenger, and the tissue that did not need the effect. Doses and “which drug to start on Tuesday” belong to the later books. If a stem gives a graph, read the ceiling and the shift before you read the drug name.

### Messengers, once, then every drug reuses them

Gq cuts PIP2 into IP3 and DAG. IP3 releases calcium. Gs raises cAMP. Gi lowers cAMP. A receptor tyrosine kinase dimerizes and phosphorylates. A steroid or thyroid hormone enters the cell and changes transcription. An ion channel does not need a second messenger at all.

| Receptor | Messenger | Where the effect shows | A drug that uses it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha-1 | Gq | Vessel constriction, pupil dilates, bladder sphincter tightens | Phenylephrine. The nose and the pupil are the same receptor |
| Alpha-2 | Gi | Less sympathetic outflow, less insulin release | Clonidine. A rebound in pressure if it is stopped suddenly is the sympathetic system waking up |
| Beta-1 | Gs | Heart rate and contractility up, renin up | Dobutamine. A beta blocker does the opposite |
| Beta-2 | Gs | Bronchi open, vessels in muscle open, uterus relaxes, potassium shifts into cells | Albuterol. Tremor and a low potassium are the same messenger |
| M1 and M3 | Gq | Glands, gut, bladder detrusor, bronchoconstriction, pupil constricts | Bethanechol. The wet, crampy, slow-heart picture if it is not selective in the stem |
| M2 | Gi | Heart rate down | The vagus. Atropine blocks it |
| Nicotinic, ganglion | Ion channel | Both sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia | A ganglion blocker drops the dominant tone of each organ |
| Nicotinic, muscle | Ion channel | The neuromuscular junction | The paralytics |
| D1 | Gs | Renal vessels open | A low-dose dopamine story. Higher doses recruit beta and then alpha |
| H1 | Gq | Itch, leak, bronchoconstriction | An antihistamine. It does not stop anaphylaxis once the mediators are out. Epinephrine does |
| H2 | Gs | Stomach acid | The acid-reduction story, not the itch story |
| V1 | Gq | Vessel constriction | The pressor face of vasopressin |
| V2 | Gs | Aquaporins in the collecting duct | Desmopressin. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus will not answer it |

**Vignette.** A man uses drops that dilate his pupil before an eye exam. His near vision is blurry and his nose is not running.
**Reasoning.** The dilator of the pupil is alpha-1. Cycloplegia, if present, is muscarinic block of the ciliary muscle. A pure alpha agonist dilates without paralyzing accommodation the way atropine does. The stem’s blur may be the loss of the small pupil’s depth of field, or a combined drop. The trap is “beta blocker,” which would not dilate the pupil. Name the iris muscle before the bottle.

**NBME trap.** They ask the messenger, not the drug. Students who memorized “albuterol opens bronchi” cannot say cAMP. The answer is the messenger.

**Hook.** Q is calcium. S is more cAMP. I is less cAMP. Steroids take longer because they rewrite the nucleus.

### Graphs: efficacy, potency, and the antagonist

Efficacy is the height of the curve. Potency is how far left it sits. A partial agonist never reaches a full agonist’s ceiling, and it can block a full agonist by occupying the receptor. That is why a partial agonist can both help and antagonize.

A competitive antagonist shifts the curve right, in parallel. Enough agonist still wins. A noncompetitive antagonist, or an irreversible one, lowers the ceiling. More agonist cannot fully win. Spare receptors are why a tissue can still reach the old ceiling after some receptors are blocked: the curve shifts, then, past a point, the ceiling falls.

| Picture | What it is | The clinical rhyme |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curve shifts right, same height | Competitive block, or less potency | More agonist overcomes it |
| Curve’s top falls | Noncompetitive block, fewer receptors, or a partial agonist’s limit | More drug does not restore the old maximum |
| Two curves, one never as tall | Partial versus full agonist | The short one can antagonize the tall one |
| A narrow gap between useful effect and harm | Small therapeutic index | Levels matter. Warfarin, lithium, digoxin, phenytoin, theophylline are the usual list |

**Vignette.** A graph of two agonists shows drug A reaching a lower maximum than drug B, at a lower concentration.
**Reasoning.** A is more potent (leftward) and less efficacious (shorter). The trap is calling A “stronger” because the dose is smaller. Stronger, if the word means anything here, is the ceiling. The diagnosis of the graph is a partial agonist compared with a full agonist, or simply a less efficacious drug. Do not add a disease.

### Kinetics that change the dose, not the diagnosis

| Idea | The sentence | What changes it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bioavailability | The fraction that reaches the blood unchanged | First-pass liver metabolism. A drug with huge first pass needs another route or a huge oral dose |
| Volume of distribution | How far the drug leaves the blood | A huge volume means tissue binding. Dialysis will not see it. Loading dose follows volume |
| Clearance | How fast the body removes it | Kidney, liver, or both. Maintenance dose follows clearance |
| Half-life | Time for the amount to fall by half, when elimination is first-order | About four half-lives to steady state, and four to wash out |
| First-order | A constant fraction per hour | Most drugs. Half-life is stable |
| Zero-order | A constant amount per hour | Phenytoin, ethanol, and high-dose aspirin. The half-life is not a comfort |
| Steady state | In equals out | A drip or a regular dose. A loading dose skips the wait |
| Ion trapping | A weak base concentrates in acid, a weak acid in base | Urine pH can trap a poison. It does not replace the antidote |

Loading dose is the bucket. Maintenance dose is the leak. Renal failure shrinks clearance, so the maintenance dose falls or the interval grows. The loading dose mostly does not, unless the volume also changed.

**Vignette.** A patient with a low creatinine clearance is started on a renally cleared drug at the usual dose and the usual interval. On day four the level is toxic. The first dose caused no problem.
**Reasoning.** The bucket was the right size. The leak was slower, so the repeated maintenance doses stacked. The diagnosis is accumulation from unchanged maintenance dosing. The trap is “the loading dose was too big,” which would have shown up on day one.

**Hook.** Load for volume. Maintain for clearance. Zero-order drugs do not have a friendly half-life.

### The autonomic story as wet, dry, fast, and slow

| Picture | Too much of | The antidote logic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wet, crampy, bradycardic, wheezing, small pupil | Muscarinic | Atropine, a competitive block |
| The same, plus fasciculations and weakness | Acetylcholinesterase is blocked, so acetylcholine is high at muscarinic and nicotinic sites | Atropine for the wet part. Pralidoxime for the enzyme, before organophosphate aging |
| Dry, hot, delirious, big pupil, urinary retention | Muscarinic block | The drug is often not labeled “atropine.” Antihistamines, tricyclics, and some antipsychotics do this as a second life. Physostigmine is a specialist antidote, not a reflex |
| Fast heart, high sugar, wide-open bronchi, low potassium | Beta agonism | A beta blocker if the heart is the problem and the lungs allow it |
| Cold, pale, high pressure, reflex slow heart | Alpha agonism | The reflex bradycardia means the baroreceptor is working. Do not call it a primary heart block |
| Paralyzed, no fasciculation, fade on train-of-four | Nondepolarizing neuromuscular block | Raising acetylcholine competes it off |
| Fasciculation, then paralysis, potassium leaves the muscle | Depolarizing block (succinylcholine) | It is not reversed the same way. A low cholinesterase makes it last. A high potassium is the danger in a vulnerable muscle |

**Vignette.** A farmer is wet with secretions, wheezing, bradycardic, and twitching. The pupils are small.
**Reasoning.** Acetylcholine is high everywhere a cholinergic receptor sits. The twitching means the neuromuscular junction is included, so this is not a pure muscarinic drug. The diagnosis is cholinesterase inhibition, classically an organophosphate. The trap is giving only atropine and thinking the weakness is anxiety. The muscle needs the enzyme regenerated if the poison is an organophosphate and it has not aged.

**Vignette.** An older adult took a bedtime allergy pill and now cannot urinate, is hot and confused, and has large pupils.
**Reasoning.** Dry, hot, mad, and a big pupil is muscarinic blockade. Many “non-autonomic” drugs block that receptor. The diagnosis is anticholinergic toxicity. The trap is sympathomimetic toxicity, which is also hot and fast but usually sweaty, not dry.

**Pair.** Cholinergic crisis is wet and weak. Anticholinergic toxicity is dry and mad. Myasthenic weakness is fatigable and dry only if a separate drug made it so.

### Toxicities that are the mechanism continuing

| Drug or class | The harm | Why it is not random |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acetaminophen | Centrilobular liver necrosis | A toxic metabolite normally eaten by glutathione. The center of the lobule sees it and has less oxygen |
| Aspirin, high dose | Anion-gap acidosis and a respiratory alkalosis early | Uncoupled oxidative phosphorylation and a stimulated respiratory center. Tinnitus is the early clue |
| Opioid | Slow breath, small pupil | The receptor in the brainstem. Naloxone is competitive and may wear off first |
| Benzodiazepine | Sedation, much less respiratory depression alone than with an opioid | A positive allosteric modulator of GABA. Flumazenil exists and can precipitate withdrawal in a dependent person |
| Tricyclic | Wide QRS, seizures, anticholinergic signs | Sodium-channel block is the heart. Bicarbonate is the teaching antidote for the wide QRS |
| Beta blocker | Slow heart, low sugar, wheeze if nonselective | Glucagon is the bypass that still raises cardiac cAMP. Confirm the current toxicology approach before you treat a person |
| Theophylline or a sympathomimetic | Tachyarrhythmia, low potassium, seizures | Beta-2 shifts potassium in. The potassium did not leave the body |
| Methotrexate | Marrow, mucosa, liver, and a folate trap | Leucovorin bypasses the blocked enzyme. It is not folic acid in the ordinary sense |
| Bleomycin | Lung fibrosis | The drug and oxygen make radicals the lung does not quench well |
| Doxorubicin | Dilated cardiomyopathy | Cumulative dose and free radicals in myocytes |
| Cyclophosphamide | Hemorrhagic cystitis | A metabolite. Mesna binds it in the urine. The teaching point is the bladder, not a random bleed |
| Vincristine | Neuropathy | Microtubules in long axons |
| Cisplatin | Kidney, ear, and a low magnesium | The metal. Hearing and magnesium are the clues |
| Amphotericin | Kidney and a low potassium and magnesium | It punches membranes, including the tubule |
| Aminoglycoside | Kidney and ear | It sits in the tubular cell and the hair cell |
| Isoniazid | Neuropathy, and hepatitis | It uses up vitamin B6. Give the vitamin. The liver injury is a separate reason to stop |
| Rifampin | Orange fluids and induced CYP enzymes | Other drugs fall in level. The color is harmless and alarming |
| Ethambutol | Color vision | The optic nerve. Ask about colors |
| Linezolid | Marrow suppression and serotonin syndrome if combined | It is also a weak MAO inhibitor. The food and drug interaction is the point |
| SSRI plus an MAO inhibitor or a triptan in the wrong combination | Clonus, fever, hyperreflexia | Serotonin syndrome. Rigid bradyreflexia is the neuroleptic malignant picture instead |
| Heparin | Bleeding, and sometimes a falling platelet count with clots | HIT is an antibody against platelet factor 4. The platelets activate. Stopping heparin is the move. Warfarin alone at the start can make the clot worse |
| Warfarin | Bleeding, and skin necrosis in protein C deficiency at the start | Protein C falls first because it is short-lived. The early state can be prothrombotic |
| Succinylcholine | A high potassium, and a long block if cholinesterase is low | Burns, denervation, and some muscular diseases up-regulate receptors |

**Vignette.** A patient on several psychiatric medicines develops clonus, a fever, and hyperreflexia after a new pain medicine. The muscles are not rigid in the lead-pipe sense, and reflexes are brisk.
**Reasoning.** Extra serotonin with a hyperreflexic, clonus-filled exam is serotonin syndrome. Lead-pipe rigidity and slow reflexes would be neuroleptic malignant syndrome. The diagnosis is serotonin syndrome. The trap is giving more of a serotonergic antiemetic because the person is agitated.

**NBME trap.** They describe the organ and want the drug class, or the drug and want the organ. A list of adverse effects without the mechanism will not transfer to a new stem.

### Autonomic drugs the stems actually use

| Goal in the stem | The class | Why a neighbor is wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raise blood pressure and cause reflex bradycardia | Alpha-1 agonist | A beta agonist would raise the rate |
| Open bronchi and shift potassium in | Beta-2 agonist | An anticholinergic opens bronchi without the potassium shift |
| Slow the AV node without a long-acting beta block | Adenosine, briefly | Digoxin is slow and toxic in its own way |
| Test for myasthenia, briefly | A short-acting cholinesterase inhibitor | A depolarizing paralytic would weaken everyone |
| Reverse a nondepolarizing block | A cholinesterase inhibitor, often with an antimuscarinic so the gut and heart do not pay | It will not reliably reverse succinylcholine |
| Dilate a pupil for an exam and paralyze accommodation | Muscarinic blocker | An alpha agonist dilates with less cycloplegia |
| Treat glaucoma by opening the trabecular meshwork with a small pupil | Muscarinic agonist | A dilating drop would worsen a closed angle |
| Block a motion-sickness signal | Antimuscarinic, often a patch | A beta blocker does not do this |

**Vignette.** Eye pressure is high, the pupil is mid-dilated, and the eye is hard and painful. Someone considers a dilating drop.
**Reasoning.** The angle is narrow and the iris is blocking the drain. Dilation crowds the angle further. A muscarinic agonist constricts the pupil and pulls the iris out of the angle. The diagnosis is acute angle closure. The trap is a routine dilating exam. This is a Step 1 mechanism with a Step 2 emergency attached. Do not dilate it.

### CYP and the few interactions worth owning

Inducers speed the metabolism of other drugs: rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, chronic alcohol, and St. John’s wort are the usual set. Inhibitors slow it: azoles, macrolides that are not azithromycin, ritonavir, ciprofloxacin, and acute alcohol are the usual set. The victim drugs are the ones with a narrow index: warfarin, some statins, theophylline, and oral contraceptives in the classic teaching. Confirm a specific pair before you change a real regimen. The exam wants the direction: inducer, level falls; inhibitor, level rises.

**Vignette.** A stable warfarin patient’s INR climbs a week after an antifungal is added. The warfarin dose did not change.
**Reasoning.** The antifungal slowed the clearance of warfarin. The diagnosis is CYP inhibition. The trap is “the patient ate more salad,” which would lower the INR by adding vitamin K, the opposite direction.

### What not to memorize here

A poster of every chemo regimen, every antibiotic dose, and every CYP pair is filler. Own the messenger, the graph, the wet-versus-dry picture, and the toxicity that is the mechanism. The feed cards that pair one antidote with one poison are the hook after this table, not a substitute for it.

## NBME worked set

Graphs, messengers, and toxidromes. The drug name is the last step.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** Two dose-response curves reach the same maximum. Curve A is left of curve B.
**Reasoning.** Same efficacy, A is more potent. The trap is calling A stronger. Strength, if it means anything here, is the ceiling.

**Vignette 2.** A curve’s maximum falls after a new drug is added. Extra agonist does not restore the old height.
**Reasoning.** Noncompetitive or irreversible block, or loss of receptors. A competitive antagonist would have shifted the curve and kept the ceiling. The trap is more agonist as the fix.

**Vignette 3.** A patient in renal failure is toxic on day four of a renally cleared drug. The first dose was fine.
**Reasoning.** Maintenance stacked because clearance fell. Loading dose was not the error. The trap is blaming the first dose.

**Vignette 4.** A farmer is wet, wheezing, bradycardic, and fasciculating. Pupils are small.
**Reasoning.** Cholinesterase inhibition. Fasciculations mean the neuromuscular junction is involved, so this is not a pure muscarinic drug. Atropine alone misses the muscle. The trap is treating it as anxiety.

**Vignette 5.** An older adult who took a bedtime allergy pill is hot, dry, confused, and unable to urinate. Pupils are large.
**Reasoning.** Muscarinic blockade from a drug that is not labeled atropine. Sympathomimetic toxicity would sweat. The trap is the word allergy.

**Vignette 6.** A graph shows drug A never reaching drug B’s maximum, even at high concentration, and A sits to the left.
**Reasoning.** A is a partial agonist or simply less efficacious, and more potent. It can occupy receptors and block B. The trap is equating a small dose with a bigger effect.

**Vignette 7.** Warfarin’s INR rises after an azole antifungal is added. The warfarin dose did not change.
**Reasoning.** The antifungal inhibited clearance. More salad would have lowered the INR. The trap is the direction.

**Vignette 8.** A wide QRS after a tricyclic overdose. The patient is also dry and delirious.
**Reasoning.** Sodium-channel block plus muscarinic block. Bicarbonate is the teaching move for the QRS. The trap is treating only the delirium.

**Vignette 9.** Succinylcholine is given to a patient with a burn several days old. The potassium was already high.
**Reasoning.** Upregulated receptors dump potassium. A nondepolarizing blocker does not do this the same way. The trap is using the depolarizing drug because it is faster.

**Vignette 10.** A patient on a beta blocker does not mount a tachycardia during hypoglycemia. Sweat may still happen.
**Reasoning.** The warning signs that use beta receptors are gone. Sweat is cholinergic. The trap is saying the glucose is fine because the heart rate is calm.

## Stem craft

Graphs and toxidromes the stem describes without the drug class.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** A patient seizes and the QRS is wide after an overdose of an old antidepressant. The mouth is dry.
**Reasoning.** Sodium-channel block widens the QRS. Muscarinic block dries the mouth. That combination is a tricyclic. A pure benzodiazepine overdose sedates without the wide QRS. Bicarbonate narrows the QRS in the teaching answer.

**Vignette 2.** Orange urine and a falling INR a week after a new tuberculosis drug.
**Reasoning.** Rifampin induced the clearance of warfarin. An inhibitor would have raised the INR. The color is the clue and is harmless. The trap is stopping the tuberculosis drug for the color.

**Vignette 3.** Hearing loss and a high creatinine after a drug given for a gram-negative bacteremia.
**Reasoning.** An aminoglycoside sat in the hair cell and the tubular cell. The trap is a beta-lactam, which does not own this pair of toxicities.

**Vignette 4.** A long QT and a pause, then a polymorphic tachycardia, in a patient on a class III drug with a low magnesium.
**Reasoning.** Potassium-channel block lengthened repolarization. Torsades. A class I drug would have widened the QRS instead. The trap is one word, antiarrhythmic.

**Vignette 5.** Fasciculations, then paralysis, and a potassium that jumps, in a patient with a muscular dystrophy.
**Reasoning.** Succinylcholine and upregulated receptors. A nondepolarizing blocker does not dump potassium this way. The trap is using the fastest paralytic in a vulnerable muscle.

## Filing the stem

A last set of stems that are easy to misfile.

The vignette withholds the name. The reasoning earns it.

**Vignette 1.** A competitive antagonist is added. The agonist curve shifts right. The same maximum is still reachable.
**Reasoning.** Enough agonist still wins. If the ceiling had fallen, the block would be noncompetitive or the tissue would have run out of spare receptors. The trap is raising the agonist dose when the ceiling is already down and expecting the old effect.

**Vignette 2.** Ethanol, phenytoin, and high-dose aspirin do not have a stable half-life.
**Reasoning.** Zero-order elimination removes a constant amount per hour. First-order removes a constant fraction. A level that was safe can become toxic with a small extra dose. The trap is using a half-life you memorized from the low-dose range.

**Vignette 3.** A loading dose is calculated from volume. The kidneys then fail. The next doses are not changed.
**Reasoning.** Maintenance follows clearance. The bucket was the right size. The leak slowed. Toxicity on day three or four is a maintenance error. The trap is cutting the loading dose in half because the creatinine is high.

**Vignette 4.** Organophosphate poisoning. Atropine dries the secretions. The weakness remains.
**Reasoning.** Atropine does not restore the acetylcholinesterase at the neuromuscular junction. Pralidoxime does, before aging. The trap is stopping at a dry mouth.

## Checklist

- [ ] Messenger before drug name.
- [ ] Potency is left-right. Efficacy is the ceiling.
- [ ] Renal failure changes maintenance, not the concept of loading.
- [ ] A “side effect” is the receptor in another organ until proven otherwise.
- [ ] Zero-order drugs do not have a comforting constant half-life.

## Common traps

- Calling a partial agonist “weaker.” It can block a full agonist and still have its own effect.
- Reversing succinylcholine with more acetylcholine as if it were rocuronium.
- Treating every anticholinergic as atropine. Some drugs (antihistamines, tricyclics, antipsychotics) block muscarinic receptors as a second life.
- Memorizing a CYP list with no clinical pair. The pairs that matter are the inducers and inhibitors that move a narrow-index drug.

## Practice this in the Qbank

Work autonomic and kinetics items until a graph is a sentence: “ceiling fell, so noncompetitive” or “shifted right, so competitive.” Organ-system drug items come later, inside those chapters. Feed cards that list an antidote are useful only after you can say which receptor was overstimulated.
