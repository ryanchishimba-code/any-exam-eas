## Renal in depth

KDIGO 2024 defines acute kidney injury as any one of these: creatinine up by at least 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, creatinine at least 1.5 times baseline within 7 days, or urine output under 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. The same KDIGO 2024 chronic kidney disease guideline defines CKD as a GFR under 60 mL/min/1.73 m², or markers of kidney damage, present for more than 3 months. Markers include albuminuria, abnormal sediment, or structural disease. ([KDIGO 2024 CKD guideline](https://kdigo.org/guidelines/ckd-evaluation-and-management/))

**Staging you must be able to read**

| GFR category | mL/min/1.73 m² | What the stem is saying |
| --- | --- | --- |
| G1 | ≥90 | Damage marker, not a low GFR |
| G2 | 60–89 | Mildly low, still needs the marker or the trend |
| G3a | 45–59 | CKD if it lasts more than 3 months |
| G3b | 30–44 | Dose-adjust many drugs |
| G4 | 15–29 | Prepare for kidney replacement |
| G5 | <15 | Kidney failure |

Albuminuria uses the ACR, not a dipstick alone. A1 is under 30 mg/g, A2 is 30–299 mg/g, and A3 is 300 mg/g or more. An ACR in the A2 or A3 range is kidney damage even when the GFR is still above 60.

**What slows progression**

KDIGO 2024 recommends an SGLT2 inhibitor for adults with CKD and type 2 diabetes, and for adults with CKD and albuminuria, down to the eGFR on the drug label (often about 20 mL/min for dapagliflozin and empagliflozin). Continue the SGLT2 inhibitor if the GFR later falls below the start threshold, unless it is not tolerated. An ACE inhibitor or an ARB is first-line blood-pressure treatment when albuminuria is present. Do not combine an ACE inhibitor with an ARB. A statin is indicated for adults 50 and older with CKD not on dialysis (KDIGO lipid guidance, still the KDIGO lipid recommendation). The 2025 AHA/ACC blood-pressure guideline keeps a general goal under 130/80 mm Hg. KDIGO discusses a systolic target under 120 mm Hg when the patient tolerates it. On a test, read which source the stem cites. Do not stack an ACE inhibitor, an ARB, and an NSAID in a volume-depleted patient: the NSAID constricts the afferent arteriole and the ACE inhibitor or ARB dilates the efferent arteriole, so GFR falls.

**How to sort a rising creatinine**

| Pattern | Supports | Next move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| History of poor intake, urine sodium low, bland sediment, creatinine rises then falls with fluids | Prerenal | Volume, stop NSAIDs, hold the ACE inhibitor or ARB during the illness |
| Muddy brown casts, urine sodium high, FeNa over 2% | Acute tubular necrosis | Supportive care. Find the cause (ischemia, contrast, myoglobin, aminoglycoside) |
| White cells, white-cell casts, eosinophils, a new drug (a beta-lactam, PPI, or NSAID) | Acute interstitial nephritis | Stop the drug. Steroids are a nephrology decision when kidney function keeps falling |
| Red cells, red-cell casts, hypertension | Nephritic syndrome | Urgent urine sediment and nephrology. Do not send the patient home on an antibiotic for a "UTI" |
| Protein over 3.5 g/day, low albumin, edema, bland or fatty casts | Nephrotic syndrome | Quantify protein, check glucose and complement, refer. Minimal change, FSGS, membranous, amyloid, and diabetes are the classic list |

FeNa under 1% suggests prerenal physiology and FeNa over 2% suggests ATN, but a loop diuretic makes FeNa unreliable. FeUrea under 35% still supports prerenal physiology in a patient on a diuretic. A creatinine that rises up to about 30% after an ACE inhibitor or ARB is started can be hemodynamic. Recheck. A larger rise, or a rise with potassium trouble, means stop the drug and look again.

**Potassium**

ECG changes (peaked T waves, a flattened P, a wide QRS) mean the membrane is unstable. Give intravenous calcium first. It does not lower the potassium. Then shift potassium into cells with insulin plus glucose, and with nebulized albuterol if there is no contraindication. Remove potassium with a gastrointestinal binder (patiromer or sodium zirconium cyclosilicate; sodium polystyrene sulfonate is the older option and is slower) or with dialysis when there is kidney failure, a crush injury, or an ECG that does not settle. Stop the ACE inhibitor, ARB, potassium-sparing diuretic, NSAID, and trimethoprim when they are the cause. Succinylcholine is a bad choice when the potassium is already high.

**Sodium**

Severe symptoms (seizure, deep coma) from low sodium get hypertonic (3%) saline, typically 100 mL over 10 minutes, which may be repeated. The limit that protects against osmotic demyelination is generally no more than 8 mEq/L in 24 hours when the risk is high (alcohol use, malnutrition, liver disease, very low sodium). Chronic hyponatremia that is mild and asymptomatic is not a bolus of saline. Check the serum osmolality, the urine osmolality, and the volume status before you name SIADH. SIADH is euvolemic, with urine that is not dilute and urine sodium that is not low. Hypertonic hyponatremia from very high glucose is translocational: correct the sodium for the glucose before you call it SIADH. High sodium with dilute urine is diabetes insipidus until proven otherwise. Do not start a water-deprivation test if the sodium is already high and the urine is already dilute.

**Stones and obstruction**

Sudden colicky flank pain to the groin with blood in the urine is a ureteral stone until imaging says otherwise. Noncontrast CT is the most sensitive test in adults who are not pregnant. Ultrasound is the first test in pregnancy. Stones 5 mm or smaller often pass. The AUA allows medical expulsive therapy with tamsulosin for a distal ureteral stone when pain is controlled and there is no infection and no solitary kidney. Fever plus an obstructing stone is a closed-space infection: urgent decompression, not a bigger pain dose. A palpable bladder, a high potassium, and a rising creatinine can be obstruction from retention. Place a catheter before you chase intrinsic renal disease.

**Dialysis indications you should not miss**

Acidosis that you cannot correct, electrolyte danger (especially potassium) that treatment has not fixed, intoxications that dialysis removes (a salicylate, lithium, methanol, ethylene glycol), volume overload that diuretics have not moved, and uremic pericarditis, encephalopathy, or bleeding. The mnemonic AEIOU is a list of reasons, not a creatinine number. There is no single creatinine that "means dialysis."

**Contrast and drugs**

KDIGO supports isotonic saline volume expansion before iodinated contrast when the GFR is low. Metformin is held around contrast when the GFR is reduced, because the risk is lactic acidosis if the GFR then falls, not a direct toxic effect of metformin on the tubule. Aminoglycosides, vancomycin in excess, amphotericin, and myoglobin injure tubules. Acyclovir and methotrexate can crystallize if the patient is dry. Look at the drug list before you call every rise in creatinine "dehydration."

> **Important to remember**
> - Calcium first when the ECG has changed. Insulin lowers the potassium. Calcium does not.
> - Albuminuria changes the drug plan even when the GFR still looks acceptable.
> - Fever plus an obstructing stone is decompression, not another day of tamsulosin.
