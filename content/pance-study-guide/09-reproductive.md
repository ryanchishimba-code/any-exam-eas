## Reproductive in depth

**Prenatal care that the blueprint expects**

Confirm pregnancy with urine or serum hCG, then date it. Transvaginal ultrasound shows a gestational sac when the hCG is in the discriminatory range used by that lab, often about 1,500 to 2,000 mIU/mL, and a yolk sac and embryo soon after. A positive hCG, an empty uterus, and pain is ectopic pregnancy until you have proved otherwise. Do not reassure the patient because the pain is mild.

Routine timing that ACOG uses: blood type and antibody screen, hemoglobin, rubella, hepatitis B surface antigen, HIV, syphilis, and urine culture in the first trimester. Cell-free DNA and nuchal translucency are offered, not forced. Anatomy ultrasound is around 18 to 22 weeks. Gestational diabetes screening is at 24 to 28 weeks (a 50 g one-hour screen, then a 100 g three-hour test if it is positive, or a 75 g one-step test). RhD-negative patients who are unsensitized receive anti-D immunoglobulin at about 28 weeks and after bleeding or delivery of an Rh-positive infant. Group B streptococcus culture is at 36 to 37 weeks (ACOG). Intrapartum penicillin is the treatment when the culture is positive, when the patient had a prior infant with invasive group B strep disease, or when status is unknown and there is fever, a long rupture, or prematurity. Tdap is given at 27 to 36 weeks of every pregnancy (ACIP), so the infant gets antibodies. Maternal RSV vaccine is offered in the seasonal window late in pregnancy (ACIP).

**Blood pressure in pregnancy**

ACOG Practice Bulletin 222 (2020) defines preeclampsia as new hypertension at or after 20 weeks plus proteinuria, or new hypertension plus a severe feature (platelets under 100,000, creatinine up, liver enzymes twice normal, pulmonary edema, a new cerebral or visual symptom, or blood pressure at or above 160/110). Eclampsia is a seizure. HELLP is hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets, and it can occur without much hypertension. Severe blood pressure is treated with intravenous labetalol, intravenous hydralazine, or immediate-release oral nifedipine. Magnesium sulfate is for seizure prevention and for treatment of eclampsia. It is not a blood-pressure drug. The antidote for magnesium toxicity (loss of reflexes, respiratory depression) is calcium gluconate. Delivery is the cure. Expectant care is considered for some patients with preeclampsia without severe features before 37 weeks, in a setting that can watch them. ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated in pregnancy. Low-dose aspirin, started between 12 and 28 weeks (ideally before 16 weeks), is preeclampsia prevention for patients with a high-risk factor such as prior preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, chronic hypertension, diabetes, or kidney disease (USPSTF 2021 aspirin recommendation).

**Bleeding**

First-trimester bleeding with a closed cervix and a continuing pregnancy is threatened abortion. An open cervix is inevitable. Incomplete abortion has passed some tissue. Missed abortion is a dead embryo that has not passed. Unstable bleeding goes to the operating room or to medical evacuation. Stable patients may choose expectant care, misoprostol, or uterine aspiration (ACOG).

Ectopic pregnancy: methotrexate is for a hemodynamically stable patient, an unruptured mass, no fetal cardiac activity, an hCG below the cutoff your protocol uses (often under 5,000), and a patient who can return for serial hCG. Surgery is for rupture, instability, or a patient who cannot be followed. A heterotopic pregnancy is rare and is more likely after assisted reproduction. Do not give methotrexate if the intrauterine pregnancy is wanted and a second pregnancy is possible.

Third-trimester bleeding: placenta previa is painless, bright red, and often after 20 weeks with a low placenta on ultrasound. Do not perform a digital vaginal exam. Abruption is painful, the uterus is firm, and bleeding may be hidden. Fetal distress is common. Vasa previa bleeds when the membranes rupture and the blood is fetal, so the fetal heart rate falls. That is an emergency cesarean.

Postpartum hemorrhage is most often uterine atony: a boggy uterus and heavy bleeding. Massage the fundus and give oxytocin. Additional uterotonics (methylergonovine if blood pressure allows, carboprost if the patient does not have asthma, misoprostol) and tranexamic acid are next. Lacerations bleed with a firm uterus. Retained placenta and coagulopathy are the other tones of the "4 Ts" (tone, trauma, tissue, thrombin). ACOG.

**Screening**

USPSTF 2024: mammography every 2 years from age 40 through 74. A palpable mass gets diagnostic imaging, not a screening mammogram. Under 30, ultrasound is often the first image. At 30 and older, diagnostic mammogram, often with ultrasound. The USPSTF 2018 cervical cancer recommendation remains the posted recommendation: cytology alone every 3 years from 21 through 29. From 30 through 65, cytology every 3 years, high-risk HPV alone every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years. Stop after 65 when the recent screens were adequate and negative. Stop after hysterectomy with removal of the cervix for a benign reason. Do not start before 21. Primary HPV screening schedules continue to be updated by ACS and by draft USPSTF language. On this exam, use the posted USPSTF intervals unless the stem names another source.

**Contraception and infection**

The CDC U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria (2024) is the table for "can this patient use this method?" Combined hormonal contraception is category 4 (do not use) with migraine with aura, age 35 or older and smoking 15 or more cigarettes a day, a history of thrombosis, and severe hypertension, among other conditions. The implant and the copper and levonorgestrel IUDs are category 1 or 2 for most of those patients. Emergency contraception: a levonorgestrel pill works best within 72 hours and loses effect with higher body weight. Ulipristal works through 120 hours. A copper IUD is the most effective emergency method through 5 days.

Pelvic inflammatory disease is uterine, adnexal, or cervical-motion tenderness in a sexually active patient when no other cause is more likely (CDC STI treatment guidelines). Treat gonorrhea and chlamydia. Admit for pregnancy, a tubo-ovarian abscess, inability to tolerate oral drugs, or a patient who looks ill. Uncomplicated gonorrhea is ceftriaxone 500 mg intramuscularly once (1 g if the patient weighs 150 kg or more). Chlamydia is doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days. In pregnancy, use azithromycin for chlamydia, not doxycycline.

Vaginal discharge: bacterial vaginosis has a pH over 4.5, clue cells, and a positive whiff test. Metronidazole treats it. Trichomonas is a motile trichomonad and also gets metronidazole, and the partner is treated. Candida has a normal pH and pseudohyphae and gets a topical azole or fluconazole. Do not treat "discharge" with one drug that covers none of the three.

**Gynecologic problems outside pregnancy**

PCOS, using the 2023 international evidence-based guideline: two of three Rotterdam features (oligo-ovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, polycystic ovaries on ultrasound) after you have excluded other causes such as thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, and nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia. Letrozole is first-line ovulation induction when pregnancy is the goal. Combined hormonal contraception treats cycle control and hirsutism when pregnancy is not the goal. Screen for diabetes and for sleep apnea clues. Endometrial protection matters because unopposed estrogen raises cancer risk.

Abnormal uterine bleeding uses the PALM-COEIN list. PALM is structural (polyp, adenomyosis, leiomyoma, malignancy or hyperplasia). COEIN is nonstructural (coagulopathy, ovulatory dysfunction, endometrial, iatrogenic, not yet classified). The first test in reproductive years is a pregnancy test. Postmenopausal bleeding is cancer until a biopsy or a sufficiently thin endometrial echo says otherwise. Heavy bleeding with a boggy uterus after delivery was covered above. A 47-year-old with heavy irregular bleeding needs a pregnancy test, a blood count, and an endometrial sample when the history suggests hyperplasia (obesity, unopposed estrogen, failed medical therapy, or persistent bleeding).

Breastfeeding contraindications to know: current illicit drug use that is not in a treatment plan the infant can be protected from, and HIV in settings where replacement feeding is safe (CDC, United States). Mastitis is a tender wedge of breast with fever. Keep feeding or pumping. Antibiotics cover staph when an abscess is not present. An abscess is drained.

> **Important to remember**
> - Magnesium for the seizure. Labetalol, hydralazine, or nifedipine for the blood pressure.
> - No digital exam when previa is possible.
> - Mammogram every 2 years from 40 through 74 (USPSTF 2024). Cervical intervals follow the posted USPSTF recommendation.
