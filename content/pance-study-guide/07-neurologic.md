## Neurologic in depth

Neurologic disease is 7% of the exam. Time windows and "which weakness is central" are the usual decisions.

**Stroke**

The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline for early management of acute ischemic stroke: check glucose and obtain a noncontrast CT or MRI to exclude hemorrhage before any reperfusion drug. For a disabling deficit, tenecteplase (0.25 mg/kg, maximum 25 mg) or alteplase (0.9 mg/kg, maximum 90 mg) is given within 4.5 hours of last known well when criteria are met. Blood pressure must be under 185/110 mm Hg before the drug and under 180/105 mm Hg for the next 24 hours. A non-disabling deficit is treated with dual antiplatelet therapy, not a fibrinolytic. Large-vessel occlusion is evaluated for thrombectomy. An extended window (4.5 to 9 hours, or wake-up stroke) uses advanced imaging that shows salvageable tissue. Do not lower blood pressure aggressively in a patient who is not receiving a fibrinolytic or thrombectomy. Permissive hypertension is intentional.

A minor stroke or high-risk TIA (NIHSS 3 or lower, or an ABCD2 pattern the stem calls high risk) gets short-term dual antiplatelet therapy, then a single agent. A thunderclap headache is a subarachnoid hemorrhage until imaging says otherwise. Noncontrast CT first. If CT is negative and the headache began more than 6 hours ago, or there is a deficit, perform a lumbar puncture. The 2023 AHA/ASA guideline is the source for that sequence. Nimodipine is the drug that prevents delayed ischemia after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. It is not a treatment for the bleed itself.

**Seizure, headache, and neuromuscular disease**

A seizure that continues for 5 minutes is status epilepticus. Benzodiazepine first (intravenous lorazepam, or intramuscular midazolam if there is no intravenous access), then a second drug (fosphenytoin, levetiracetam, or valproate). Do not stack benzodiazepines while the clock runs. A brief generalized seizure during a fever in a toddler, with a normal exam afterward, is a simple febrile seizure. It is not epilepsy treatment. An infant with stiff, scissoring legs needs a developmental evaluation for cerebral palsy, not an antiepileptic drug started from the waiting room.

Migraine acute treatment is an NSAID or a triptan when there is no vascular contraindication. A prior myocardial infarction removes the triptan. Cluster headache is oxygen and a subcutaneous triptan. Image a headache when it is thunderclap, when it is new after age 50, when there is a neurologic deficit, papilledema, cancer, or immunosuppression, or when the pattern has changed. Morning headache with vomiting and papilledema is raised intracranial pressure. Acetazolamide is used for idiopathic intracranial hypertension after imaging and a lumbar puncture have been planned. Do not lumbar-puncture a patient with a mass and a shifted midline.

Guillain-Barré syndrome is ascending weakness and lost reflexes after an infection, often Campylobacter. Treatment is intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange. Do not use a corticosteroid as the treatment. Monitor the vital capacity. Myasthenia gravis is fatigable weakness, ptosis, and diplopia. A cholinergic crisis and a myasthenic crisis are separated by the story (too much drug versus not enough) and by support of the airway. Pyridostigmine is the symptom drug. A thymoma is sought. Multiple sclerosis is a young adult with neurologic episodes separated in time and space, often starting with optic neuritis. Acute relapse is a high-dose corticosteroid. Disease-modifying therapy is started after the diagnosis. It is not the treatment of the acute flare.

**Vertigo, trauma, and neuropathy**

Vertigo that starts on rolling over in bed, lasts under a minute, and has no other neurologic sign is benign paroxysmal positional vertigo. The Dix-Hallpike maneuver and a canalith repositioning maneuver are the treatment. The BPPV clinical practice guideline (2017) remains the cited standard. Continuous vertigo with a skew deviation, a direction-changing nystagmus, or a new severe headache is central until imaging excludes a stroke. A lucid interval after head trauma is an epidural hematoma from the middle meningeal artery. That patient goes to neurosurgery. A subdural hematoma is slower and is more common in older adults and in patients on anticoagulation. Facial weakness that includes the forehead is peripheral (Bell palsy) when the rest of the exam is normal. Forehead sparing is a central lesion. Steroids are started early for Bell palsy. Eye care prevents corneal injury.

## Transient ischemia, tremor, and Parkinson disease

A transient ischemic attack is a focal deficit that resolves. The short-term risk is a completed stroke. After a minor stroke or a high-risk TIA, and after imaging has excluded a bleed, short-term dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus clopidogrel) is used for about 21 days, then a single agent (AHA/ASA 2021 secondary-prevention guidance). Do not continue two antiplatelet drugs for a year after a TIA and call it the same plan as a coronary stent. Do not give a fibrinolytic for a deficit that has already resolved.

Parkinson disease is rest tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia. Levodopa is the most effective drug for motor symptoms. A dopamine agonist is sometimes chosen in a younger patient to delay levodopa, at the cost of impulse-control symptoms. Do not use an antipsychotic that blocks dopamine, such as haloperidol, for the hallucinations of Parkinson disease. Quetiapine or clozapine is the usual discussion when a drug is required. Essential tremor is a bilateral action tremor. It improves with alcohol in the classic story and is treated with propranolol or primidone when a drug is needed. It is not a rest tremor.

A sudden severe headache that reaches maximum in seconds is a subarachnoid hemorrhage until a CT, and a lumbar puncture when the CT is negative and the suspicion remains, say otherwise. Do not reassure a thunderclap headache because the exam is normal in the first hour.

> **Important to remember**
> - Disabling deficit within 4.5 hours: tenecteplase or alteplase, after CT, with blood pressure under 185/110 (AHA/ASA 2026).
> - Non-disabling deficit: dual antiplatelet therapy, not a fibrinolytic.
> - Lost reflexes and ascending weakness: Guillain-Barré. Fatigable ptosis: myasthenia.
