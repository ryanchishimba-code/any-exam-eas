## USMLE Step 1

### usmle1-001 · Biochemistry

- Outline: General principles: biochemistry and molecular biology
- Front: After a carbohydrate meal, liver glycolysis speeds up. Which step is committed, and what slows it when energy is already high?
- Answer: Phosphofructokinase-1. ATP and citrate slow it.
- Clue: Fructose-6-phosphate becomes fructose-1,6-bisphosphate at this step.
- First-line: AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate speed the enzyme. ATP and citrate slow it.
- Trap: Naming hexokinase. It is regulated, but it is not the committed step.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: The content outline places pathway regulation in general principles. PFK-1 is the committed step of glycolysis.

### usmle1-002 · Biochemistry

- Outline: General principles: biochemistry and molecular biology
- Front: During a long fast the liver makes glucose. Which enzymes does glycolysis refuse to run in reverse?
- Answer: Pyruvate carboxylase, PEPCK, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, and glucose-6-phosphatase.
- Clue: These four steps are the irreversible ones. The rest of the pathway is shared.
- First-line: Biotin is required for pyruvate carboxylase. Glucagon raises fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase activity by lowering fructose-2,6-bisphosphate.
- Trap: Saying the whole pathway simply reverses when insulin falls.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Gluconeogenesis is a general-principles pathway. Only the irreversible enzymes are unique to it.

### usmle1-003 · Biochemistry

- Outline: General principles: biochemistry and molecular biology
- Front: A toddler has lactic acidosis and neurologic injury after carbohydrate loads. Pyruvate, lactate, and alanine are all high.
- Answer: Pyruvate dehydrogenase is blocked. Use ketogenic fuel, not more glucose.
- Clue: Pyruvate, lactate, and alanine rise together because they share one pool.
- First-line: Thiamine is a cofactor. A ketogenic diet bypasses the block.
- Trap: Giving more glucose, which only makes more pyruvate that cannot be oxidized.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: PDH links glycolysis to the citric acid cycle. Carbohydrate loading worsens the lactic acidosis.

### usmle1-004 · Biochemistry

- Outline: General principles: biochemistry and molecular biology
- Front: A poison makes the inner mitochondrial membrane leak protons. Oxygen use rises, ATP falls, and the patient is hot.
- Answer: This is an uncoupler. The chain runs, but the gradient makes heat.
- Clue: Oxygen consumption is high while ATP is low.
- First-line: Separate this from a chain blocker, which stops oxygen use and ATP together.
- Trap: Calling every mitochondrial poison a chain inhibitor.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Uncoupling is a core oxidative-phosphorylation concept. Thermogenin, high-dose aspirin, and dinitrophenol are the classic examples.

### usmle1-005 · Biochemistry

- Outline: General principles: biochemistry and molecular biology
- Front: A man develops dark urine two days after a sulfa drug. The smear shows bite cells and denatured hemoglobin precipitates.
- Answer: Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase cannot refill NADPH.
- Clue: The inheritance is X-linked. Oxidative stress denatures hemoglobin, and the spleen bites the precipitates out.
- First-line: Stop the oxidant. An enzyme assay during the hemolysis can look normal, because the oldest cells are already gone.
- Trap: Trusting a normal enzyme level drawn on the day of the hemolysis.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: The pentose phosphate pathway is the source of NADPH in red cells. Infection, sulfa drugs, primaquine, and fava beans are typical triggers.

### usmle1-006 · Biochemistry

- Outline: General principles: biochemistry and molecular biology
- Front: Bleeding gums, poor wound healing, and corkscrew hairs. The vessels are fragile and collagen will not cross-link.
- Answer: Vitamin C is missing. Prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases need it.
- Clue: Hydroxylation of proline and lysine lets collagen form stable triple helices.
- First-line: Replace vitamin C. The bleeding is from vessels, not from a missing clotting factor.
- Trap: Treating this as vitamin K deficiency because the patient is bleeding.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Ascorbate is required for collagen hydroxylation. Scurvy is the clinical picture of that missing step.

### usmle1-007 · Biochemistry

- Outline: General principles: biochemistry and molecular biology
- Front: A megaloblastic anemia comes with loss of vibration sense. Methylmalonic acid is high and homocysteine is high.
- Answer: This is cobalamin deficiency, not folate deficiency alone.
- Clue: Both deficiencies stall DNA synthesis. Only cobalamin handles methylmalonyl-CoA.
- First-line: Replace B12. Folate alone can improve the anemia and leave the cord injury going.
- Trap: Treating a high mean corpuscular volume with folate before the methylmalonic acid is known.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Methylmalonic acid separates B12 deficiency from folate deficiency. Subacute combined degeneration is the cord finding.

### usmle1-008 · Biochemistry

- Outline: General principles: biochemistry and molecular biology
- Front: A child has a musty body odor, fair skin, and developmental delay. Phenylalanine is high and tyrosine is low.
- Answer: Phenylalanine hydroxylase is deficient. Tyrosine is now essential.
- Clue: Phenylalanine cannot become tyrosine, so pigment and neurotransmitter precursors fall.
- First-line: Restrict phenylalanine and avoid aspartame. Tetrahydrobiopterin deficiency is the variant that also needs neurotransmitter precursors.
- Trap: Calling every fair-haired infant with delay a storage disease and missing the diet.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Phenylketonuria is a core amino-acid pathway. The diet is the treatment that prevents further injury.

### usmle1-009 · Genetics

- Outline: General principles: human development and genetics
- Front: An autosomal recessive disease affects 1 in 10,000 newborns. About how common are carriers?
- Answer: About 1 in 50. The carrier frequency is 2pq.
- Clue: Incidence is q squared, so q is 1 in 100. When p is near 1, 2pq is about 1 in 50.
- First-line: Use this for counseling in a large population that mates at random and is not under selection.
- Trap: Reporting the allele frequency as if it were the carrier frequency.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Hardy-Weinberg is the quantitative-methods tool the outline expects for carrier estimates.

### usmle1-010 · Genetics

- Outline: General principles: human development and genetics
- Front: A trait shows up in males across generations. Affected men have unaffected sons and carrier daughters.
- Answer: X-linked recessive. There is no male-to-male transmission.
- Clue: Daughters of affected men are obligate carriers.
- First-line: Draw the pedigree before choosing autosomal recessive, which can pass from father to son.
- Trap: Calling it autosomal dominant only because more than one generation is affected.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Transmission pattern is a genetics general principle. Absence of father-to-son transmission is the key.

### usmle1-011 · Genetics

- Outline: General principles: human development and genetics
- Front: A child is profoundly hypotonic, then develops insatiable hunger. The paternal copy of 15q is missing or silent.
- Answer: Prader-Willi syndrome. The maternal copy of that region is silent.
- Clue: The same band from the other parent causes ataxia, inappropriate laughter, and almost no speech.
- First-line: Name the parent of origin. The chromosome number alone does not separate the two syndromes.
- Trap: Swapping the syndromes because both involve chromosome 15.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Imprinting is a genetics principle. Prader-Willi needs the paternal contribution. Angelman needs maternal UBE3A.

### usmle1-012 · Genetics

- Outline: General principles: human development and genetics
- Front: A family has colon cancer and endometrial cancer at young ages. Tumor microsatellites are unstable lengths.
- Answer: Lynch syndrome. DNA mismatch repair has failed.
- Clue: Microsatellite instability is the footprint of mismatch-repair loss.
- First-line: This is not the pathway with hundreds of polyps. That pathway is APC.
- Trap: Calling every inherited colon cancer familial adenomatous polyposis.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Mismatch repair fixes replication errors. Lynch syndrome is the clinical consequence, with colon and endometrial cancer.

### usmle1-013 · Genetics

- Outline: General principles: human development and genetics
- Front: A child burns after minutes in the sun and develops skin cancers. Cells cannot clear ultraviolet dimers.
- Answer: Nucleotide excision repair has failed.
- Clue: Thymine dimers are the lesion. The pathway cuts out a short damaged oligonucleotide.
- First-line: Strict sun avoidance follows from the mechanism.
- Trap: Confusing this with mismatch repair, which fixes copy errors rather than ultraviolet dimers.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Xeroderma pigmentosum is the disease of failed nucleotide excision repair.

### usmle1-014 · Pathology

- Outline: General principles: biology of tissue response to disease
- Front: Heart muscle and kidney after a blocked artery show preserved architecture with ghost cells. Brain after ischemia turns to liquid.
- Answer: Coagulative necrosis in solid organs. Liquefactive necrosis in the brain.
- Clue: Enzymes from the tissue itself digest the brain. Ischemia in heart and kidney denatures those enzymes.
- First-line: Name the pattern from the organ. Do not treat every infarct as the same histology.
- Trap: Calling brain infarcts coagulative because the cause was ischemia.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Necrosis patterns are tissue response to injury. Organ of origin predicts the pattern.

### usmle1-015 · Pathology

- Outline: General principles: biology of tissue response to disease
- Front: A squamous lung cancer presents with severe hypercalcemia. Parathyroid hormone itself is low.
- Answer: The tumor is secreting parathyroid hormone-related peptide.
- Clue: The peptide mimics the hormone at bone and kidney, so calcium rises and the gland shuts down.
- First-line: Treat the calcium and the cancer. Do not operate on a normal parathyroid.
- Trap: Sending the patient for parathyroidectomy because the calcium is high.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Paraneoplastic PTHrP is a classic squamous-cell product. Suppressed native PTH separates it from primary hyperparathyroidism.

### usmle1-016 · Pharmacology

- Outline: General principles: pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic processes
- Front: A drug binds the same pocket as the substrate. More substrate restores the maximum velocity. Apparent Km rises.
- Answer: Competitive inhibition. Vmax is unchanged.
- Clue: On a double-reciprocal plot the lines meet on the y-axis, because 1 over Vmax is the same.
- First-line: A noncompetitive inhibitor lowers Vmax and does not yield to more substrate.
- Trap: Saying every inhibitor lowers the maximum velocity.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Competitive versus noncompetitive inhibition is a pharmacodynamics principle on the outline.

### usmle1-017 · Pharmacology

- Outline: General principles: pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic processes
- Front: A patient in status epilepticus needs a therapeutic level now. Maintenance dosing would take many half-lives.
- Answer: Give a loading dose. It depends on volume of distribution, not clearance.
- Clue: Loading dose is target concentration times volume, divided by bioavailability.
- First-line: Clearance sets the maintenance rate. Volume sets the size of the first dose.
- Trap: Raising the drip and waiting. Steady state still takes about four half-lives.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Loading versus maintenance dose is a core pharmacokinetic distinction.

### usmle1-018 · Pharmacology

- Outline: General principles: pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic processes
- Front: A drug's half-life grows longer as the level rises. A constant amount, not a constant fraction, leaves each hour.
- Answer: Elimination is zero order. The enzymes are saturated.
- Clue: Phenytoin, ethanol, and high-dose aspirin are the usual examples.
- First-line: Small dose changes can cause large level changes once the pathway is full.
- Trap: Using one fixed half-life to time the next blood level.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Zero-order kinetics is a pharmacokinetics principle. Saturated enzymes remove a fixed amount per unit time.

### usmle1-019 · Cell biology

- Outline: General principles: biology of cells
- Front: Damaged DNA should stop a cell before it copies that DNA. One protein halts G1. Another holds the transcription factor until it is phosphorylated.
- Answer: p53 halts G1. Hypophosphorylated Rb holds E2F.
- Clue: p53 raises p21, which keeps the kinase from phosphorylating Rb.
- First-line: Loss of either brake allows a damaged cell to divide. Rb loss is shared by retinoblastoma and osteosarcoma.
- Trap: Saying p53 directly phosphorylates Rb.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Cell-cycle checkpoints are general cell biology. p53 and Rb are the two brakes tested most often.

### usmle1-020 · Microbiology

- Outline: General principles: microbial biology
- Front: A secreted bacterial protein causes disease at very low dose, may be encoded by a phage, and is destroyed by heat. The wall lipid does the opposite.
- Answer: Exotoxin versus endotoxin.
- Clue: Endotoxin is lipopolysaccharide. It is heat stable and drives fever through interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor.
- First-line: Exotoxins are proteins with specific mechanisms. Endotoxin is the gram-negative wall.
- Trap: Calling every fever-producing bacterial product an exotoxin.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: The outline separates toxin biology from the organism list. Heat stability and chemistry tell them apart.

### usmle1-021 · Microbiology

- Outline: General principles: microbial biology
- Front: An enveloped virus uses one surface protein to find CD4 and a chemokine receptor, and a second protein to fuse.
- Answer: gp120 binds. gp41 fuses.
- Clue: The chemokine receptor is CCR5 early and CXCR4 later.
- First-line: A fusion inhibitor targets gp41. A receptor blocker targets CCR5. They are not the same drug.
- Trap: Swapping the two proteins, or treating every HIV drug as a reverse-transcriptase inhibitor.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: HIV entry is a microbial-biology and pharmacology junction. Binding and fusion are different steps.

### usmle1-022 · Immunology

- Outline: General principles: immune responses
- Front: A child has repeated infections with staphylococci and aspergillus, and a test of oxidative burst does not turn blue.
- Answer: NADPH oxidase is missing. Catalase-positive organisms survive inside the phagocyte.
- Clue: The burst is what kills organisms that bring their own catalase.
- First-line: Protect against those organisms. The nitroblue tetrazolium or dihydrorhodamine test shows the missing burst.
- Trap: Blaming antibodies. B cells are intact. The phagocyte cannot finish the kill.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Chronic granulomatous disease is failed NADPH oxidase. Catalase-positive organisms are the clinical clue.

### usmle1-023 · Immunology

- Outline: General principles: immune responses
- Front: Recurrent Neisseria infections start in adolescence. The membrane-attack complex cannot form.
- Answer: Terminal complement, C5 through C9, is missing.
- Clue: The same risk appears in a patient treated with a C5 inhibitor.
- First-line: Vaccinate against meningococcus and treat infections promptly. Earlier complement defects look different and often include immune-complex disease.
- Trap: Calling every complement problem a terminal-complex problem.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: The membrane-attack complex is the shared defense against Neisseria. That is the immune-response fact.

### usmle1-024 · Immunology

- Outline: Immune system
- Front: A boy has very high IgM, almost no IgG or IgA, and Pneumocystis. His T cells cannot signal B cells to switch class.
- Answer: CD40 ligand is missing.
- Clue: Without that signal, B cells stay on IgM.
- First-line: This is cell-mediated failure as well as antibody failure, which is why Pneumocystis appears.
- Trap: Calling it isolated agammaglobulinemia. Those patients lack B cells and do not have this opportunistic pattern.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Hyper-IgM syndrome is failed class switching from a missing CD40 ligand, usually X-linked.

### usmle1-025 · Immunology

- Outline: Immune system
- Front: After six months of age a boy has recurrent bacterial infections, no tonsils, and no B cells. Viral infections are handled normally.
- Answer: Bruton tyrosine kinase is missing. B cells never mature.
- Clue: T cells are intact, so viruses and fungi are less of the story.
- First-line: Give immunoglobulin. Do not expect lymphoid tissue on exam.
- Trap: Confusing this with a combined deficiency, which loses T-cell function too.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: X-linked agammaglobulinemia presents after maternal IgG fades. The missing tissue is B-cell tissue.

### usmle1-026 · Immunology

- Outline: Immune system
- Front: An infant has low calcium, no thymic shadow, and a conotruncal heart defect.
- Answer: The third and fourth pharyngeal pouches did not form.
- Clue: Those pouches make the inferior parathyroids and the thymus. Neural-crest cells also fail the outflow tract.
- First-line: Protect from live vaccines and irradiated blood until the immune defect is defined. Calcium and the heart need their own care.
- Trap: Calling this a pharyngeal-arch cartilage problem. The pouches, not the arch cartilages, are the missing parts.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: DiGeorge syndrome is a third-and-fourth pouch field defect. Hypocalcemia, no thymus, and outflow-tract defects travel together.

### usmle1-027 · Immunology

- Outline: Immune system
- Front: Sudden swelling of the face and airway with no urticaria, often after a dental procedure. Complement C4 is low all the time.
- Answer: C1 inhibitor is missing or not working.
- Clue: The pathway that makes bradykinin is unchecked. Histamine is not the mediator.
- First-line: Airway threats need a bradykinin-pathway drug, not the allergy algorithm alone.
- Trap: Giving epinephrine and antihistamines and considering the problem solved.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Hereditary angioedema is a complement regulatory disease. Low C4 between attacks is the screening clue.

### usmle1-028 · Hematologic

- Outline: Blood and lymphoreticular system
- Front: A tired patient has a low ferritin, a high iron-binding capacity, and a low serum iron. Another anemic patient has low iron but a low binding capacity and a normal or high ferritin.
- Answer: The first is iron deficiency. The second is anemia of chronic disease.
- Clue: Ferritin is the storage marker that separates them. Binding capacity rises only when stores are empty.
- First-line: Replace iron when stores are empty. Treat the underlying disease when stores are locked away.
- Trap: Giving iron to every low serum iron. Chronic disease already has iron the marrow cannot use.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Iron studies separate deficiency from anemia of inflammation. That pairing is a blood-system essential.

### usmle1-029 · Hematologic

- Outline: Blood and lymphoreticular system
- Front: Red cells sickle when oxygen falls. Acid, dehydration, and a high cell hemoglobin concentration make it worse.
- Answer: Deoxyhemoglobin S polymerizes.
- Clue: The polymer distorts the cell, which then occludes small vessels and hemolyzes.
- First-line: Oxygen, fluids, and pain control for a crisis. Hydroxyurea raises hemoglobin F over time.
- Trap: Treating the shape change as an antibody problem.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Sickling is a hemoglobin polymerization disease. Hypoxia, acidosis, and high mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration favor the polymer.

### usmle1-030 · Hematologic

- Outline: Blood and lymphoreticular system
- Front: A family has anemia, spherical red cells, and a spleen that is doing too much. After the spleen is removed the anemia improves and encapsulated bacteria become the risk.
- Answer: The membrane scaffold, often ankyrin or spectrin, is weak.
- Clue: The spleen traps the poorly deformable spheres. Parvovirus can suddenly stop red-cell production.
- First-line: Folate supports the high turnover. Splenectomy helps the anemia and then requires vaccine protection.
- Trap: Removing the spleen and forgetting the infection risk that follows.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Hereditary spherocytosis is a red-cell membrane disease. Splenectomy trades hemolysis for encapsulated-organism risk.

### usmle1-031 · Hematologic

- Outline: Blood and lymphoreticular system
- Front: Mild mucosal bleeding, a normal platelet count, and a normal or slightly long PTT. The factor VIII carrier protein is low.
- Answer: von Willebrand factor is low or not working.
- Clue: It carries factor VIII and sticks platelets to collagen. The platelet count itself is normal.
- First-line: Desmopressin helps many patients with type 1 disease. It is the wrong drug when the factor binds platelets too tightly.
- Trap: Transfusing platelets for a normal count.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: von Willebrand disease is the common inherited mucocutaneous bleeding disorder. The count is not the defect.

### usmle1-032 · Hematologic

- Outline: Blood and lymphoreticular system
- Front: A boy has hemarthroses. PTT is long, PT is normal, and the bleeding corrects when factor VIII is added.
- Answer: Factor VIII is missing.
- Clue: The inheritance is X-linked. Factor IX deficiency looks similar and does not correct with factor VIII.
- First-line: Replace the missing factor. Desmopressin can help mild factor VIII deficiency, not factor IX deficiency.
- Trap: Giving vitamin K for an intrinsic-pathway bleeding disorder.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Hemophilia A is factor VIII. The joint bleeding and the isolated PTT prolongation are the picture.

### usmle1-033 · Hematologic

- Outline: Blood and lymphoreticular system
- Front: A middle-aged adult has a very high neutrophil count, a low leukocyte alkaline phosphatase, and a chromosome swap between 9 and 22.
- Answer: BCR-ABL is driving the marrow.
- Clue: A reactive neutrophil count has a high alkaline phosphatase and no Philadelphia chromosome.
- First-line: A tyrosine kinase inhibitor targets the fusion. Blast crisis means the disease has left the chronic phase.
- Trap: Calling every extreme neutrophil count a leukemia.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Chronic myeloid leukemia is defined by BCR-ABL. The alkaline phosphatase separates it from a leukemoid reaction.

### usmle1-035 · Behavioral

### usmle1-036 · Neurologic

- Outline: Nervous system and special senses
- Front: One weak limb is stiff, with brisk reflexes and an upgoing toe. Another is flaccid, wasted, and fasciculating, with quiet reflexes.
- Answer: The first is an upper motor neuron. The second is a lower motor neuron.
- Clue: Upper motor neurons inhibit the reflex arc. Their loss releases it.
- First-line: Localize before you image. A mixed picture can be both, as in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.
- Trap: Calling every weak limb a stroke.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: The motor-neuron signs are a nervous-system localization rule used throughout the outline.

### usmle1-037 · Neurologic

- Outline: Nervous system and special senses
- Front: Sudden weakness of the face and arm, with little leg weakness, and the patient cannot find words. The other pattern is a weak leg with preserved speech.
- Answer: The first is the middle cerebral artery. The second is the anterior cerebral artery.
- Clue: The middle cerebral artery supplies the lateral cortex. The anterior cerebral artery supplies the medial leg area.
- First-line: A visual-field cut with little weakness points at the posterior cerebral artery instead.
- Trap: Treating every hemiparesis as the same arterial territory.
- Source: USMLE Content Outline (2024)
- URL: https://www.usmle.org/prepare-your-exam/step-1-materials
- Why this source: Arterial territories are nervous-system anatomy. Face and arm versus leg is the bedside split.

### usmle1-038 · Neurologic

- Outline: Nervous system and special senses
- Front: One side of the body is weak and has lost vibration. Pain and temperature are lost on the other side, a level below the injury.

### usmle3-073 · Cardiovascular

- Outline: Foundations of independent practice: perioperative care
- Front: An ACE inhibitor for uncomplicated hypertension is written as take on the morning of surgery with a sip of water.
- Answer: Hold it that morning.
- Clue: These drugs raise the chance of low pressure after induction. A beta blocker the patient already takes is the drug you continue.
- First-line: Hold the ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker the morning of surgery unless heart failure makes the team decide otherwise.
- Trap: Giving every blood pressure pill on the morning of surgery, including the ACE inhibitor.
- Source: ACC/AHA perioperative cardiovascular guideline (2024)
- URL: https://www.ahajournals.org/
- Why this source: ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers are often held the morning of surgery because of intraoperative hypotension.

### usmle3-074 · Prevention

- Outline: Foundations of independent practice: population health
- Front: A healthy 52-year-old has never received a pneumococcal vaccine and is in clinic for a blood pressure check.
- Answer: Give a pneumococcal conjugate vaccine. Fifty is the age.
- Clue: The adult schedule lowered the routine age to 50. A visit for something else is still a vaccine visit.
- First-line: One conjugate vaccine per the current schedule, and document it. Do not wait for the 65th birthday.
- Trap: Telling the patient to come back at 65.
- Source: CDC adult immunization schedule (2025)
- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/
- Why this source: ACIP recommends a pneumococcal conjugate vaccine for adults 50 and older. Confirm the current schedule before the feed is shown to students.

### usmle3-075 · Prevention

- Outline: Foundations of independent practice: population health
- Front: A patient with hives after eating eggs asks whether they can receive the influenza vaccine.
- Answer: Yes. Egg allergy is not a contraindication.
- Clue: Any age-appropriate influenza vaccine can be used. A history of hives, or even a more severe egg reaction, is not a reason to skip it.
- First-line: Vaccinate with the usual vaccine and observe as you would any patient. The only refusal is a severe reaction to a prior dose of the vaccine itself.
- Trap: Sending the patient away every autumn because of an egg allergy.
- Source: CDC influenza vaccine guidance (2025)
- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/flu/
- Why this source: Egg allergy of any severity is not a contraindication to influenza vaccination.

### usmle3-076 · Neurologic

- Outline: Foundations of independent practice: systems-based practice
- Front: A patient had a seizure yesterday. They ask if they may drive to work tomorrow and whether you will keep the seizure off any report.
- Answer: They may not drive. Follow your state's reporting rule.
- Clue: The seizure-free interval is set by the state. Some states require the physician to report. Some put the duty on the driver.
- First-line: Tell the patient not to drive, document the advice, and report if your state requires it. Do not promise secrecy you cannot keep.
- Trap: A private suggestion to be careful, with no mention of the law.
- Source: AAN and state driver-licensing principles (2017)
- URL: https://www.aan.com/
- Why this source: After a seizure the patient does not drive until the legal seizure-free interval is met, and state reporting rules are followed.

### usmle3-077 · Ethics

- Outline: Foundations of independent practice: systems-based practice
- Front: An older adult has bruises in different stages and flinches when the caregiver is asked to step out. The caregiver says the patient falls a lot.
- Answer: Report suspected abuse. Do not investigate it yourself first.
- Clue: A vulnerable adult with an inconsistent story is reported to adult protective services.
- First-line: Make the report, treat the injuries, and document the findings. You do not need proof.
- Trap: Waiting until you are sure, or confronting the caregiver as your only action.
- Source: Elder Justice and APS reporting standards (2020)
- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/elder-abuse/
- Why this source: Suspected abuse of a vulnerable adult is reported. Certainty is not required.

### usmle3-078 · Pediatrics

- Outline: Foundations of independent practice: systems-based practice
- Front: A toddler has a spiral fracture of the femur. The story is that the child rolled off a couch. The exam shows older bruises the story does not explain.
- Answer: Report suspected abuse.
- Clue: The fracture and the story do not match, and there are older injuries.
- First-line: Treat the fracture, report to child protective services, and do not send the child home to the same unexplained risk.
- Trap: Accepting the couch story because spiral fractures can sometimes be accidental.
- Source: AAP child abuse pediatrics guidance (2015)
- URL: https://www.aap.org/
- Why this source: Injuries that do not match the developmental story, especially with older unexplained bruises, are reported as suspected abuse.

### usmle3-079 · Perioperative

- Outline: Foundations of independent practice: perioperative care
- Front: A person who can become pregnant is scheduled for elective surgery next week. The last menstrual period is not in the chart. No pregnancy test is ordered.
- Answer: Test before the operation.
- Clue: Elective surgery and anesthesia in an unrecognized pregnancy are avoidable.
- First-line: A pregnancy test after consent, or a documented reason it was declined. Do not discover the pregnancy in the operating room.
- Trap: Proceeding because the patient says they are probably not pregnant.
- Source: ASA pregnancy testing before anesthesia (2021)
- URL: https://www.asahq.org/
- Why this source: Patients who could be pregnant are offered pregnancy testing before elective anesthesia.

### usmle3-080 · Pulmonary

- Outline: Foundations of independent practice: perioperative care
- Front: A patient with known obstructive sleep apnea is coming for surgery. Their machine is at home. The recovery order says oxygen by nasal cannula only.
- Answer: Bring the machine and use it after surgery.
- Clue: The danger is the hours after anesthesia, when sedatives and opioids meet an untreated airway.
- First-line: Have the patient bring their device, use it in recovery, and limit the sedatives.
- Trap: Discovering the apnea after a desaturation in the recovery room.
- Source: ASA obstructive sleep apnea perioperative guideline (2014)
- URL: https://www.asahq.org/
- Why this source: Patients with obstructive sleep apnea should use their positive-airway-pressure device in the perioperative period.
